# Summary of: 2025 Country Report - Italy: Economic, Social, and Structural Policy Assessment

## Context Metadata

- **Source Document**: SWD(2025) 212 final - 2025 Country Report - Italy
- **Effective Period**: 2024 (Outturn), 2025-2026 (Projections)
- **Issuer**: European Commission
- **Document Type**: Country Report / Commission Staff Working Document

## Executive Summary

The European Commission's 2025 Country Report on Italy presents a mixed economic picture characterized by moderate growth and improving fiscal indicators, overshadowed by persistent structural vulnerabilities. Italy’s real GDP growth stabilized at 0.7% in 2024, supported by investment financed through the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRP) and net external demand, while private consumption remained subdued by households' desire to rebuild savings. Inflation is expected to stabilize around the ECB’s 2% target in 2025-2026.

Fiscally, Italy achieved a primary surplus of 0.4% of GDP in 2024, the first since 2019, driven by the phase-out of the "Superbonus" housing renovation tax credits and energy support measures. However, the public debt-to-GDP ratio reversed its declining trend, projected to rise to 136.7% in 2025 due to lagged borrowing needs and sizeable debt service costs. The country has committed to a seven-year fiscal adjustment path under its Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTFSP), endorsed by the Council in January 2025, which aims to ensure debt sustainability.

Structurally, Italy faces significant challenges that hamper productivity and competitiveness. These include sluggish productivity growth, low R&D investment, an underdeveloped venture capital market, and a justice system that, while improving, still suffers from lengthy proceedings. The labor market shows resilience with record employment rates, but issues persist with low labor force participation (especially among women and youth), high skills mismatches, and significant regional disparities. The South (Mezzogiorno) continues to lag in GDP per capita, infrastructure, and service delivery, exacerbated by demographic decline and brain drain. Additionally, Italy is highly exposed to climate risks and relies heavily on imports for critical raw materials and energy, necessitating urgent adaptation and green transition investments.

## Key Highlights

- **[Fiscal Consolidation and Debt Dynamics]**: Italy returned to a primary surplus in 2024 (0.4% of GDP) after phasing out costly tax credits. Despite this, the debt-to-GDP ratio is projected to increase to 136.7% in 2025, driven by the cash-flow impact of past tax credits and the interest-growth differential. The Commission notes that high public debt and rising pension costs due to aging demographics restrict fiscal policy flexibility and pose medium-term sustainability risks.
- **[Growth and Inflation Outlook]**: Real GDP growth is expected to remain moderate (0.7% in 2024, with slow expansion forecast for 2025-2026) due to global trade uncertainties and weak productivity. Inflation is stabilizing around 2% as energy prices normalize and wage growth remains moderate, preserving competitiveness but only partially offsetting past household purchasing power losses.
- **[Structural Vulnerabilities and Productivity]**: Weak productivity growth persists due to insufficient R&D investment (private R&D is half the EU average), limited venture capital, and low tertiary education attainment. The justice system, a key bottleneck for investment, has seen reduced backlogs but still has the longest expected trial durations in the EU. Corruption remains a significant perceived obstacle to business (50% of firms).
- **[Labor Market and Social Challenges]**: Employment rates reached record highs, yet labor force participation remains low, particularly for women (57.4% vs 70.8% EU avg) due to inadequate childcare and long-term care services. Skills mismatches are acute, with high rates of over-qualification and shortages in ICT and green sectors. Regional disparities are stark, with the South suffering from lower employment, higher poverty, and infrastructure deficits.
- **[Green Transition and Energy Competitiveness]**: Italy is a leader in clean tech manufacturing (solar PV, wind) but faces high wholesale electricity prices due to a gas-heavy mix and taxes/levies. The country is highly vulnerable to climate risks (floods, droughts) and has a large insurance protection gap. Dependencies on imported critical raw materials and semiconductors pose supply chain risks.
- **[Financial Sector Resilience]**: The banking sector has strengthened, with capital adequacy ratios at 20% and non-performing loans declining to 2.8%. However, banks remain heavily exposed to domestic sovereign debt. Capital markets are underdeveloped, with low equity investment by households and institutional investors, limiting funding for innovative startups and SMEs.
