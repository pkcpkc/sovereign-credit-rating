# Summary of: 2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Belgium

## Context Metadata

- **Source Document**: 2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on the rule of law situation in Belgium
- **Effective Period**: 2025 (covering developments primarily from late 2024 to mid-2025)
- **Issuer**: European Commission
- **Document Type**: Commission Staff Working Document

## Executive Summary

The European Commission’s 2025 Rule of Law Report on Belgium highlights a mixed picture of progress and persistent challenges across the four pillars of the rule of law: the justice system, anti-corruption framework, media pluralism/freedom, and other institutional checks and balances.

The justice system maintains high perceived independence, though structural resource deficiencies and long-standing efficiency issues, particularly excessive length of proceedings, remain critical concerns. The Government intends to transfer budgetary management powers to the judiciary but faces criticism regarding the linkage of efficiency reforms to disciplinary measures. Progress has been made in publishing open judicial positions, but vacancies remain unfilled due to unattractive working conditions.

In the anti-corruption domain, Belgium scores relatively low on corruption perceptions, yet significant gaps persist. New investigative structures are planned, but concerns remain regarding the handling of high-level corruption cases, information leaks, and potential political interference. Integrity frameworks for politicians and police require strengthening, with limited progress on lobbying transparency, revolving doors, and the verification of asset declarations.

Media freedom and pluralism benefit from strong legal foundations and independent regulators, though public service media face governance and budgetary pressures. Recent legislative steps have improved protections for journalists, including decriminalising defamation, but civic space remains narrowed, with concerns over restrictions on demonstrations and access to public documents.

Finally, compliance with final court rulings remains an area of limited progress. The Belgian Government is under supervision by the Council of Europe for structural issues identified by the ECtHR. Additionally, civic space concerns are exacerbated by proposed measures that could restrict the right to demonstrate.

## Key Highlights

- **Judicial Independence and Management**: Perceived judicial independence remains high (64% public, 72% companies). The Government plans to transfer budgetary management of the justice system from the executive to the judiciary, though the High Council for Justice and Court of Cassation criticize the link to disciplinary reforms. Structural resource shortages persist, with salaries and working conditions cited as barriers to filling vacancies.
- **Justice Efficiency**: Backlogs and length of proceedings remain problematic, particularly in the Court of Appeal of Brussels. The High Council for Justice published data on criminal case durations, and a Recovery and Resilience Facility-funded project aims to improve statistical collection. The Government is addressing Court of Audit criticisms regarding the poor management and fraud risks in justice digitalisation.
- **Anti-Corruption Structures and Integrity**: A new financial investigation service (FIOD-SRFF) and specialised prosecution service are planned. However, concerns persist over political interference and information leaks in high-level corruption cases handled by the CDBC-OCRC. Police integrity remains weak due to disparate policies across local zones and lack of a unified Code of Conduct.
- **Lobbying and Revolving Doors**: No further progress was made on lobbying legislation; the new Government has not decided on draft rules. Limited progress was made on "revolving doors" via a non-binding circular, but binding rules on cooling-off periods remain absent.
- **Asset Declarations**: The system of asset and interest declarations for politicians suffers from a lack of verification and transparency, as declarations are sealed and only accessible during criminal investigations. Civil society criticizes the setup, and compliance, while high, relies solely on individual responsibility.
- **Media Pluralism and Freedom**: Media regulators (VRM, CSA, Medienrat) have strengthened legal and operational independence, particularly under the Digital Services Act. Public service media (VRT, RTBF, BRF) face budgetary pressures and governance scrutiny. The Media Pluralism Monitor reports low risk for political independence, though slightly increased from 2024.
- **Journalist Protections**: Belgium decriminalised defamation and introduced harsher penalties for crimes against journalists. Efforts are underway to transpose the EU anti-SLAPP Directive. However, journalists report increasing harassment, legal intimidation, and online threats.
- **Access to Public Documents**: Recent federal legislation improved the transparency framework, but procedural shortcomings remain, including non-binding appeal bodies. The Council of Europe Convention on Access to Official Documents has been signed but not ratified.
- **Compliance with Court Rulings**: Limited progress was made in ensuring public authorities comply with final rulings. The Government remains under Council of Europe supervision for structural issues from the *Camara v. Belgium* case. On Jan 1, 2025, 17 leading ECtHR judgments were pending implementation.
- **Civic Space**: Civic space is rated as "narrowed." Stakeholders raise concerns over new obstacles to the right to demonstrate, including proposed judicial bans for individuals convicted of acts during protests. The Constitutional Court issued relevant judgments in late 2024 regarding subsidy eligibility and urgent court orders.
