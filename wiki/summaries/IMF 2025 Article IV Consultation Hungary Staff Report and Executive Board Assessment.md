# Summary of: IMF 2025 Article IV Consultation: Hungary Staff Report and Executive Board Assessment

## Context Metadata

- **Source Document**: Hungary: 2025 Article IV Consultation—Press Release, Staff Report, and Statement by the Executive Director for Hungary
- **Effective Period**: 2025 (Data through Q2 2025, Projections to 2030)
- **Issuer**: International Monetary Fund (IMF)
- **Document Type**: Article IV Staff Report and Executive Board Summary

## Executive Summary

The IMF’s 2025 Article IV Consultation for Hungary highlights an economy at a challenging juncture, characterized by stagnant output over the past three years, persistently high inflation above the central bank’s 3% target, and elevated public debt. While the Hungarian economy demonstrated resilience in 2024 with a current account surplus and improved fiscal deficit, the near-term outlook remains subdued due to high domestic and external uncertainty. Growth is projected to be modest (0.7% in 2025), driven primarily by private consumption and favorable wage dynamics, with a recovery to 2.0% in 2026 contingent on investment revival.

Key risks identified by the IMF Executive Board include deepening geoeconomic fragmentation, potential escalation in trade measures (particularly US tariffs), intensification of regional conflicts, and delays in necessary fiscal adjustments. A significant downside risk involves the continued suspension or cancellation of approximately €19 billion in EU funds due to rule of law and governance shortcomings.

The IMF emphasizes the need for additional fiscal effort to rebuild buffers and ensure debt sustainability, recommending a structural primary surplus of around 1.75% of GDP in the medium term. This requires high-quality revenue enhancements (reducing tax exemptions) and spending rationalization (phasing out energy subsidies and administrative overhead). Monetary policy is advised to remain tight into 2026 to durably return inflation to target, while phasing out distortive price and margin controls. Structural reforms to boost productivity, enhance energy security, and improve governance are deemed critical to unlocking growth and EU funding. The financial sector is assessed as broadly sound but faces vulnerabilities from corporate sector risks, growing sovereign-bank linkages, and housing market pressures.

## Key Highlights

- **Stagnant Growth and Elevated Inflation**: Real GDP stagnated for three years, with Q1 2025 showing a contraction of 0.4% y/y. Inflation remains above target at 4.4-4.6% in mid-2025, driven by core services and wage pressures. The IMF projects inflation to gradually decelerate to 3% by 2027, conditioned on a tight monetary stance.
- **Fiscal Sustainability Concerns**: Public debt reached 73.5% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to rise to ~79% by 2030 under current policies. The IMF estimates a "moderate" risk of sovereign stress, citing high gross financing needs (avg. 17% of GDP), rising FX debt share (over 30%), and significant contingent liabilities from SOEs. The staff recommends an additional cumulative fiscal adjustment of ~2% of GDP over 2025-2028 to bring the deficit below 3% by 2027.
- **Monetary Policy and Regulatory Distortions**: The Magyar Nemzeti Bank (MNB) has kept the base rate at 6.5% since September 2024. The IMF supports maintaining a tight stance into 2026. The report criticizes regulatory interventions such as price, interest, and margin caps, arguing they distort market signals, defer price pressures, and weaken the effectiveness of monetary policy. These measures should be phased out.
- **External Sector and EU Funds**: Hungary recorded a current account surplus of 2.2% of GDP in 2024, assessed as substantially stronger than fundamentals imply. However, €19 billion in EU funds (including Recovery and Resilience Facility and Cohesion funds) remain suspended pending rule of law reforms. The cancellation or further delay of these funds poses a significant downside risk to fiscal and external balances.
- **Financial Sector Vulnerabilities**: While banks are well-capitalized (CAR 20.5%) and liquid, vulnerabilities are increasing. Key risks include the sovereign-bank nexus (banks hold high amounts of government debt), corporate sector risks amid high interest rates and FX exposure, and buoyant housing prices fueled by subsidized lending. The IMF urges caution in relaxing macroprudential limits for green/first-time home buyers unless justified by financial stability grounds.
- **Structural Reform Priorities**: Stagnant productivity is a major drag on competitiveness. The IMF recommends fostering firm dynamism by reducing barriers to factor mobility, limiting state aid, and strengthening access to risk capital for young firms. Energy security and the transition to renewables are also highlighted, noting that Hungary is one of the most energy-intensive economies in Europe. Governance reforms are essential to unlock EU funds and create a predictable business environment.
