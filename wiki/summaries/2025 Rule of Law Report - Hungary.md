# Summary of: 2025 Rule of Law Report Hungary

## Context Metadata
*   **Source:** European Commission Staff Working Document (SWD(2025) 917 final)
*   **Date of Issue:** July 8, 2025
*   **Scope:** Assessment of the rule of law situation in Hungary, covering the judiciary, anti-corruption framework, media pluralism, and institutional checks and balances.

## Executive Summary
The 2025 Rule of Law Report for Hungary highlights a complex landscape where significant progress in judicial remuneration is contrasted by persistent concerns regarding judicial independence, high-level corruption, and media freedom. While the *Kúria* (Supreme Court) shows improvements in case allocation and uniformity of law, lower courts still lack transparency. The government continues to utilize extensive emergency powers, and the "state of danger" remains in effect until November 2025. Significant hurdles remain in the implementation of anti-corruption measures, specifically regarding high-level corruption and lobbying regulations. Media pluralism is severely threatened by state advertising dominance, lack of regulator independence, and increased pressure on journalists by the Sovereignty Protection Office.

## Key Highlights

### I. Justice System
*   **Judicial Independence:** Public perception of judicial independence remains low (37%). While the National Judicial Council (NJC) exercises its powers, there is ongoing friction between the NJC and the *Kúria* President regarding judicial reforms.
*   **Remuneration & Reforms:** Significant progress was made in increasing salaries for judges and prosecutors through a three-step plan until 2027. However, the NJC withdrew from some agreements due to concerns over legislative amendments that bypass consultation.
*   **Case Allocation:** Transparency in lower-instance courts has stagnated, while the *Kúria* has successfully implemented a transparent allocation system.
*   **Judicial Quality:** New rules (effective 2025/2026) raise the minimum age for judges to 35 and allow judges to serve until age 70.

### II. Anti-Corruption Framework
*   **Public Perception:** Corruption remains perceived as widespread (88% of citizens).
*   **Strategy Implementation:** The 2024-2025 National Anti-Corruption Strategy is ongoing but lacks a robust track record for high-level corruption cases.
*   **Asset Declarations:** Significant shortcomings remain in the oversight and transparency of asset declarations for high-ranking officials.
*   **Lobbying & Revolving Doors:** No progress has been made on comprehensive reforms, though legislation is planned for November 2025.
*   **Whistleblowing:** While a framework exists, whistleblowing is not yet an effective tool for detecting corruption crimes.

### III. Media Pluralism and Media Freedom
*   **Regulatory Independence:** The NMHH is still considered to have high risk due to government influence over the Media Council.
*   **State Advertising:** The state remains the dominant advertiser, channeling up to 80% of revenue to pro-government media conglomerates (KESMA).
*   **Journalist Safety:** Journalists face increased pressure, including investigations by the Sovereignty Protection Office and selective denial of access to public events.
*   **Freedom of Information:** While procedural treatment is faster, the scope of access remains limited, particularly regarding public funding.

### IV. Institutional Checks and Balances
*   **Emergency Powers:** The government's extensive use of "state of danger" powers undermines legal certainty and impacts the single market.
*   **Law-making Process:** Concerns persist regarding the lack of public consultation for critical legislation and the use of "technical" amendments to bypass debate.
*   **Investment Protection:** Only 46% of companies feel confident in investment protection, citing frequent legislative changes as a primary concern.
*   **Civil Society:** The environment for CSOs remains "obstructed," with new legislation threatening to move CSO registers to an administrative authority and restricting foreign funding.
