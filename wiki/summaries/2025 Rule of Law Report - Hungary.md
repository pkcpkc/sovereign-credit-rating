# Summary of: 2025 Rule of Law Report - Hungary

## Context Metadata
*   **Source Document:** Commission Staff Working Document SWD(2025) 917 final
*   **Date of Issue:** July 8, 2025
*   **Subject:** Assessment of the rule of law situation in Hungary, covering the judiciary, anti-corruption framework, media pluralism, and institutional checks and balances.
*   **Key Stakeholders:** European Commission, Hungarian National Judicial Council (*OBT*), *Kúria* (Supreme Court), Integrity Authority, and various civil society organizations.

## Executive Summary
The 2025 Rule of Law Report highlights a complex landscape in Hungary characterized by significant progress in judicial remuneration and digitalization, contrasted by persistent systemic issues regarding judicial independence, corruption oversight, and media freedom. While the government has implemented salary increases for the justice system, it has faced criticism for proceeding with legislative reforms without sufficient consultation with the National Judicial Council. The report notes a lack of progress in high-level corruption investigations, lobbying regulations, and the transparency of political party financing. Furthermore, the extensive use of emergency powers and the "state of danger" continues to undermine legal certainty, while media outlets and civil society organizations face increasing pressure from the Sovereignty Protection Office.

## Key Highlights

### I. Justice System
*   **Judicial Independence:** Perceived independence remains low, with only 37% of the public and 38% of companies viewing it as "fairly or very good."
*   **Judicial Reforms:** The National Judicial Council (NJC) withdrew from a four-party agreement in early 2025, citing a lack of consultation on subsequent legislative amendments that raised the minimum age for judges to 35 and allowed service until age 70.
*   **Case Allocation:** Transparency in lower-instance courts remains poor, though the *Kúria* (Supreme Court) has successfully implemented uniform case allocation rules.
*   **Remuneration & Quality:** Significant progress was made in increasing salaries for judges and prosecutors (aiming for a 48% increase by 2027), though structural indexation remains absent.
*   **Efficiency:** Hungary ranks highly for the speed of resolving civil and commercial cases, though a lack of adequate compensation for protracted criminal proceedings remains a concern.

### II. Anti-Corruption Framework
*   **Perception & Progress:** Corruption remains perceived as widespread (88% of citizens). While some low-level codes of conduct were established, high-level corruption remains largely unaddressed.
*   **Integrity Authority:** The authority faces significant obstacles in accessing state databases and has reported limited practical impact from the Anti-Corruption Task Force.
*   **Prosecutorial Oversight:** A new mechanism allows judges to review prosecutorial decisions not to investigate corruption, which has seen a doubling in usage since 2024.
*   **Asset Declarations:** Significant shortcomings remain in the transparency and oversight of asset declarations for senior political functions.

### III. Media Pluralism and Media Freedom
*   **Regulatory Independence:** The National Media and Infocommunications Authority (NMHH) is still viewed as having high risk due to its composition by Parliament-nominated members.
*   **State Advertising:** The state remains the dominant advertiser, channeling the majority of resources to pro-government media outlets (e.g., KESMA).
*   **Journalist Safety:** Journalists face increasing pressure, including smear campaigns and investigations by the Sovereignty Protection Office regarding "foreign interests."
*   **Access to Information:** While court processing is faster, the scope of access—particularly regarding public funding—remains restricted.

### IV. Institutional Checks and Balances
*   **Emergency Powers:** The prolonged "state of danger" allows the Government to override Acts of Parliament, creating legal uncertainty for businesses and the single market.
*   **Law-making Process:** Concerns persist regarding the lack of public consultation on key legislative drafts and the practice of introducing substantive amendments at the very late stages of the process.
*   **Investment Protection:** Only 46% of companies feel confident that investments are protected, primarily due to frequent legislative changes and a perceived lack of quality in the law-making process.
*   **Civil Society Space:** The environment for CSOs is described as "obstructed," exacerbated by the Sovereignty Protection Office's investigations and a proposed law that would significantly restrict access to foreign funding.
