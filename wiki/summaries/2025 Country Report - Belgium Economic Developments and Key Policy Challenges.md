# Summary of: 2025 Country Report - Belgium: Economic Developments and Key Policy Challenges

## Context Metadata

- **Source Document**: COMMISSION STAFF WORKING DOCUMENT SWD(2025) 201 final
- **Effective Period**: Data cut-off April 30, 2025; Projections for 2025-2029
- **Issuer**: European Commission
- **Document Type**: Country Report / Economic Policy Surveillance

## Executive Summary

The 2025 Country Report for Belgium, published by the European Commission on June 4, 2025, outlines a trajectory of slowing economic growth accompanied by significant deterioration in public finances. After growing by 1% in 2024, driven by private consumption, Belgium's GDP growth is projected to decelerate to 0.8% in 2025. This slowdown is attributed to weakening exports (impacted by external shocks such as US tariffs) and a cooling of domestic investment and consumption.

The most critical challenge identified is the severe pressure on public finances. The general government deficit expanded to 4.5% of GDP in 2024 and is forecast to rise further to 5.4% in 2025, driven by rising expenditure—particularly ageing-related costs and increased defence spending—against a stable revenue base of roughly 50% of GDP. Without policy action, public debt is projected to surge from 104.7% of GDP in 2024 to 126.4% by 2035. Belgium has submitted a Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP) covering 2025–2029, which the Commission has positively assessed. The plan utilizes the "National Escape Clause" to accommodate higher defence spending but commits to a seven-year fiscal adjustment period underpinned by structural reforms in pensions, taxation, and labour markets.

Structurally, Belgium faces persistent headwinds regarding competitiveness and labour market dynamism. The employment rate, despite reaching a historic high of 72.3% in 2024, remains below the EU average due to low activity rates, significant skills mismatches, and disincentives to work created by a complex, high-tax benefit system. Labour productivity growth has been weak, hampered by low business dynamism, regulatory burdens, and limited innovation diffusion. The report also highlights environmental and energy challenges, noting that Belgium is falling short of its 2030 climate targets and that high electricity prices and fossil fuel subsidies hinder the green transition.

## Key Highlights

- **[Fiscal Deterioration & Debt Trajectory]**: The government deficit widened to 4.5% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to hit 5.4% in 2025. Net expenditure growth is forecast at 5.0% for 2025, exceeding the MTP's maximum rate but permitted under the National Escape Clause for defence spending. Public debt, already high at 104.7% of GDP, is on an unsustainable upward trajectory, projected to reach 126.4% by 2035 without intervention.

- **[Activation of National Escape Clause]**: Belgium successfully utilized the National Escape Clause within the reformed EU Economic Governance Framework (Regulation (EU) 2024/1263) to legally exceed net expenditure limits for defence purposes. Defence spending is projected to rise to 1.7% of GDP in 2025. This mechanism allows the deviation while maintaining compliance with the broader fiscal roadmap, provided reforms and investments are delivered.

- **[Ageing-Related Fiscal Pressure]**: Belgium faces one of the steepest projected increases in ageing-related spending in the EU. By 2070, age-related expenditure is projected to rise by 5.1 percentage points of GDP (vs EU avg 1.4), driven primarily by pensions (+3.5 pps) and long-term care (+1.7 pps). This demographic burden severely constrains fiscal space and necessitates the structural pension reforms included in the MTP.

- **[MTP Reforms & Fiscal Framework Weaknesses]**: The endorsed Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan commits to reforms over a 7-year extension period, including a bonus-malus pension system, labour tax cuts financed by capital gains/property taxes, and spending reviews. However, the report highlights that Belgium’s national fiscal framework remains weak due to the lack of effective budgetary coordination between federal and regional entities and the under-resourced nature of the High Council of Finance.

- **[Labour Market Disincentives]**: Despite record employment, the activity rate remains low (77.2% vs EU 83.3%). The report identifies high personal income taxes, unlimited unemployment benefits, and complex non-cash benefits as major disincentives to work. The new federal coalition plans to limit unemployment benefits to two years for those under 55 and link benefits to income, aiming to boost activation.

- **[Productivity & Competitiveness Challenges]**: Labour productivity growth has lagged the EU average since the 2000s. Competitiveness is eroded by high labour costs (exacerbated by automatic wage indexation during inflation) and a stagnant real effective exchange rate. Business dynamism is low, characterized by a high presence of "zombie firms" and restrictive regulations in services, which trap productive resources.

- **[Energy Transition & Climate Shortfall]**: Belgium is projected to miss its 2030 climate target for effort-sharing sectors, achieving only a 42.6% reduction instead of 47%. High electricity-to-gas price ratios (distorted by taxes) disincentivize electrification. The rollout of renewables is slow due to grid constraints and permitting delays, and the country maintains significant environmentally harmful fossil fuel subsidies (0.85% of GDP) with no planned phase-out before 2030.
