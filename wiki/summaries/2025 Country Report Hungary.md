# Summary of: 2025 Country Report Hungary

## Context Metadata

## Executive Summary

The 2025 Country Report for Hungary, issued by the European Commission, outlines a macroeconomic landscape characterized by subdued growth, persistent inflationary pressures, and significant structural vulnerabilities. While GDP growth is projected to rebound slowly from 0.5% in 2024 to 2.5% in 2026, driven partly by manufacturing investments in the electric vehicle industry, the outlook remains highly uncertain due to global value chain integration risks and tariff prospects. Inflation, though declining from its 2023 peak, remains above target due to underlying domestic demand and food price pressures, complicating monetary policy effectiveness.

Fiscal sustainability is a primary concern, with public debt projected to rise to 74.5% of GDP by 2025 and debt-servicing costs remaining among the highest in the EU. The government's budgetary framework suffers from weaknesses, including frequent revisions, a lack of multiannual spending ceilings, and the use of "state of danger" decrees that bypass parliamentary oversight. These factors undermine policy predictability and accountability.

Structurally, Hungary faces severe challenges in its business environment, marked by regulatory volatility, sector-specific taxes, and state intervention that crowds out private actors. Innovation is hampered by low R&D intensity, skills shortages, and limited access to market-based financing. The energy sector relies heavily on Russian fossil fuels, posing security risks, while the rapid expansion of solar capacity has not been matched by grid flexibility or storage solutions, leading to price volatility.

Socially, poverty and inequality are rising, with vulnerable groups, particularly the Roma community and low-skilled workers, facing significant barriers. The education system struggles with segregation and low tertiary attainment, while healthcare spending remains critically low, impacting life expectancy and workforce productivity. The report emphasizes the urgent need for structural reforms, improved anti-corruption frameworks, and accelerated implementation of EU funds to ensure long-term economic convergence and social fairness.

## Key Highlights

- **[Subdued Growth and Inflation Dynamics]**: GDP growth was 0.5% in 2024, with forecasts for 0.8% in 2025 and 2.5% in 2026. Inflation declined to 3.7% in 2024 from a peak of 17.0% in 2023, but underlying pressures remain high at 5.9%, risking prolonged elevated inflation expectations.
- **[High Debt Servicing and Fiscal Risks]**: Public debt rose to 73.5% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to increase to 74.5% in 2025. Debt-servicing costs are projected to remain among the highest in the EU, with implicit interest rates near 6%, posing medium-to-long-term sustainability risks.
- **[Budgetary Framework Weaknesses]**: The 2026 budget law revised the deficit target from 2.9% to 3.7% of GDP. The medium-term budgetary framework is criticized for lacking multiannual spending ceilings, frequent revisions, and the use of emergency decrees that limit transparency and accountability.
- **[Business Environment and Regulatory Volatility]**: Hungary ranks among the last in the EU for competitiveness. Key barriers include unpredictable regulatory changes, high energy and banking costs, sector-specific taxes targeting foreign-owned firms, and a lack of equal treatment in public procurement.
- **[Energy Dependence and Security Risks]**: Hungary remains heavily dependent on Russian fossil fuels, with over 70% of natural gas and 80% of crude oil originating from Russia. Efforts to diversify supply are slow, and the domestic energy market suffers from high prices for businesses due to regulated household prices and taxes.
- **[Social Inequality and Poverty Trends]**: The at-risk-of-poverty-or-social-exclusion (AROPE) rate increased to 20.2% in 2024. Poverty is particularly severe among children, the Roma community, and persons with disabilities. Income inequality remains high, with the top 1% holding 33% of total wealth.
- **[Education and Skills Shortages]**: Labour productivity is one of the lowest in the EU, stagnant for 15 years. Tertiary education attainment is among the lowest in the EU (32.3%), and skills shortages in sectors like healthcare, construction, and manufacturing hamper growth.
- **[Weak Anti-Corruption and Institutional Framework]**: Corruption is perceived as widespread by 78% of companies. The anti-corruption framework is deemed ineffective, with delays in implementing Recovery and Resilience Plan (RRP) measures. Judicial independence and the rule of law remain under scrutiny.
- **[Financial Sector Resilience vs. Market Distortions]**: The banking sector is robust, with high capital ratios (CET1 at 18.8%) and low non-performing loans. However, government interventions, such as subsidised loans and tax incentives for banks to hold government debt, distort credit allocation and limit monetary policy transmission.
- **[Innovation and R&D Stagnation]**: R&D intensity (1.4%) remains below the EU average (2.2%). Public R&D spending is low, and business innovation is concentrated in a few large foreign firms. Venture capital and private equity markets are underdeveloped, limiting funding for start-ups and scale-ups.

Water resilience is poor, with only 11.3% of surface waters in good ecological status. The circular economy transition is slow, with recycling rates below EU averages and high dependency on critical raw material imports.
