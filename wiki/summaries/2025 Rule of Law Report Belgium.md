# Summary of: 2025 Rule of Law Report Belgium

## Context Metadata

- **Source Document**: 2025 Rule of Law Report - Country Chapter on Belgium (SWD(2025) 901 final)
- **Effective Period**: Reference period covering developments up to mid-2025, specifically highlighting the 2024-2025 period.
- **Issuer**: European Commission
- **Document Type**: Commission Staff Working Document / Country Chapter

## Executive Summary

The 2025 Rule of Law Report for Belgium indicates a mixed but generally positive trajectory regarding the rule of law, with notable strengths in judicial independence and anti-corception perceptions, alongside persistent challenges in justice system efficiency, integrity framework enforcement, and civic space protection.

A key development is the high perceived judicial independence among both the general public and businesses, prompting government plans to transfer budgetary management powers for the justice administration from the executive to the judiciary. While progress has been made in addressing structural resource deficiencies and improving justice efficiency through better data collection, significant backlogs remain, particularly at the Court of Appeal of Brussels. The digitalization of justice faces scrutiny from the Court of Audit regarding governance and fraud risks, though an action plan has been submitted.

In the anti-corruption domain, Belgium maintains a relatively low perception of corruption. The new government plans to establish specialized investigative and prosecutorial structures targeting financial crime and corruption. However, concerns persist regarding the handling of high-profile cases, potential political influence, and limited prosecution of foreign bribery. Integrity policies within the police and for public officials show gaps in monitoring and enforcement, particularly concerning "revolving doors" and asset declarations, which lack transparency and robust verification.

Media freedom benefits from strong legal foundations for regulatory authorities across all communities, though public service media face governance and budgetary pressures. Recent legislative steps have strengthened protections for journalists, including decriminalizing defamation and enhancing penalties for attacks on them. However, civic space is described as "narrowed," with concerns over disproportionate measures against protesters and legislative obstacles to the right to demonstrate.

Finally, compliance with final rulings of national courts and European Court of Human Rights judgments remains a weak spot, with the government under ongoing Council of Europe supervision for structural issues. The Federal Human Rights Institute has raised concerns about budget cuts to equality bodies, potentially undermining human rights protection.

## Key Highlights

- **Judicial Independence and Reform**: Perceived judicial independence remains high (64% of public, 72% of companies). The government intends to transfer budgetary management for the justice system to the judiciary, enhancing managerial autonomy, though this is linked to pending reforms in evaluation and disciplinary systems. Bar Associations raise concerns about procedural safeguards for legal professional privilege, particularly regarding recording bans and videoconferencing.

- **Justice System Resources and Efficiency**: Some progress in addressing structural resource deficiencies, including publishing magistrate vacancies, but budgets and judge-to-population ratios remain below EU averages. The Court of Audit criticized the digitalization strategy for lacking oversight and fraud controls; an action plan was submitted in September 2024. Backlogs, especially at the Brussels Court of Appeal, remain a critical issue, though some statistical data on case duration has been published.

- **Anti-Corruption Framework**: Belgium scores 69/100 on the Corruption Perceptions Index. The new government plans to create specialized financial investigation and prosecution services targeting corruption and organized crime. Good cooperation exists between the Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC) and prosecutors, but concerns exist over information leaks, political influence in high-profile cases, and low prosecution rates for foreign bribery.

- **Integrity and Asset Declarations**: Integrity policies in the police are disparate, with plans for voluntary mergers and career-long integrity checks. Monitoring of integrity policies for ministers and MPs is weak, lacking enforcement mechanisms. Asset declarations are submitted by 99% of required officials, but verification is limited to criminal investigations, raising transparency concerns.

- **Lobbying and Political Financing**: No progress on lobbying reform, with the new government undecided on draft rules. The House of Representatives updated its gifts and benefits rules slightly, but no register was introduced. Limited progress on "revolving doors" rules; a non-binding circular was issued, but no new binding legislation. Political party financing reform stalled.

- **Whistleblower Protection**: Laws adopted for whistleblowers in Parliament, though their scope is narrower than for other civil servants. General whistleblower protection is largely in place, with functioning mechanisms in the public sector.

- **Media Pluralism and Freedom**: Media regulators (VRM, CSA, Medienrat) are independent and strengthening capacities under the Digital Services Act. Public service media (VRT, RTBF, BRF) face governance and budgetary challenges. Recent laws decriminalized defamation and increased penalties for attacks on journalists. Progress on transposing the EU Anti-SLAPP Directive is underway. Concerns persist about journalist safety, SLAPPs, and political interference.

- **Access to Documents and Civic Space**: Some progress on access to public documents, but procedural complexities and non-binding appeal bodies remain. Civic space is "narrowed"; stakeholders raise concerns about disproportionate measures against protesters, including potential judicial bans on demonstrations. The Constitutional Court annulled some restrictive provisions, but obstacles remain.

- **Checks and Balances and Human Rights**: The Federal Human Rights Institute warns about budget cuts to equality bodies (Unia). Compliance with final national court rulings is weak, and the government is under Council of Europe supervision for implementing European Court of Human Rights judgments (e.g., *Camara v. Belgium*). The rate of implementing leading judgments is 70%, with some cases pending for over a decade.
