## Executive Summary

The 2025 Country Report for Italy, issued by the European Commission, outlines a nation at a critical juncture of macroeconomic stabilization and structural transformation. Following a significant reduction in the government deficit to 3.4% of GDP in 2024, Italy has achieved its first primary surplus since 2019. However, the public debt-to-GDP ratio remains on an upward trajectory, projected to reach 136.7% by 2025, driven by lagged fiscal impacts of past housing renovation tax credits and unfavorable interest-growth differentials.

The report identifies high public debt, weak productivity growth, and structural rigidities as key vulnerabilities. While employment rates have reached historic highs, labor market participation remains low, particularly among women and young people, exacerbated by regional disparities between the North and the Mezzogiorno (South). The economic outlook is characterized by moderate growth, supported by Recovery and Resilience Plan (RRP) funding, but constrained by global uncertainty, trade policy risks, and high energy costs.

Key policy challenges include the need to accelerate RRP implementation, improve public administration and judicial efficiency, and address deep-seated structural issues such as skills mismatches, high tax evasion, and regional inequalities. The report emphasizes that despite improvements in banking sector resilience and fiscal consolidation, sustained growth requires decisive action on productivity, innovation, and social fairness to ensure long-term debt sustainability and competitiveness.

## Key Highlights

- **[Fiscal Consolidation Achieved]**: Italy's government deficit fell sharply to 3.4% of GDP in 2024 from 7.2% in 2023, resulting in a primary surplus of 0.4% of GDP, the first since 2019, driven by cuts in housing renovation tax credits and buoyant personal income tax revenues.
- **[Debt Trajectory Concerns]**: Despite the primary surplus, the public debt-to-GDP ratio is projected to rise to 136.7% by end-2025, reversing a downward trend seen up to 2023, due to sizeable borrowing needs linked to lagged fiscal impacts of past tax credits and unfavorable interest-growth differentials.
- **[Growth Stabilization]**: Real GDP growth stabilized at 0.7% in 2024, with forecasts indicating slow but steady expansion (0.6-0.9%) through 2026, supported by household consumption and corporate investment linked to RRP funding.
- **[Labor Market Paradox]**: Employment rates reached historic highs, yet labor force participation remains low, especially among women and youth. Structural mismatches persist, with high unemployment among the low-qualified and significant regional disparities in employment outcomes.
- **[Productivity and Innovation Deficit]**: Weak productivity growth remains a core vulnerability, attributed to insufficient investment in R&D (1.31% of GDP vs. EU average 2.22%), limited venture capital, and a dominance of micro-enterprises with low innovation capacity.
- **[Judicial and Administrative Reform]**: Significant efforts are underway to improve judicial efficiency and reduce case backlogs through RRP-supported recruitment of court staff, though the duration of civil trials remains the highest in the EU.
- **[Energy and Climate Vulnerability]**: Italy faces high exposure to climate risks, with significant economic losses from extreme weather events. High wholesale electricity prices, driven by gas dependency, stifle industrial competitiveness, prompting a push for accelerated renewable deployment and grid interconnections.
- **[Regional Disparities]**: The Mezzogiorno (South) continues to lag significantly behind the North in GDP per capita, productivity, and access to public services, with demographic decline and brain drain exacerbating regional inequalities.
- **[Banking Sector Resilience]**: The banking sector has shown improved resilience with strong capital adequacy ratios (20%) and decreasing non-performing loans (2.8%), though exposure to sovereign debt and state-guaranteed loans remains a concern for some cooperative banks.
- **[Social Fairness Challenges]**: In-work poverty rates remain high, particularly among part-time and fixed-term workers. The new minimum income scheme (Assegno di Inclusione) has reduced coverage and poverty-alleviation effects compared to its predecessor, highlighting ongoing challenges in social protection adequacy.
