# Structural features

**Structural features** represent the foundational, long-term characteristics of a sovereign entity that determine its baseline creditworthiness. In the context of **Fitch Ratings**' **Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)**, this is the most heavily weighted analytical pillar, accounting for **53.7%** of the model's output. It serves as the primary determinant of a sovereign's capacity to meet debt obligations by assessing underlying economic development, political stability, and institutional quality.

## Core Components in Sovereign Rating Models

The assessment of structural features relies on a combination of quantitative variables within the SRM and qualitative adjustments via the **Qualitative Overlay (QO)**.

### 1. Quantitative Drivers (SRM Variables)
The SRM uses Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression to score the following key metrics, which serve as proxies for a country's economic strength and vulnerability:

*   **Governance Indicators**: Derived from the **World Bank** Worldwide Governance Indicators, these metrics evaluate the quality of public institutions, rule of law, control of corruption, and government effectiveness. Strong governance is critical for policy credibility and debt sustainability.
*   **GDP Per Capita**: Used as a primary proxy for economic development and income levels. Higher per capita income generally correlates with greater tax bases, deeper domestic capital markets, and higher resilience to economic shocks.
*   **Share in World GDP**: This variable assesses the size of the economy relative to the global total. Small economies are often viewed as more vulnerable to external shocks, having less diversified export bases and limited influence in international financial markets.
*   **Default History**: Specifically, the number of years since the last default or restructuring. This historical metric serves as a lagging indicator of willingness to pay and institutional memory regarding debt obligations.

### 2. Qualitative Adjustments (Qualitative Overlay)
While the SRM provides a baseline score, the **Qualitative Overlay (QO)** allows analysts to adjust the rating by up to **±2 notches** for this pillar (within the overall ±3 notch cap for the entire rating) to account for factors not fully captured by the quantitative model:

*   **Political Stability & Geopolitical Risks**: Assessments of the likelihood of political upheaval, regime change, or external geopolitical conflicts that could disrupt economic activity or policy continuity.
*   **Financial Sector Health**: Evaluated through the **Banking Systemic Indicator (BSI)** and **Macro-Prudential Indicator (MPI)**. A fragile banking system can amplify sovereign risk through contingent liabilities and reduced credit transmission.
*   **Business Environment Quality**: Factors such as regulatory efficiency, property rights protection, and ease of doing business, which influence long-term investment flows and productivity growth.

## Strategic Significance in Credit Analysis

The dominance of structural features in the Fitch model (over 50% weight) highlights their role as the "anchor" of sovereign credit analysis:

1.  **Baseline Resilience**: Strong structural features provide a buffer against cyclical downturns. Countries with high governance scores and diversified economies are better positioned to implement adjustment policies during crises without requiring external assistance.
2.  **Policy Credibility**: Structural attributes directly influence the **Qualitative Overlay** assessments of policy credibility. Institutions with strong structural foundations are more likely to see their fiscal and monetary policies implemented effectively.
3.  **Differentiation from Other Pillars**: While **Public Finances** and **External Finances** reflect current and near-term fiscal/external positions, Structural Features reflect the *long-term* trajectory. A country may have temporary fiscal stress but strong structural features, leading to a higher rating than a country with strong short-term balances but weak institutions.

## Integration with Emerging Risks

Recent updates to the methodology (effective September 2025) have begun to integrate new structural considerations:

*   **Climate Risk**: The introduction of **Climate Vulnerability Signals (Climate.VS)** adds a layer to structural analysis. Sovereigns with high exposure to physical risks (e.g., floods, heatwaves) or transition risks (e.g., fossil fuel dependence) may face structural downgrades if these risks are deemed material and not already reflected in standard economic metrics.
*   **Long-Term Prospects**: Structural features are increasingly viewed through a multi-decade lens, incorporating projections for demographic trends and technological adaptation capabilities, which underpin the 2035 and 2050 climate and economic scenarios.
