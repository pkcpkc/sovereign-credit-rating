# Current account deficit

A **current account deficit** represents a situation where a country's total spending on trade in goods and services, income, and transfers exceeds its total earnings from these items. It indicates that a nation is a net borrower from the rest of the world, financing the excess of domestic spending over domestic production through capital inflows. In the context of sovereign credit rating and macroeconomic stability analysis, the sustainability of this deficit is assessed by its magnitude relative to GDP, its financing sources (e.g., foreign direct investment vs. short-term portfolio flows), and the country's external liquidity position.

## Macroeconomic Significance and Sovereign Credit Risk

The current account balance is a critical indicator of external competitiveness and fiscal sustainability. A persistent or widening deficit can signal structural weaknesses, such as low productivity, overvalued exchange rates, or excessive domestic demand driven by fiscal expansion. For credit rating agencies and institutional investors, the current account deficit is analyzed alongside the **Net International Investment Position (NIIP)** and **external debt** to determine vulnerability to external shocks and shifts in investor sentiment.

Key risk factors associated with a widening current account deficit include:

*   **Financing Reliance**: Deficits must be financed by capital account surpluses (inflows). Reliance on volatile short-term capital inflows or bank lending increases refinancing risks.
*   **Exchange Rate Pressure**: Large deficits can lead to currency depreciation pressures, potentially exacerbating inflation and increasing the local currency value of external debt.
*   **Fiscal Linkages**: In economies with fixed or managed exchange rates, current account deficits are often mirrored by high fiscal deficits, as excessive public spending fuels import demand.
*   **Debt Sustainability**: Persistent deficits contribute to a deteriorating NIIP (becoming more negative), increasing the interest burden on foreign creditors and reducing national income available for domestic consumption or investment.

## Case Study: Romania (2024–2025)

The 2025 Country Report for **Romania** highlights a critical juncture where macroeconomic vulnerabilities are exacerbated by a widening current account deficit. This case illustrates the direct linkage between fiscal policy, external imbalances, and sovereign credit risks.

### Key Indicators and Dynamics

*   **Deficit Widening**: Romania’s current account deficit widened to **8.4% of GDP in 2024**, a significant deterioration driven by high import demand resulting from fiscal expansion and weak external competitiveness.
*   **Drivers of Imbalance**:
    *   **Fiscal Expansion**: A government deficit of **9.3% of GDP** in 2024, fueled by generous public sector wage increases and higher public investment, boosted domestic demand but primarily channeled into imports rather than domestic production.
    *   **Weak Net Exports**: Real GDP growth slowed to 0.8% in 2024, driven by private consumption but dampened by weak net exports and a slowdown in private investment.
    *   **Energy and Competitiveness**: High energy prices and a lack of structural productivity gains contributed to external pressures. The exchange rate, managed by the **National Bank of Romania** to remain steady against the Euro, limits the automatic adjustment mechanism that typically corrects trade imbalances.
*   **External Position**:
    *   Romania remains a net borrower from the rest of the world, with a **Negative Net International Investment Position (NIIP) of -41.5% of GDP**.
    *   Liabilities to foreigners amount to 83% of GDP, while assets on foreigners stand at 39% of GDP.
    *   Despite the negative NIIP, the country maintains a relatively low net external debt position, mitigated by strong official foreign reserves (20.8% of GDP).

### Implications for Credit Rating and Policy

The widening current account deficit poses several threats to Romania's sovereign credit profile and macroeconomic stability:

1.  **Financing Risks**: The deficit increases reliance on foreign capital inflows to finance domestic investment and consumption. Any sudden stop in capital flows could lead to funding shortfalls, forcing reliance on national budget resources or emergency financing.
2.  **Currency Vulnerability**: While the **Lei** is not a reserve currency, the managed float regime provides some flexibility. However, persistent deficits increase the risk of devaluation if investor confidence wanes, which could trigger imported inflation.
3.  **Fiscal-External Nexus**: The current account deficit is closely linked to the fiscal deficit. The European Commission’s endorsement of Romania’s **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP)** (2025–2031) aims to address this by consolidating fiscal policy. Measures such as public wage freezes and tax reforms are intended to reduce domestic demand pressures and, consequently, import volumes, thereby narrowing the current account gap.
4.  **Investment Climate**: Regulatory uncertainty and legislative instability are cited as top barriers to long-term investment. This affects the quality of capital inflows; if inflows are not comprised of stable Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) but rather volatile portfolio investments, the external position remains fragile.

### Mitigating Strengths

Despite the risks, Romania possesses several strengths that support external liquidity:
*   **Strong Reserves**: Official foreign reserves at 20.8% of GDP provide a buffer against external shocks.
*   **Banking Sector Health**: The banking sector is well-capitalized (CET1 ratio of 20%) with low non-performing loans, reducing the risk of a banking sector crisis triggered by external imbalances.
*   **EU Membership and Schengen Accession**: Joining the Schengen Area in 2025 is expected to facilitate trade and foreign investment, potentially improving the external position over the medium term.
