# Loss severity and recovery ratings

**Loss severity and recovery ratings** are analytical tools used by credit rating agencies to estimate the potential financial loss investors may incur if a sovereign borrower defaults on its obligations. These metrics quantify the **Recovery Rate**—the percentage of the principal value expected to be recovered over the life of the bond following a default event—thereby informing the **Loss Given Default (LGD)**.

In the context of sovereign credit analysis, specifically within the [[Fitch Ratings Sovereign Rating Criteria (Effective 15 September 2025)]], these ratings are critical for differentiating the risk profile of specific debt instruments from the overall sovereign creditworthiness. They are primarily applied to sub-investment grade sovereigns to adjust instrument ratings relative to the sovereign's [[Issuer Default Rating (IDR)]].

## Methodological Framework (Fitch Ratings)

### Applicability and Thresholds
Fitch Ratings incorporates a view on potential recoveries via **Recovery Ratings (RRs)** specifically for sovereign issuers with a Long-Term [[Issuer Default Rating (IDR)]] of **'B+'** or below. For investment-grade issuers (rated **'BB-'** and above), instrument ratings generally align with the IDR, under the assumption of average recovery prospects and lower probability of default requiring granular loss severity analysis.

### Recovery Rating Scale
The framework utilizes a discrete scale to categorize expected recoveries:
*   **Scale:** **RR1** to **RR6**.
*   **Function:** These ratings reflect the estimated median recovery amount (as a percentage of principal) expected in a default scenario.
*   **Impact:** The assigned Recovery Rating is used to notch the rating of specific debt instruments up or down from the sovereign's base IDR.

### Instrument Rating Adjustments
For sub-investment grade sovereigns, the final rating of a specific debt instrument is derived by adjusting the sovereign's IDR based on the instrument's Recovery Rating.
*   **Notching Range:** Senior unsecured instrument ratings may be notched up by as much as **+3 notches** or down by as much as **-2 notches** from the IDR.
*   **Rationale:** This adjustment accounts for legal and structural factors that may enhance or impair recovery prospects relative to the general sovereign risk.

## Determinants of Loss Severity and Recovery

The assessment of loss severity is driven by several structural and legal factors inherent to the sovereign's debt profile and governance:

1.  **Legal Status of Debt:** The priority of claims in a restructuring or bankruptcy-like scenario. Senior unsecured debt typically holds a specific position in the creditor hierarchy.
2.  **Debt Structure and Maturity:** The presence of short-term debt versus long-term debt, and the composition of local currency (LC) versus foreign currency (FC) debt.
3.  **Sovereign Restructuring History:** A history of [[Distressed Debt Exchanges (DDE)]] or past defaults influences the expectation of future recoveries. Fitch defines a DDE by a material reduction in debt terms (principal, interest, maturity, or currency) designed to avoid traditional default.
4.  **Currency of Denomination:**
    *   **Local Currency (LC):** May offer different recovery prospects if the sovereign has [[Fiscal financing flexibility]] and control over the monetary base, potentially allowing for higher effective recoveries via inflation or monetary financing, though this carries its own risks.
    *   **Foreign Currency (FC):** Recoveries are often more rigidly tied to external liquidity and negotiation outcomes with international creditors.
5.  **Contingent Liabilities:** The potential crystallization of [[Contingent liabilities]], such as support for the [[Banking Systemic Indicator (BSI)]] or State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs), can impact the overall debt burden and the capacity to pay in a distressed scenario.

## Integration with Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)

While the [[Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)]] primarily focuses on the *probability* of default (via pillars like [[Structural features]], [[Public finances]], and [[External finances]]), Recovery Ratings address the *severity* of loss.

*   **Distinct Risk Dimension:** A sovereign may have a high probability of default (low IDR) but potentially high recovery rates (high RR) if it has a strong history of honoring debt or robust legal frameworks for restructuring. Conversely, a sovereign with a history of disorderly defaults may face both low IDRs and low RRs.
*   **Qualitative Overlay (QO):** Factors influencing recovery, such as political stability and the credibility of institutional frameworks, may also feed into the [[Qualitative Overlay (QO)]] adjustments for the overarching IDR.

## Significance for Investors and Analysts

1.  **Yield Differential Analysis:** Investors use Recovery Ratings to assess the risk-adjusted yield of sovereign bonds. A notch-up due to high recovery prospects can make a sub-investment grade bond more attractive.
2.  **Portfolio Risk Management:** Understanding loss severity helps in stress-testing portfolios against default scenarios. Knowing the expected loss given default is crucial for calculating Economic Capital and Value at Risk (VaR).
3.  **Restructuring Strategy:** For holders of distressed sovereign debt, the Recovery Rating provides a benchmark for expected outcomes in potential negotiations or Debt Distressed Exchanges.
