# Inflation

Inflation refers to the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services is rising, subsequently eroding purchasing power. In the context of contemporary economic reporting, it serves as a critical indicator of macroeconomic stability and fiscal health.

## Current Context: Hungary (2023–2025)
Recent data highlights significant inflationary pressures within the Hungarian economy:
- **Peak Inflation:** Hungary recorded a peak inflation rate of **17.0% in January 2023**.
- **Drivers:** Persistent price pressures are currently driven by underlying economic factors and rising food costs.
- **Impact on Growth:** High inflation has contributed to a period of slow economic recovery, with GDP growth remaining modest (0.5% in 2024) as the economy navigates structural vulnerabilities.

## Economic Interdependencies
Inflation does not exist in isolation and interacts with several key economic indicators:
- **[[GDP Growth]]**: High inflation can dampen real growth by increasing costs for consumers and businesses.
- **[[Public Debt]]**: Inflationary environments often correlate with high debt-servicing costs. In Hungary, public debt reached 73.5% of GDP in 2024, complicated by elevated interest rates.
- **Wage Dynamics & Unit Labour Costs (ULCs)**: While nominal wages may rise rapidly to keep pace with inflation, there is a risk that rising unit labour costs could undermine international competitiveness if they are not matched by corresponding gains in productivity.
- **Cost of Living**: Rapid price increases have contributed to a deteriorating housing market, where house prices in Hungary saw the highest increase in the EU (230% between 2010 and 2024), leading to significant cost overburdens for low-income households.
