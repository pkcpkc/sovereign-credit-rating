# Green transition

The **green transition** refers to the structural shift toward a low-carbon economy, encompassing energy decarbonization, renewable energy deployment, building renovation, and climate adaptation. In the context of sovereign credit profiles for major EU economies like **France**, **Italy**, and **Belgium**, as well as emerging economies like **Romania** and **Slovakia**, this transition presents a complex interplay between long-term competitiveness gains, energy security, and short-term fiscal, administrative, and social challenges.

While all five nations are committed to the European Green Deal and utilize **Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF/RRP)** funds to support this shift, their starting positions, specific bottlenecks, and associated macroeconomic risks differ significantly. The transition is increasingly viewed as a core determinant of **[[fiscal sustainability]]**, **[[growth_prospects]]**, and sovereign risk, with failure to implement reforms threatening debt trajectories, external stability, and industrial competitiveness.

## Strategic Importance and Country-Specific Status

### Belgium: Fiscal Pressure vs. Climate Commitments
Belgium presents a profile characterized by a strong industrial base and high public debt, where the green transition imposes significant additional fiscal pressures amidst a need for broad fiscal consolidation.

*   **Climate Targets and Coordination Gaps**: Belgium’s current projections fall short of EU Effort Sharing Regulation goals for 2030. A major bottleneck is the lack of coordination between federal and regional entities, leading to fragmented climate policies. The IMF emphasizes the need for a coherent national strategy consolidating these efforts, potentially through a revitalized fiscal council with binding burden-sharing rules.
*   **Fiscal Space Constraints**: With public debt at 104.1% of GDP in 2024 and projected to rise to 123% by 2030 under unchanged policies, fiscal space for new green investments is limited. The green transition, alongside defense spending and aging population costs, contributes to projected fiscal deficits.
*   **Structural Reform Needs**: To finance the transition without exacerbating debt unsustainability, Belgium must improve the efficiency of public investment in infrastructure and healthcare. Additionally, shifting the tax burden from labor to capital and environmental sources is recommended to support competitiveness while generating revenue for green initiatives.
*   **Innovation and R&D**: Belgium possesses an innovative economy with high R&D spending, providing a potential strength for adopting green technologies. However, sluggish labor productivity growth limits the economy's capacity to absorb the costs of transition efficiently.

### France: High Low-Carbon Baseline, Administrative Bottlenecks
France holds a strong position in low-carbon energy generation, with **95% of electricity production** derived from low-carbon sources, primarily nuclear power. This high baseline provides a competitive advantage in energy-intensive industries and contributes to energy security. However, the transition is currently hampered by structural bottlenecks rather than technological capability.

*   **Renewable Energy Deployment**: Progress is stalled by complex permitting procedures and the absence of a firm national renewable energy target for 2030.
*   **Building Decarbonization**: Significant shortfalls exist in deep renovation targets. These are driven by budget constraints, high administrative burdens, and acute **skills shortages** in the construction and renovation sectors.
*   **Regulatory Environment**: High administrative burdens and regulatory restrictions persist, particularly in sectors critical to the green economy, hindering investment efficiency.

### Italy: Industrial Strength vs. Structural Vulnerabilities
Italy presents a contrasting profile, characterized by strong manufacturing capabilities in clean technologies but significant vulnerabilities in energy costs and climate resilience.

*   **Clean Tech Leadership**: Italy is a leader in clean technology manufacturing, particularly in **solar PV** and **wind** components, as well as automotive supply chains. This diversifies its export base and supports industrial competitiveness.
*   **Energy Cost Competitiveness**: Unlike France, Italy faces high wholesale electricity prices due to a gas-heavy energy mix and significant taxes/levies. This undermines the competitiveness of energy-intensive industries and increases vulnerability to global fossil fuel price shocks.
*   **Climate Risk Exposure**: Italy is highly exposed to physical climate risks, including floods and droughts, with a large insurance protection gap. This necessitates urgent and costly adaptation investments.
*   **Supply Chain Dependencies**: The country relies heavily on imports for critical raw materials and semiconductors, posing supply chain risks that threaten the scaling of green technologies.

### Romania: Rapid Fiscal Expansion vs. Green Investment Needs
Romania represents a case of rapid economic convergence hampered by fiscal indiscipline and administrative delays in implementing green reforms.

*   **Renewable Progress vs. Grid Constraints**: Romania has made notable progress, with renewables accounting for **48% of electricity generation** in 2024. However, energy prices remain high due to lingering fossil fuel dependence and grid limitations.
*   **Implementation Lag**: The implementation of the **Recovery and Resilience Plan (RRP)** is significantly delayed, with only **14% of milestones and targets fulfilled**. Key bottlenecks include procurement delays, political instability, and weak administrative capacity, slowing down critical green infrastructure projects.
*   **Fossil Fuel Subsidies**: The country faces challenges in decarbonizing industry and phasing out fossil fuel subsidies, which distort the market and hinder the transition.
*   **Climate Vulnerability**: Romania is highly exposed to climate-related risks, including floods and droughts. Water management and wastewater treatment compliance remain poor, creating long-term fiscal liabilities if not addressed.

### Slovakia: Energy Security Dilemma and Institutional Backsliding
Slovakia represents a unique risk profile where the green transition is constrained by deep institutional weaknesses, geopolitical energy dependencies, and a concentrated industrial base.

*   **Energy Security and Geopolitical Risk**: Slovakia’s energy mix remains vulnerable due to heavy reliance on Russian fossil fuels (over **80% of crude oil** and ~**70% of natural gas**). While the nuclear fleet is expanding, this dependence creates significant geopolitical vulnerability and exposure to supply shocks.
*   **Slow Renewable Transition**: The transition to renewables is slow, with only **24% of electricity** coming from renewable sources in 2024. High energy prices for non-household consumers hinder industrial competitiveness, particularly for the dominant automotive sector.
*   **Institutional and Rule of Law Barriers**: Sovereign credit risks are exacerbated by severe governance deficits. The dismantling of specialized anti-corruption agencies (National Crime Agency, Special Prosecution Office) and reduced transparency in public procurement undermine the efficient allocation of green investment funds. 85% of companies cite a high perceived level of corruption as a barrier.
*   **RRP Implementation Challenges**: Slovakia has fulfilled **33% of RRP milestones**, but implementation is slowed by inefficient public procurement, administrative fragmentation, and insufficient project preparation. Accelerating the absorption of these EU funds is critical to supporting the green and digital transitions, yet regulatory unpredictability poses a risk to long-term project viability.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit and Fiscal Sustainability

The green transition is a critical factor in the **[[fiscal sustainability]]** and **[[debt_burden]]** analysis for these countries, creating distinct fiscal pressures and investment imperatives.

### France: Balancing Consolidation and Investment
1.  **Fiscal Pressure vs. Investment Needs**:
    *   France faces a high general government deficit (5.8% of GDP in 2024) and rising public debt (113% of GDP).
    *   The Commission emphasizes that **fiscal consolidation efforts must not undermine essential investments** in the green transition. This creates a policy dilemma: reducing deficits to improve creditworthiness may slow down necessary green infrastructure spending, while insufficient investment risks long-term competitiveness and environmental compliance costs.
    *   **Contingent Liabilities**: State-owned enterprises like **[[EDF]]** play a central role in the energy transition. Risks associated with these entities pose potential **contingent liabilities** for the state, impacting sovereign credit rating factors.

2.  **Administrative Efficiency and Credit Rating Factors**:
    *   **Policy Credibility**: The complexity of administrative procedures impacting businesses is cited as a threat to policy credibility. Streamlining green transition projects requires reducing this burden.
    *   **Policymaking Stability**: Delays in reform implementation, exacerbated by political fragmentation post-2024, risk delaying crucial green transition milestones, potentially affecting **[[growth_prospects]]** and the effective use of EU funds.

3.  **EU Funding and Structural Reforms**:
    *   France is advanced in implementing **Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF)** measures (82% of milestones fulfilled). Continued successful implementation is vital for accessing funds that support the green transition without adding to the debt burden.
    *   The **Medium-term fiscal-structural plan** endorsed by the Council in 2025 requires robust spending reviews to ensure that green transition investments are efficient and do not exacerbate fiscal imbalances.

### Italy: Debt Dynamics and Fiscal Flexibility
1.  **Fiscal Constraints on Green Spending**:
    *   Italy achieved a primary surplus in 2024, but the public debt-to-GDP ratio is projected to rise to **136.7% in 2025**, driven by lagged borrowing needs from past tax credits (Superbonus phase-out) and sizeable debt service costs (approx. 3.9% of GDP).
    *   High debt levels and rising pension costs due to demographic aging restrict **fiscal policy flexibility**, making it difficult to finance new green investments without risking debt sustainability.

2.  **Investment Efficiency and R&D**:
    *   Sluggish productivity growth is driven by insufficient **R&D investment** (private R&D is half the EU average) and an underdeveloped venture capital market. This limits the domestic financing options for innovative green startups and SMEs.
    *   The **Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRP)** is a critical funding source for digital and green transitions, helping to offset weak private investment. However, the effectiveness of these funds depends on overcoming judicial inefficiencies and bureaucratic hurdles.

3.  **Contingent Liabilities and Banking Sector**:
    *   Banks remain heavily exposed to domestic sovereign debt, creating a "doom loop" risk. While the banking sector has improved resilience (capital adequacy at 20%, NPLs at 2.8%), any fiscal slip triggered by underfunded climate adaptation or green transition failures could spill over into financial stability.
    *   Potential support needs for state-owned enterprises and local governments with limited fiscal capacity (particularly in the South) pose further contingent liability risks.

### Romania: Fiscal Slippage and RRP Implementation Risks
1.  **High Deficit and Debt Accumulation**:
    *   Romania’s government deficit surged to **9.3% of GDP** in 2024, driven by generous public sector wage increases and high public investment spending. Public debt rose rapidly to **54.8% of GDP**.
    *   The surge in deficit limits fiscal space for additional green investments. The **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP)** aims to correct this over 2025-2031, but the immediate focus on consolidation (wage freezes, tax reforms) risks crowding out long-term structural investments in green infrastructure.

2.  **RRP Delays and Investment Inefficiency**:
    *   With only **14% of RRP milestones fulfilled**, Romania risks losing access to crucial non-repayable EU funds designated for green and digital transitions. This delays the modernization of infrastructure and the reduction of carbon intensity.
    *   Weak administrative capacity and corruption risks in public procurement undermine the efficiency of green investments, potentially leading to higher costs and lower environmental impact per euro spent.

3.  **External Vulnerabilities and Energy Costs**:
    *   The current account deficit widened to **8.4% of GDP** in 2024. High energy prices and reliance on imported fossil fuels for certain sectors contribute to external pressures.
    *   A slow green transition exacerbates trade imbalances, as failure to decarbonize industry reduces competitiveness against EU peers and increases exposure to Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) costs.

### Slovakia: Fiscal Rigidity and Structural Reform Needs
1.  **Fiscal Constraints and Tax Mix Inefficiencies**:
    *   Slovakia faces a persistent structural deficit (**5.3% of GDP** in 2024) and a rising debt trajectory (projected to reach **63.0% of GDP** by 2026). The tax system is skewed towards labor taxes, with insufficient use of **environmental and property taxation**. This inefficiency limits the government's ability to generate revenue for green investments without further straining the labor market.
    *   Recent consolidation measures (VAT and CIT increases) aim to stabilize debt but may dampen near-term growth, reducing the fiscal space for long-term green infrastructure.

2.  **Contingent Liabilities in Healthcare and SOEs**:
    *   Significant debt in the hospital sector and state-owned enterprises creates off-budget risks. If the green transition requires modernization of these assets, the state faces significant contingent liabilities that could strain already tight fiscal positions.

3.  **RRP Implementation and Administrative Fragmentation**:
    *   While Slovakia has absorbed some EU funds, implementation is hindered by administrative fragmentation and weak project preparation. The deadline for full RRP implementation (August 2026) poses a risk of unspent funds if administrative bottlenecks are not resolved.
    *   The underdeveloped capital market (venture capital activity at **0.01% of GDP**) restricts private financing for green SMEs, leaving the state to bear the disproportionate burden of transition financing.

### Belgium: High Debt and Coordination Challenges
1.  **Fiscal Consolidation vs. Green Investment**:
    *   Belgium’s public debt is projected to rise to **123% of GDP by 2030** under unchanged policies, largely driven by rigid spending and wage indexation. The IMF recommends a 7-year fiscal adjustment to reduce debt to ~78% of GDP.
    *   **Green Transition Costs**: The green transition, alongside defense and aging population costs, is cited as a key pressure on the fiscal deficit. The IMF stresses that fiscal adjustment must prioritize rationalizing current spending (social benefits, public wages) rather than cutting public investment, which is essential for green infrastructure.
    *   **Efficiency Gains**: Improving the efficiency of public spending in healthcare, education, and infrastructure is crucial to free up resources for climate goals without worsening debt sustainability.

2.  **Tax Reform and Competitiveness**:
    *   Belgium has a high labor tax wedge (52.7%). The IMF recommends shifting the tax burden from labor to capital and environmental sources. This reform would simultaneously improve labor market efficiency and generate revenue to support the green transition, enhancing competitiveness.
    *   Phasing out fossil fuel subsidies and implementing carbon taxation are recommended to meet climate targets while reducing fiscal distortions.

3.  **Regional Coordination and Policy Credibility**:
    *   Weak coordination among federal and regional governments regarding climate and fiscal targets undermines policy effectiveness. The IMF calls for a coherent national strategy to consolidate federal and regional efforts, potentially enforced by a revitalized fiscal council.
    *   Failure to coordinate risks missing 2030 climate targets, which could lead to EU penalties and increased long-term fiscal liabilities from climate adaptation.

Low tertiary education attainment and low labor force participation (especially among women at 57.4% vs EU avg 70.8%) hamper the workforce's ability to transition to green jobs.
    *   **Romania**: Despite an improved employment rate of 69.5%, severe skills mismatches exist. High demand for STEM and digital skills is not met by the education system. Early school leaving remains high, and vocational education and training (VET) relevance to the labor market is weak, hindering the absorption of green technologies.
    *   **Slovakia**: The labor market is tight with historic lows in unemployment (5.1%), but this masks significant structural issues. There are severe shortages of skilled workers in healthcare and education, exacerbated by an aging workforce. The transition of the automotive sector to electric vehicle (EV) production requires upskilling, but low private R&D (**0.58% of GDP**) limits innovation-driven job creation.
    *   **Belgium**: Sluggish labor productivity growth is a key concern. The IMF recommends aligning education curricula with market needs to improve efficiency. While Belgium has high R&D spending, translating this into widespread productivity gains requires better labor market integration and reducing hiring/firing costs to facilitate the shift toward green sectors.

*   **Social Fairness and Regional Disparities**:
    *   **France**: Inadequate progress in building decarbonization can worsen energy poverty, particularly among children and in outermost regions, exacerbating **social fairness** concerns.

The South is particularly vulnerable to climate risks and has lower capacity to invest in green infrastructure, potentially widening the economic divide.

High poverty rates (AROPE at 27.9%) and inadequate social protection mean that the costs of the green transition (e.g., higher energy prices) disproportionately affect vulnerable households. Rural areas face specific challenges in waste management and access to clean energy.
    *   **Slovakia**: Deep socio-economic disparities persist, with the Roma community highly marginalized (unemployment rate >53%). High energy prices exert upward pressure on household budgets, risking energy poverty. The transition must address these inequalities to maintain social stability, which is already fragile due to institutional backsliding.

*   **Competitiveness**:

    *   **Italy** benefits from a strong clean-tech manufacturing base but is hampered by high energy costs and energy poverty risks.
    *   **Romania** has a diversified export base but faces risks from its low R&D intensity (0.52% of GDP, lowest in EU) and fragmented innovation system. Failure to integrate green innovation threatens its long-term industrial competitiveness.
