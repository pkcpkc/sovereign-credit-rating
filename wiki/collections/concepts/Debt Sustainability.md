# Debt Sustainability

**Debt Sustainability** refers to the assessment of a sovereign entity's ability and willingness to service its financial obligations to non-official commercial creditors over the medium to long term without default or requiring excessive external assistance. It is a core component of **Sovereign Credit Rating** analysis, serving as the primary determinant of a country's creditworthiness and borrowing costs.

Sustainability is not defined by the absolute level of debt alone, but by the interplay between the existing **Debt Burden**, fiscal performance, economic growth prospects, institutional capacity, and structural reforms. A high debt ratio may be sustainable if supported by strong economic growth, low interest rates, and credible fiscal frameworks. Conversely, lower debt levels may be unsustainable if accompanied by weak institutions, high inflation, or limited market access.

## Core Assessment Frameworks

### S&P Global Ratings Methodology (2017)
According to the **S&P Global Sovereign Rating Methodology**, debt sustainability is evaluated through a five-pillar framework: Institutional, Economic, External, Fiscal, and Monetary.
*   **Fiscal Assessment**: The methodology bases initial fiscal assessments on changes in **Net General Government Debt** rather than gross debt, focusing on the trajectory of debt accumulation.
*   **Indicative Rating Matrix**: Ratings are derived by combining the Institutional/Economic Profile with the External/Fiscal/Monetary Profile.
*   **Supplemental Adjustments**: Extremely high fiscal debt burdens can trigger supplemental adjustments that lower the rating by more than one notch, even if other pillars are strong. This acts as a cap on the maximum achievable rating based on debt risk.

### European Commission Surveillance
The **European Commission** assesses debt sustainability through the **Stability and Growth Pact** framework, focusing on:
*   **Long-term Fiscal Sustainability**: Projecting future deficits and debt dynamics under current policies.
*   **Structural Expenditure Pressure**: Analyzing the impact of aging costs (pensions, healthcare) on long-term budget balances.
*   **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plans**: Evaluating government commitments to deficit reduction and structural reforms. Countries such as **Italy**, **Malta**, **Poland**, **France**, and **Romania** are currently subject to or have recently completed such plans to correct excessive deficits.

### IMF Article IV Consultations
The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** evaluates debt sustainability through Article IV consultations, focusing on:
*   **Debt Trajectory Analysis**: Assessing whether public debt is on a solidly downward path under unchanged policies. Recent assessments for **France**, **Poland**, **Romania**, and **Slovakia** highlight the critical role of fiscal consolidation plans in stabilizing debt-to-GDP ratios.
*   **Fiscal Space and Consolidation**: Evaluating the government's capacity to raise revenues or cut spending to offset rising pressures (e.g., aging, defense, green transition). The IMF increasingly emphasizes "frontloaded" structural efforts to rebuild buffers.
*   **Structural Reforms**: Linking debt sustainability to productivity-enhancing reforms, labor market flexibility, and public administration efficiency.

### Fitch Ratings Sovereign Rating Criteria (2025-2026)
Fitch Ratings employs a comprehensive framework for assigning sovereign credit ratings, defining credit risk as a forward-looking assessment of the government's capacity and willingness to honor debt obligations. The methodology is anchored by four analytical pillars: Structural Features, Macroeconomic Performance, Public Finances, and External Finances.

*   **Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)**: A quantitative multiple-regression model using 18 key variables serves as the starting point for rating assignment. The pillars are weighted as follows: Structural Features (53.7%), External Finances (17.3%), Public Finances (19.1%), and Macroeconomic Performance (9.8%).
*   **Qualitative Overlay (QO)**: Analysts adjust the SRM output by up to +3/-3 notches to account for factors not fully captured by the model, such as political stability, financial sector health, and geopolitical risks.
*   **Public Finances Assessment**: Focuses on gross general government debt/GDP, interest payments/revenue, fiscal balance, and foreign-currency debt share. The QO evaluates fiscal financing flexibility, debt sustainability (using the **Debt Dynamics Model**), contingent liabilities, and demographic pressures.
*   **External Finances Evaluation**: Includes reserve-currency flexibility, sovereign net foreign assets, commodity dependence, FX reserves, external interest service, and current account balance + FDI. The QO assesses vulnerability to shocks using the **International Liquidity Ratio**.

## Key Determinants of Sustainability

### 1. Debt Burden and Structure
The sustainability of debt is heavily influenced by its composition and servicing costs.
*   **Interest Burden**: High interest payments relative to GDP or revenue can erode fiscal flexibility. For instance, **Hungary** faces high debt-servicing costs with implicit interest rates near 6%, despite moderate total debt levels (74.5% of GDP).
*   **Currency Denomination**: Debt denominated in foreign currency (FX) exposes sovereigns to exchange rate risk, potentially leading to unsustainability if the local currency depreciates. Conversely, **local-currency debt** (as seen in **Austria**, **France**, **Belgium**, and **Poland**) generally enhances sustainability by reducing refinancing risks and avoiding currency mismatches. Note: **Romania** faces specific risks from unhedged FX corporate loans and financial euroization.
*   **Maturity Profile**: Short maturities create refinancing pressure, while long-term bonds provide stability. **Belgium** maintains the longest average residual maturity in the Euro Area (11.1 years), significantly lowering refinancing risk despite high debt levels. **France** also benefits from a long average maturity (8 years).

### 2. Fiscal Performance and Flexibility
Sustainability depends on the government's ability to adjust fiscal balances.
*   **Primary Balance**: The ability to generate primary surpluses is crucial for stabilizing debt ratios. Countries like **Belgium** and **France** face challenges due to persistent deficits (exceeding 4-5% of GDP) and rising debt trajectories. **Poland** and **Romania** have recently widened deficits due to social benefits and wage increases, requiring urgent consolidation.
*   **Structural vs. Cyclical Deficits**: Sustainable debt dynamics require addressing structural imbalances (e.g., rigid expenditure on pensions and healthcare) rather than just cyclical downturns.
*   **Fiscal Rules**: The presence of credible fiscal rules and multiannual planning frameworks enhances sustainability by constraining political discretion. **Belgium** is noted for lacking a fully developed multiannual fiscal planning framework, increasing sustainability risks. **Slovakia** and **Poland** have implemented ambitious Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plans (MTFSP) to stabilize debt.

### 3. Macroeconomic Context
Economic growth and inflation dynamics directly impact the debt-to-GDP ratio.
*   **Growth-Difference Effect**: If nominal GDP growth exceeds the effective interest rate on debt, the debt ratio tends to stabilize or decline. **Austria**'s recessionary environment (negative GDP growth) contrasts with its strong institutional framework, creating a tension between economic weakness and fiscal resilience. **Poland**'s robust growth (2.8% in 2024) helps offset higher deficits, whereas **France**'s slowing growth (0.6% projected for 2025) exacerbates sustainability concerns.
*   **Inflation**: High inflation can erode the real value of domestic currency debt (benefiting the debtor) but often comes with higher interest rates and loss of credibility (hurting sustainability). **Hungary**'s high inflation volatility poses significant risks to its debt sustainability. **Romania** and **Slovakia** have seen inflation spikes due to tax reforms and energy price adjustments, impacting real debt dynamics.

### 4. Institutional and Political Risk
The willingness to maintain debt sustainability is linked to institutional quality.
*   **Institutional Strength**: Strong institutions, rule of law, and policy continuity are critical. **Austria** benefits from strong institutions and a culture of timely debt repayment, which supports sustainability despite high debt levels.
*   **Political Fragmentation**: Political instability can lead to policy reversals and budgetary delays, undermining sustainability. **France**'s fragmented political landscape has led to delayed budget adoption and slippage in deficit targets, raising medium-term fiscal sustainability risks. **Romania**'s recent government changes have raised questions about the implementation of fiscal consolidation packages.
*   **Rule of Law**: Weak rule of law and corruption, as identified in **Hungary**, increase the risk premium on borrowing and undermine investor confidence, threatening sustainability. **Slovakia** faces challenges regarding governance and judicial independence, which affect EU fund absorption and investor confidence.

### 5. Climate Risk and Long-Term Vulnerabilities
Recent frameworks, such as the **Sovereign Climate Risk Model (SCRM)** introduced by Fitch, integrate climate considerations into debt sustainability assessments.
*   **Climate Vulnerability Signal (Climate.VS)**: A screening tool (0-100 scale) assessing physical risks (e.g., heatwaves, floods) and transition risks (fossil fuel dependence, green energy costs). Sovereigns with a Climate.VS ≥ 50 in 2035 receive additional analytical scrutiny, potentially leading to Qualitative Overlay (QO) adjustments that reflect long-term credit risks.
*   **Transition Costs**: The cost of adapting to green energy transitions and carbon taxes impacts fiscal buffers, particularly for economies heavily reliant on fossil fuels or high-emission industries.

## Regional Case Studies in Debt Sustainability

### Italy
*   **Status**: High public debt (projected to reach 136.7% of GDP by 2025) creates significant sustainability risks, despite a return to primary surplus (0.4% of GDP in 2024).
*   **Key Factors**: Risks are driven by lagged fiscal impacts of past tax credits, unfavorable interest-growth differentials, and weak productivity growth.
*   **Mitigating Factors**: A strong record of timely debt repayment, credible governance framework, and positive net international investment position (NIIP) help mitigate external vulnerability. However, high structural expenditure, demographic pressures, and regional disparities (North vs. Mezzogiorno) pose long-term challenges.

### Malta
*   **Status**: Debt sustainability is assessed as low risk, with a debt-to-GDP ratio stabilizing below 50%.
*   **Key Factors**: Strong economic growth (6.0% in 2024), robust domestic demand, and a strong service sector (tourism, financial services, iGaming) support fiscal consolidation.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: Long-term fiscal sustainability is threatened by projected increases in age-related spending (pensions, healthcare). Structural weaknesses include low R&D investment, administrative burdens, and reliance on corporate taxation.

### Poland
*   **Status**: Debt sustainability is under pressure, with the general government deficit exceeding the 3% reference value (5.3% of GDP in 2023, projected 5.9% in 2024), triggering an **Excessive Deficit Procedure**.
*   **Key Factors**: The country has submitted a medium-term fiscal-structural plan aiming to reduce the deficit below 3% by 2028. High defense spending and public sector wage increases have driven the deficit.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: Rising budget deficits, public debt increase (projected 65% by 2028), and an aging society putting pressure on the pension system are key threats.
*   **Mitigating Factors**: Fastest growing economy in the EU, strong private consumption, current account surplus, and a resilient banking sector. The establishment of a **Fiscal Council** (2024) enhances fiscal oversight.

### Austria
*   **Status**: High debt levels (81% of GDP) are considered sustainable due to strong institutional frameworks, credible governance, and a strong record of timely debt repayment.
*   **Key Factors**: Low inflation (relative to historical highs), stable exchange rate, and deep local-currency markets mitigate risks.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: Two successive years of recession (2023-2024), demographic aging, sluggish productivity growth, and rising spending pressures from defense and the green transition challenge fiscal sustainability. The IMF recommends bold fiscal consolidation, including pension reforms.

### Belgium
*   **Status**: High public debt (projected to reach 123% of GDP by 2030) creates significant sustainability risks, though the risk of sovereign stress is assessed as moderate.
*   **Key Factors**: High-quality debt management, the longest average residual maturity in the Euro Area (11.1 years), and a large domestic investor base provide stability.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: Political fragmentation, complex fiscal federalism, and rising interest costs threaten long-term sustainability.
*   **Mitigating Factors**: The new government has committed to a seven-year fiscal adjustment under the EU Economic Governance Framework to reduce the deficit below 3% and place debt on a downward path.

### France
*   **Status**: High public debt (113.1% of GDP in 2024) and a deficit of 5.8% pose significant sustainability risks, leading to credit rating downgrades (e.g., Moody's downgrade to Aa3).
*   **Key Factors**: The IMF recommends a credible package of structural fiscal measures to bring the deficit below 3% by 2029, emphasizing spending rationalization over tax increases. The **Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plan (MTFSP)** aims to place debt on a downward path.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: Political fragmentation, fiscal slippages, rising debt service costs, and demographic pressures (pensions, healthcare) threaten sustainability.
*   **Mitigating Factors**: Strong record of timely debt repayment, deep and liquid sovereign debt market, diversified investor base, and robust financial sector regulation (confirmed by 2025 FSAP).

### Romania
*   **Status**: Debt sustainability is under strain, with the fiscal deficit rising to 8.7% of GDP in 2024, driven by pension reforms and wage increases. The IMF warns of potential credit rating downgrades if consolidation fails.
*   **Key Factors**: A comprehensive fiscal reform package (VAT hikes, tax measures) was adopted in 2025 to reduce the deficit below 6% by 2026 and below 3% by 2031.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: High fiscal deficit execution risks, political uncertainty, and a growing sovereign-bank nexus. Unhedged FX loans and high consumer credit growth add financial stability risks.
*   **Mitigating Factors**: Investment-grade sovereign credit rating, well-capitalized banking system, and progress in tax administration digitalization.

### Slovakia
*   **Status**: Debt sustainability is challenged by a large fiscal deficit and rising public debt, projected to reach 74% of GDP by 2030 without further action.
*   **Key Factors**: An ambitious fiscal consolidation plan targets a deficit reduction to ~2% by 2028. The IMF emphasizes the need for further measures to address aging-related costs.
*   **Vulnerabilities**: High exposure to automotive sector shocks, demographic headwinds, and governance concerns affecting EU fund absorption.
*   **Mitigating Factors**: Strong institutional framework, including an independent Council for Budgetary Responsibility, and commitment to stabilizing the debt-to-GDP ratio.
