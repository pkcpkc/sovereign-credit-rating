# Reserve-currency flexibility

**Reserve-currency flexibility** is a critical component of the [[External Finances]] pillar in the [[Fitch Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)]]. It serves as a quantitative variable that measures a sovereign's ability to finance external obligations using its own currency, which is held in significant quantities by foreign investors and central banks. This status confers distinct advantages in terms of [[Debt Sustainability]], access to international capital markets, and resilience against external shocks.

## Definition and Methodology

In the context of the [[Fitch Ratings Sovereign Rating Criteria]] (effective September 15, 2025), reserve-currency flexibility is explicitly incorporated into the SRM to assess a country's external liquidity profile.

*   **Data Source**: Fitch derives this metric based on data from the [[International Monetary Fund]] (IMF) [[COFER]] (Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves) dataset. This data tracks the global distribution of currencies held in official foreign exchange reserves, providing a proxy for a currency's international status and acceptance.
*   **Quantitative Weight**: As part of the External Finances pillar, which holds a **17.3% weight** in the overall SRM, reserve-currency flexibility contributes to the baseline quantitative score. The External Finances pillar itself is the third most significant contributor to the model, following [[Structural Features]] (53.7%) and [[Public Finances]] (19.1%).

## Impact on Sovereign Credit Ratings

Reserve-currency flexibility influences sovereign credit ratings through both quantitative modeling and qualitative adjustments, affecting the [[Issuer Default Rating (IDR)]] for both Local-Currency (LC) and Foreign-Currency (FC) debt.

### 1. Rating Alignment and Differentiation
*   **LC vs. FC Alignment**: Sovereigns with high reserve-currency flexibility typically exhibit aligned LC and FC ratings. The ability to issue debt in a currency that is globally accepted reduces the risk of currency mismatch and enhances the credibility of sovereign guarantees.
*   **Differentiation Triggers**: In the absence of reserve-currency flexibility, differentiation between LC and FC ratings is more likely, particularly if the sovereign lacks access to foreign currency or if domestic capital markets are shallow. However, possessing reserve-currency status minimizes this wedge by ensuring that domestic investors hold significant assets in the sovereign's currency, reducing pressure for capital flight during stress events.

### 2. Short-Term Ratings and Liquidity
*   **Mapping Methodology**: For short-term ratings, Fitch maps Long-Term (LT) ratings to Short-Term (ST) ratings. If a sovereign possesses **reserve-currency flexibility**, Fitch selects the higher of two ST rating options for its FC ratings. This reflects the lower short-term rollover risk and higher liquidity associated with reserve currencies.
*   **International Liquidity Ratio (ILR)**: Reserve-currency flexibility is closely linked to the [[International Liquidity Ratio (ILR)]]. A sovereign with a robust ILR (>100%) and reserve-currency status is viewed as having superior short-term external liquidity risk management capabilities.

### 3. Qualitative Overlay (QO) Adjustments
While the SRM captures the baseline status, the [[Qualitative Overlay (QO)]] allows analysts to adjust ratings by ±2 notches per pillar (capped at ±3 overall) based on unmodeled factors.
*   **External Financing Flexibility**: Analysts assess whether the sovereign's reserve-currency status translates into resilient market access. A sovereign with deep international liquidity and reserve-currency status is less vulnerable to sudden stops in capital flows.
*   **Shock Vulnerability**: The QO evaluates vulnerability to external shocks. Reserve-currency countries are generally more insulated from balance-of-payments crises compared to non-reserve currency nations, as their currency often appreciates during global risk-off events (flight to quality), providing a natural buffer.

## Strategic Significance for Macro-Financial Stability

The presence of reserve-currency flexibility provides a sovereign with unique macroeconomic policy space and financial stability advantages:

*   **Fiscal Financing Flexibility**: Governments can issue large volumes of debt in their domestic currency to foreign investors without facing prohibitive risk premiums. This expands the [[Public Finances]] capacity to absorb shocks.
*   **Exchange Rate Stability**: Reduced reliance on foreign currency borrowing mitigates the risk of sovereign default due to currency depreciation (the "original sin" problem).
*   **Global Systemic Importance**: High reserve-currency status is correlated with larger shares in world GDP and deeper financial markets, reinforcing the [[Structural Features]] pillar of the rating model.

*   [[Qualitative Overlay (QO)]]: The mechanism for adjusting the quantitative SRM output based on qualitative assessments of external financing flexibility.
