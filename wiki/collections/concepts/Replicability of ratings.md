# Replicability of ratings

**Replicability of ratings** refers to the extent to which an independent analyst or observer can reproduce a sovereign credit rating using the published methodology and available public data. In the context of [[S&P Sovereign Credit Rating Methodology]], high replicability implies that the rating is a deterministic output of quantifiable economic and fiscal metrics. Low replicability indicates that the rating process relies heavily on subjective judgment, qualitative assessments, and discretionary adjustments that are not transparently quantifiable.

## Definition and Significance

The concept of replicability is central to the critique of modern credit rating agencies. A fully replicable rating system would allow external validators to audit the logic and accuracy of ratings. However, as highlighted in the analysis of the S&P methodology by Alex Stomper (HU Berlin), the rating process is described as **"partially non-replicable"** due to the significant room for discretionary judgment.

The significance of this lack of replicability lies in its impact on:
*   **Transparency and Accountability**: If ratings cannot be independently verified, it becomes difficult to hold rating agencies accountable for errors or biases.
*   **Market Confidence**: Investors rely on ratings as objective benchmarks. Subjectivity introduces potential for inconsistency, which can undermine trust in the rating's predictive power regarding [[Country Risk Analysis]] and [[Financial Stability]].
*   **Policy Implications**: Governments may find it challenging to understand precisely which policy changes will improve their rating if the linkage between specific actions and the final rating score is obscured by discretionary adjustments.

## Sources of Non-Replicability in S&P Methodology

According to the 2017 S&P Global Ratings framework, as presented in 2024 by Alex Stomper, several structural elements within the five-pillar assessment framework contribute to the irreproducibility of final ratings:

### 1. Qualitative Institutional Assessment
The [[Institutional Assessment]] pillar evaluates factors such as:
*   **Government Effectiveness and Predictability**: These are subjective measures of policymaking stability.
*   **Debt Payment Culture**: A weak "debt payment culture" can cap the institutional assessment at a specific level ('6'), but defining the threshold for "weak" is inherently qualitative.
*   **Cohesive Civil Society**: Assessing the strength of civil society is highly interpretive.
*   **External Security Risks**: The assessment of "war risk" or security threats involves significant analyst discretion in determining the severity and likelihood of such events.

### 2. Discretionary "Supplemental Adjustment Factors"
Even after deriving an "indicative rating" from the five pillars (Institutional, Economic, External, Fiscal, Monetary), S&P analysts apply **supplemental adjustment factors** at their discretion. These include:
*   **Event Risk**: Subjective evaluation of potential political or economic shocks.
*   **Extreme Liquidity Weakness**: While [[External Liquidity]] is measured, the determination of what constitutes "extreme" weakness and how it should penalize the rating is discretionary.
*   **Fiscal Debt Levels**: Similarly, the impact of "extremely high" fiscal debt is adjusted based on analyst judgment rather than a fixed formula.

### 3. The Final Rating Override
The methodology explicitly allows analysts to override the indicative rating if they deem it necessary. This final step separates the mechanical output of the framework from the final published rating, introducing the largest source of variance and non-replicability.

## Citizen Science Initiative

To address the opacity of these discretionary elements, a **"Citizen Science"** approach has been proposed. This initiative aims to:
1.  Apply the S&P methodology rigorously to a set of European countries using only public data.
2.  Compare the independently derived "indicative ratings" with the actual published S&P ratings.
3.  Document and analyze the discrepancies.

The goal is not merely an academic exercise but a systematic effort to **expose the subjective nature** of sovereign credit rating determinations. By tracking where and how discretion is applied, this approach seeks to quantify the "replicability gap" and provide empirical evidence on the extent to which ratings are driven by objective fundamentals versus subjective analyst judgment.
