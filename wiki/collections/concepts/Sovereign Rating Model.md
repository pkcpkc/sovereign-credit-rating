# Sovereign Rating Model

The **Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)** is a quantitative multiple-regression framework utilized by [[Fitch Ratings]] to determine the baseline Issuer Default Rating (IDR) for sovereign entities and certain central banks. Introduced as a core component of Fitch's rating methodology (effective September 2025), the SRM serves as the quantitative starting point for credit assessment, which is subsequently adjusted via a **Qualitative Overlay (QO)** to account for factors not fully captured by the statistical model.

The SRM is designed to provide a transparent, data-driven assessment of creditworthiness, focusing on the government's forward-looking capacity and willingness to honor debt obligations to private creditors. It operates on a scale from 'AAA' to 'CCC+' and integrates 18 key variables organized into four primary analytical pillars: **Structural Features**, **Macroeconomic Performance**, **Public Finances**, and **External Finances**.

## Methodology and Framework

The SRM's output is not the final rating; it is a calibrated baseline. The model's structure reflects the relative importance of different economic and political drivers in predicting default risk, with weights assigned to each pillar based on historical default data and regression analysis.

### 1. Structural Features (Weight: 53.7%)
This pillar carries the highest weight in the SRM, reflecting the foundational role of political and institutional stability in sovereign credit risk. It includes:
*   **Governance Indicators:** Derived from the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI), covering voice and accountability, political stability, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption.
*   **Economic Size and Diversification:** Measured by the country's share of world GDP and GDP per capita.
*   **History of Default:** The number of years since the last sovereign default or restructuring event.
*   **Monetary Sovereignty:** Represented by the broad money supply, indicating the capacity for domestic financing.

### 2. Macroeconomic Performance (Weight: 9.8%)
This pillar assesses the stability and growth trajectory of the economy, using:
*   **Growth Volatility:** Measured by the standard deviation of real GDP growth.
*   **Inflation Stability:** Measured by consumer price inflation trends.
*   **Growth Level:** Real GDP growth rates.

### 3. Public Finances (Weight: 19.1%)
This pillar evaluates fiscal sustainability and the burden of government debt, including:
*   **Debt Burden:** Gross general government debt as a percentage of GDP.
*   **Interest Service:** Interest payments relative to revenue.
*   **Fiscal Balance:** The government's primary fiscal balance.
*   **Currency Composition of Debt:** The share of debt denominated in foreign currency, which impacts vulnerability to exchange rate shocks.

### 4. External Finances (Weight: 17.3%)
This pillar measures the economy's external position and resilience to balance-of-payments crises, including:
*   **Reserve-Currency Flexibility:** The extent to which the currency is used internationally.
*   **Net Foreign Assets:** The country's net international investment position.
*   **Commodity Dependence:** The share of exports or revenues derived from commodities.
*   **Liquidity Metrics:** Foreign exchange reserves and the International Liquidity Ratio (ILR).
*   **Current Account Balance:** Including Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) inflows.

## The Qualitative Overlay (QO)

The SRM's quantitative output is adjusted by a **Qualitative Overlay (QO)** to incorporate factors that are difficult to quantify or where data is limited. The QO allows analysts to adjust the rating by up to **+3/-3 notches** based on specific qualitative assessments:

*   **Political Stability:** Risks arising from political unrest or institutional weakness not fully captured by governance indices.
*   **Financial Sector Health:** The stability of the domestic banking system and its linkage to the sovereign.
*   **Fiscal Flexibility:** The government's ability to adjust taxes and spending, including demographic pressures.
*   **External Financing Resilience:** The ability to access foreign capital markets and the depth of domestic financial markets.

## Relation to Other Models and Tools

The SRM is part of a broader suite of analytical tools used by Fitch to assess sovereign risk:

*   **Sovereign Climate Risk Model (SCRM):** Used to assess long-term physical and transition risks related to climate change. The output, known as the **Climate Vulnerability Signal (Climate.VS)**, acts as a screening tool. Sovereigns with a Climate.VS score ≥ 50 by 2035 receive enhanced scrutiny, which may result in negative QO adjustments.
*   **Macro-Prudential Indicator Model (MPI):** Complements the SRM by focusing on financial stability indicators, particularly relevant for assessing banking sector stress and its impact on sovereign credit.

## Impact on Credit Ratings

The SRM directly influences the assignment of Long-Term (LT) and Short-Term (ST) IDRs:
*   **LT IDR:** The SRM provides the baseline for the LT FC (Foreign Currency) IDR. This is then adjusted by the QO to arrive at the final LT FC IDR.
*   **LC vs. FC IDRs:** The model helps differentiate between Local-Currency (LC) and Foreign-Currency (FC) ratings. LC IDRs reflect the risk of default in local currency, heavily influenced by monetary policy and domestic market depth. FC IDRs reflect the risk of default in foreign currency, influenced by reserve levels and access to international capital.
*   **Default Events:** The SRM informs the assessment of events such as Missed Payments, Distressed Debt Exchanges (DDEs), and forced redenominations, which trigger a downgrade to 'RD' (Restructuring Default).

## Significance in Country Risk Analysis

The Sovereign Rating Model represents a significant evolution in sovereign credit analysis by:
1.  **Enhancing Transparency:** By explicitly defining the variables and weights, it reduces the opacity of rating methodologies.
2.  **Integrating Climate Risk:** Through the Climate.VS, it systematically incorporates long-term environmental risks into credit assessments.
3.  **Standardizing Assessment:** It provides a consistent framework for comparing sovereigns across different regions and economic structures, aiding investors in managing country risk exposure.
