# competitiveness

**Competitiveness** refers to the ability of an economy to maintain or increase its share of global markets while sustaining high standards of living. In the context of sovereign credit analysis and macroeconomic surveillance, it is determined by unit labour costs, productivity growth, institutional quality, the real effective exchange rate, and the alignment of market mechanisms with state intervention. Weak competitiveness erodes export potential, widens current account deficits, and necessitates internal devaluation, which is socially and politically costly. It serves as a primary driver of [[fiscal sustainability]] by influencing GDP growth trajectories, tax base stability, and the long-term viability of public debt.

Structural headwinds to competitiveness vary by country but commonly involve labour market rigidities, productivity stagnation, regulatory burdens, distortions caused by excessive state intervention, and external vulnerabilities related to exchange rates and energy dependency.

## Key Dimensions and Structural Challenges

### 1. Labour Cost Pressures, Wages, and Taxation
*   **Wage Indexation (Belgium Context):** Belgium suffers from automatic wage indexation, where wages are adjusted for past inflation. This mechanism has historically led to **competitiveness losses** during periods of high inflation, as wages rise faster than productivity. This results in a high real effective exchange rate, making Belgian exports more expensive relative to competitors.
*   **Tax Burden on Production (France Context):** France faces high taxes on production that hinder competitiveness. These fiscal pressures contribute to declining business confidence and reduced investment incentives.
*   **Tax Complexity and Framework (Poland Context):** Poland ranks last in the EU for tax framework complexity. This high administrative burden acts as a significant drag on innovation and business activity, deterring investment and increasing compliance costs for firms.
*   **Unit Labour Costs (Poland Context):** Poland faces pressures from high unit labour costs, which threaten cost competitiveness, particularly in manufacturing and export-oriented sectors. These costs are exacerbated by a tight labour market and demographic constraints.
*   **Tax Wedge on Labour (Belgium Context):** Belgium has one of the highest tax burdens on labour in the EU. This high [[tax-wedge on labour]] acts as a significant drag on formal economic activity, reducing the incentive to work and increasing the unit cost of production for firms.
*   **Labour Market Activation:** Reforms aim to boost [[labour market participation]] by limiting unemployment benefits (e.g., Belgium’s two-year limit for those under 55) and linking benefits to income. This seeks to reduce [[institutional fragmentation]] in welfare delivery and improve work incentives.
*   **Nominal Wage Growth vs. Productivity (Hungary Context):** Hungary exhibits strong nominal wage growth, which poses a competitiveness risk when decoupled from productivity gains. With labour productivity stagnant for 15 years, rising nominal wages increase unit labour costs, threatening international competitiveness unless offset by productivity improvements or exchange rate adjustments.

### 2. Weak Productivity Growth and Innovation
*   **Productivity Lag (Belgium):** Labour [[productivity growth]] has been weak over the past decade and lags behind the EU average since the 2000s. This stagnation is attributed to low business dynamism, regulatory burdens, and limited diffusion of innovation from R&D to the broader economy.
*   **Productivity Gap (Poland Context):** Poland’s labour productivity remains low at 67% of the EU average. This structural deficit limits the country's ability to compete in high-value-added markets and constrains long-term income growth.
*   **Recovery and Stagnation (France):** While French labour [[productivity growth]] is recovering from recent losses, it remains hampered by skills shortages, low business R&D intensity, and high administrative burdens.
*   **Long-term Stagnation (Hungary):** Hungary faces severe structural challenges with labour productivity among the lowest in the EU and having shown no improvement in 15 years. This stagnation is driven by an increase in low-skilled employment rather than innovation. Low R&D intensity (1.38% of GDP vs. EU average of 2.24%) and a mismatch between education outcomes and labour market needs further constrain productivity.
*   **Innovation vs. Implementation:** Despite high R&D intensity in some economies (e.g., Belgium at 3.3% of GDP) and world-class research institutions, the translation of this investment into productivity gains is often inefficient. In France, the efficiency of support schemes like the research tax credit (CIR) needs improvement to boost innovation output and private sector engagement. In Hungary, dependence on Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) for innovation, rather than domestic R&D, creates vulnerabilities to global capital shifts.
*   **Science-Business Collaboration (Poland Context):** A weak link between science and business hinders the commercialization of research and technological advancement in Poland. Strengthening this collaboration is critical for boosting productivity and industrial competitiveness.
*   **Skills Shortages:** Both Belgium and France face challenges related to skills mismatches. In France, educational inequalities and declining attractiveness of the teaching profession impact long-term human capital development. In Hungary, teacher shortages and low tertiary attainment (32.3% for ages 25-34) hinder the transition to high-value-added activities. In Poland, severe shortages of skilled workers in manufacturing, construction, and STEM fields, combined with a declining basic skills level among youth, create a bottleneck for growth. The vocational education and training (VET) system lacks effectiveness in meeting labor market needs, further exacerbating the skills crisis.

### 3. Business Dynamism, Regulatory Barriers, and State Intervention
*   **Zombie Firms and Resource Misallocation (Belgium):** The presence of "zombie firms" (unproductive firms kept alive by soft budget constraints or credit access) traps capital and labour, preventing more dynamic firms from expanding. This lack of [[business dynamism]] reduces overall economic efficiency and competitive resilience.
*   **Regulatory Volatility and Complexity:**
    *   **France:** High administrative burdens and regulatory restrictions persist, particularly in the services and retail sectors. Complex administrative procedures impact businesses, hindering market entry and competition.
    *   **Hungary:** Frequent use of emergency decrees to cap prices (e.g., food, energy) introduces regulatory volatility and uncertainty, deterring long-term investment.
    *   **Poland:** A complex and fast-changing regulatory framework, characterized by frequent legislative changes with short preparation times (average 31 days), deters investment and undermines policy predictability. Perceived erosion of judicial independence and weak ex post evaluation of legislation further weaken the institutional foundation for competitiveness.
*   **State Intervention and Market Distortions (Hungary Context):** Hungary’s competitiveness is significantly hampered by heavy state intervention. This includes subsidized lending distorting credit allocation, administrative price controls, and sector-specific taxes reducing profitability. Weak anti-corruption frameworks increase the cost of doing business.
*   **Business Environment and Investment Climate:**
    *   **Poland:** While Poland has seen strong investment growth funded by EU resources, the regulatory environment and perceived rule of law issues pose risks to private sector confidence. However, the establishment of an independent Fiscal Council and improved regulatory governance offer potential strengths for long-term stability.
    *   **France:** Record-high corporate bankruptcies reflect the strain of high interest rates, economic uncertainty, and a challenging business environment.
*   **Digitalization and Technology Adoption:** France lags in the digitalization of SMEs and the adoption of advanced technologies like AI. Limited diffusion of innovation from R&D to the broader economy undermines long-term competitiveness gains.

### 4. Energy Transition and Green Competitiveness
*   **Energy Security and Costs:** Belgium’s high import dependency for fossil fuels and critical raw materials exacerbates vulnerability to external shocks (e.g., energy price spikes).
*   **High Electricity Prices (Poland Context):** Poland faces high electricity prices driven by taxation and generation costs, which pose a direct threat to industrial competitiveness. While Poland has emerged as a clean tech hub (producing 60% of Europe’s lithium batteries), the energy mix remains heavily dominated by fossil fuels (70% of electricity generation). This creates a vulnerability to carbon pricing mechanisms and supply shocks.
*   **Renewable Deployment Barriers:**
    *   **France:** Deployment is hindered by complex permitting procedures and a lack of firm national renewable energy targets for 2030.
    *   **Poland:** The transition requires faster deployment of renewables, grid modernization, and the phase-out of fossil fuel subsidies to align with EU climate goals and reduce industrial energy costs.
*   **Geopolitical Energy Dependency (Hungary Context):** Hungary remains heavily dependent on Russian fossil fuels, creating significant supply security risks. High fossil fuel subsidies distort market signals and hinder the transition to net-zero technologies.
*   **Decarbonization and Investment:** Building decarbonization in France lags due to budget constraints and administrative barriers. In Poland, high electricity prices and fossil fuel reliance threaten industrial competitiveness, necessitating accelerated grid connectivity and renewable investment. Phasing out environmentally harmful fossil fuel subsidies (Belgium, Hungary) and improving energy security through diversification are critical for long-term competitiveness in a carbon-constrained global economy.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit Ratings and Fiscal Sustainability

Weak competitiveness has direct and indirect implications for sovereign risk profiles, particularly through its effect on growth, fiscal space, and external vulnerabilities.

*   **Fiscal Sustainability:** Declining competitiveness reduces export revenues and GDP growth, which in turn lowers the tax base relative to GDP.
    *   **Belgium:** Exacerbates [[fiscal sustainability]] challenges in the face of rising [[ageing costs]] and [[debt sustainability]] pressures.
    *   **France:** Persistently high deficits (5.8% of GDP in 2024) and rising public debt are driven partly by sluggish tax revenues linked to economic slowdown.
    *   **Hungary:** Low productivity growth complicates debt sustainability, with a narrow tax base limiting fiscal flexibility.
    *   **Poland:** The high budget deficit (6.6% of GDP in 2024) and rising public debt (projected 58.0% of GDP in 2025) are compounded by rising defense spending (2.8% of GDP). Competitiveness losses from high energy costs and regulatory complexity hinder the generation of surplus revenues needed for fiscal consolidation under the Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP). The low tax-to-GDP ratio further limits fiscal flexibility.
*   **External Imbalances and Vulnerability:** Persistent competitiveness losses can lead to external imbalances, increasing the country's vulnerability to external shocks.
    *   **France:** Faces a negative net international investment position and high reliance on foreign capital.
    *   **Hungary:** Negative NIIP (-31.8% of GDP) and high energy import bills impact the trade balance. Dependence on FDI creates vulnerabilities to global capital shifts.
    *   **Poland:**
        *   **Exchange Rate Risks:** Appreciation of the złoty poses risks to cost competitiveness, potentially eroding export margins.
        *   **External Position:** Despite a current account surplus, Poland maintains a Net International Investment Position (NIIP) deficit of -30.8% of GDP. Reliance on FDI for investment and manufacturing makes the economy sensitive to global investor sentiment and geopolitical tensions (e.g., proximity to the war in Ukraine).
        *   **Regional Disparities:** Significant differences in competitiveness between western/capital regions and eastern rural areas create internal imbalances, requiring costly regional development interventions.
*   **Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength:** The ability to restore competitiveness through structural reforms is a key factor in [[policy_credibility_and_institutional_strength]].
    *   **France:** Political instability has reduced fiscal ambitions and complicated structural reform implementation.
    *   **Hungary:** Regulatory volatility and weak rule of law undermine policy predictability, increasing investor risk premiums.
    *   **Poland:** Frequent legislative changes and concerns over judicial independence weaken institutional quality. However, the implementation of the medium-term fiscal-structural plan and the establishment of an independent Fiscal Council represent steps toward enhancing policy credibility. The perceived erosion of the rule of law remains a threat to long-term investment confidence.
*   **Investment Climate:** High administrative burdens and regulatory complexity deter domestic and foreign investment. In Poland, the complex tax framework and regulatory volatility hamper innovation. In Hungary, distorted credit allocation crowds out private investment. Improving the business environment is essential for growing the economy out of debt and enhancing debt affordability.

## Strategic Reforms and Policy Responses

To address competitiveness deficits and enhance fiscal resilience, the following measures are highlighted in Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plans (MTP) and EU recommendations:

*   **Labour Market Activation and Skills Development:**
    *   **General:** Reforms aim to boost participation by improving work incentives and addressing skills mismatches.
    *   **Poland:** Critical need to improve the effectiveness of Vocational Education and Training (VET), increase adult learning participation, and enhance basic skills education to address the demographic decline and skills shortages in STEM and manufacturing.
    *   **Hungary:** Increasing domestic R&D intensity and addressing the skills gap through education reform.
*   **Tax and Regulatory Reform:**
    *   **Belgium:** Shifting the tax burden from labour to capital gains and property taxes.
    *   **France:** Reducing high taxes on production and streamlining tax administration.
    *   **Poland:** Simplifying the complex tax framework and regulatory environment to improve business confidence and foster innovation. Enhancing the transparency and predictability of legislative processes is crucial to attracting long-term investment.
*   **Productivity Enhancing Measures:**
    *   **Innovation Diffusion:** Improving the efficiency of R&D incentives and reducing regulatory barriers.
    *   **Poland:** Strengthening science-business collaboration to better translate research into commercial applications and productivity gains.
    *   **Digitalization:** Accelerating SME digitalization and AI adoption.
*   **Green and Energy Transition:**
    *   **France:** Simplifying permitting for renewables and setting firm national targets.
    *   **Belgium:** Phasing out fossil fuel subsidies and improving energy security.
    *   **Hungary:** Diversifying energy sources and aligning state support with market mechanisms.
    *   **Poland:** Accelerating the phase-out of fossil fuel subsidies, improving grid connectivity and flexibility, and expanding renewable energy deployment to reduce industrial energy costs and meet EU climate targets. Leveraging the emerging clean tech manufacturing sector (e.g., batteries) to create a competitive advantage in green industries.
*   **Institutional and Governance Improvements:**
    *   **Hungary:** Enhancing competition enforcement, reducing discretionary state intervention, and strengthening the rule of law.
    *   **Poland:** Strengthening judicial independence and regulatory governance to restore investor confidence. Utilizing EU funds (Recovery and Resilience Facility, Cohesion Policy) effectively to drive infrastructure and structural reforms, while ensuring robust oversight and impact assessment of legislation.
