# Anti-Corruption Framework

An **anti-corruption framework** constitutes the legal, institutional, and procedural architecture designed to prevent, detect, and sanction corrupt practices within a state. In the context of sovereign credit analysis and country risk assessment, the robustness of this framework is a critical determinant of **institutional quality**, **policy credibility**, and **economic efficiency**. Effective anti-corruption mechanisms ensure the fair allocation of public resources, protect investment incentives, and maintain investor confidence in the rule of law. Weaknesses in this framework—such as insufficient oversight of political financing, opaque lobbying practices, inadequate whistleblower protections, politicized judicial enforcement, broad immunities for top executives, or judicial interpretations that create impunity (e.g., statute of limitations loopholes)—introduce systemic risks that can elevate borrowing costs, deter foreign direct investment (FDI), and destabilize fiscal sustainability.

The efficacy of these frameworks varies significantly across jurisdictions, ranging from mature democracies with strong but imperfect oversight (e.g., France, Belgium) to states with transitioning frameworks addressing historical rule of law deficits (e.g., Poland, Romania) and states with deteriorating institutional checks and high corruption perceptions (e.g., Hungary, Slovakia). Comparative analysis of these systems highlights how specific vulnerabilities in transparency, judicial independence, and civic space directly impact sovereign credit ratings. Recent developments in Slovakia (2024–2025), Malta (2024–2025), Poland (2025), and Romania (2025) further illustrate the complexities of transitioning from post-scam reforms, resolving long-standing constitutional crises, or managing judicial case-law shifts that affect the finality of corruption convictions. These cases underscore the intersection of citizenship-by-investment schemes, judicial backlog, the operational capacity of anti-corruption bodies, the separation of prosecutorial powers from political executive control, and the critical role of supranational bodies like the **European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO)** and the **Venice Commission**. In Slovakia specifically, the dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities and the broad powers of the Prosecutor General have led to a measurable decline in high-level corruption investigations, signaling a retreat from institutional checks and balances.

## Core Components and Institutional Architecture

The efficacy of an anti-corruption framework relies on a multi-layered ecosystem of specialized agencies, legislative bodies, judicial institutions, and civil society oversight. Recent developments in France, Belgium, Hungary, Malta, Poland, Romania, and Slovakia (2024–2025) illustrate divergent trajectories in strengthening or weakening these pillars.

### 1. Specialized Investigative and Prosecution Bodies
*   **France**: The framework relies on the **National Financial Prosecutor (PNF)** for complex financial crimes and the **Anti-Corruption Agency (AFA)**, which was reorganized in 2025 into distinct public and private sector departments to enhance specialization. The **Central Office for Combating Corruption and Tax Offences (OCLCIFF)** also plays a key role.
*   **Belgium**: The primary federal body is the **Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC)**. Recent reforms have focused on establishing the **Financial Investigation Service (FIOD-SRFF)** and a specialized prosecution service for financial crime to improve technical capacity in tracing illicit flows.
*   **Hungary**: Despite the existence of a **National Anti-Corruption Strategy** and the **Integrity Authority**, there is no robust track record of prosecuting high-level corruption. The Integrity Authority continues to face significant obstacles in accessing data and fulfilling oversight duties.
*   **Malta**: Significant progress has been made in centralizing prosecutorial authority. The transfer of prosecutorial powers for serious offenses to the **Office of the Attorney General** was completed in September 2024, enhancing prosecutorial independence. Investigative bodies, including the Financial Crime Investigation Department (FCID), have increased resources. However, the **Permanent Commission Against Corruption (PCAC)** remains a vulnerability; it operates with limited part-time staff, lacks operational readiness, and has never referred a case for prosecution, failing to demonstrate concrete results.
*   **Poland**: A major structural shift is underway. The **European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO)** became fully operational in Poland on January 1, 2025, providing a critical supranational enforcement mechanism. Domestically, the long-standing **Central Anti-Corruption Bureau (CBA)** is scheduled to be dissolved and replaced by a new **Central Office for Combating Corruption** within the police force. While investigations into large-scale corruption cases involving politicians are ongoing, the transition period and the lack of a comprehensive national anti-corruption strategy present continuity risks. The separation of the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General role, finalized in early 2025, aims to enhance the functional independence of the prosecution service.
*   **Romania**: The **National Anti-Corruption Directorate (DNA)** maintains a stable occupancy rate and continues to secure convictions in high-level cases, despite vacancies. The implementation of the **2021-2025 National Anti-Corruption Strategy** is on track, with a new strategy under preparation. However, the effectiveness of the DNA is challenged by judicial interpretations that may lead to case dismissals. Legislative steps are being explored to extend the mandates of high-ranking prosecutors from three to five years to ensure continuity. The EPPO is also operational in Romania, serving as an external check.
*   **Slovakia**: The anti-corruption institutional landscape has undergone a significant contraction. The dissolution of specialized entities, namely the **National Crime Agency (NAKA)** and the **Specialized Prosecutor's Office (SPO)**, has resulted in a marked drop in the number of corruption cases investigated and increased operational inefficiencies. The **Bureau for Combating Crime (UBOK)** has assumed broader responsibilities, but lacks the specialized focus and independence previously held by NAKA. The **Prosecutor General’s Office** retains broad powers to annul final decisions and interfere in high-profile cases, raising concerns about political influence and the finality of justice. A new **National Anti-Corruption Strategy 2024-2029** is in preparation, but its effectiveness is questioned given the dismantling of key enforcement pillars.
*   **European Integration**: In France, Belgium, Malta, Poland, Romania, and Slovakia, the **EPPO** serves as a critical external check, although its domestic coordination varies. In Hungary, tensions with EU oversight bodies, including the **Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)** and **GRECO**, highlight systemic gaps. In Malta, the CJEU’s April 2025 ruling that the investor citizenship scheme violates EU Law underscores the role of supranational courts. In Slovakia, the alignment of criminal codes with the EU **Protection of the European Union’s Financial Interests (PIF) Directive** (amended in Feb 2024 and Dec 2024) represents a legislative strength, yet enforcement remains fragmented. In Romania, the **High Court of Cassation and Justice (HCCJ)** has referred questions to the CJEU (Case C-280/25) regarding the compatibility of domestic case-law on the statute of limitations with EU law, highlighting the tension between national judicial autonomy and supranational legal standards.

### 2. Integrity Policies and Oversight Mechanisms
*   **Asset and Interest Declarations**:
    *   *France*: The **High Authority for Transparency in Public Life (HATVP)** has strengthened its capacity, increasing staff to handle a 48% rise in asset declarations. However, a significant structural gap remains: lobbying disclosure rules do not apply to top-executive level officials, creating a transparency loophole.
    *   *Belgium*: Systems relying on sealed declarations accessible only during criminal investigations are deemed insufficient. High compliance rates without independent auditing create reputational risks, as noted in the 2025 Rule of Law Report.
    *   *Hungary*: The asset declaration system is weak, lacking effective oversight and systematic follow-up for non-compliance. This absence of verification undermines the deterrent effect of declarations.
    *   *Malta*: Integrity measures for high-level politicians remain inadequate. While ministers filed annual asset and interest declarations in March 2025, these documents remain unpublished, contrary to OECD recommendations. MPs are not covered by public administration conflict of interest rules, and there is limited coverage of conflict of interest regulations for high-ranking officials.
    *   *Poland*: Transparency in political party financing remains limited, and broad immunities for top executives hinder criminal accountability, creating significant gaps in high-level oversight.
    *   *Romania*: The credibility of the asset declaration system was significantly weakened in May 2025 when the **Constitutional Court of Romania** ruled that requirements for spouses’ and children’s asset declarations were unconstitutional. This ruling creates a major transparency gap, potentially allowing high-ranking officials to conceal illicit wealth through family members, thereby undermining the **National Integrity Agency (ANI)**’s ability to manage conflicts of interest effectively.
    *   *Slovakia*: Transparency in asset declarations is under threat. Restrictions on public access to asset disclosures are being considered, which would reduce the visibility of officials' wealth. No progress has been made in strengthening the verification mechanisms for these declarations, leaving a key preventive tool weakened.
*   **Lobbying Transparency and "Revolving Doors"**:
    *   *France*: While new rules for foreign lobbyists are expected in 2025, the exclusion of senior executives from disclosure requirements is a persistent vulnerability.
    *   *Belgium*: The absence of binding legislation on lobbying registers and mandatory cooling-off periods, replaced by non-binding circulars, creates loopholes for **undue political influence** and regulatory capture.
    *   *Hungary*: Legislation on lobbying and revolving doors, scheduled for November 2025, has not yet materialized. This delay, combined with a lack of transparency in political party financing and electoral campaigns, exacerbates risks of conflict of interest.
    *   *Malta*: Transparency in the implementation of the National Anti-Fraud and Corruption Strategy is lacking. The "commercialization" of citizenship, previously enabled by the investor citizenship scheme, revealed deep structural vulnerabilities in how public authority was leveraged for private gain, a risk area that remains sensitive despite the scheme's invalidation.
    *   *Poland*: No progress has been made in introducing comprehensive lobbying rules, which is cited as a key weakness in the anti-corruption strategy. The absence of binding regulations increases the risk of regulatory capture and opaque influence peddling.
    *   *Romania*: No progress has been made on introducing lobbying rules for **Members of Parliament (MPs)**. This legislative gap, combined with pending legislation to improve transparency in political party financing, leaves high-level political decision-making susceptible to opaque influence.
    *   *Slovakia*: There has been no progress in regulating lobbying activities. The lack of a mandatory lobbying register and clear rules on "revolving doors" prevents the detection of undue influence in legislative and administrative processes, sustaining a high risk of regulatory capture.
*   **Whistleblower Protection**: Essential for internal detection. France sees increased usage of whistleblowing mechanisms, though resource constraints for protection persist. Belgium enacted new laws in March 2025 to strengthen protections within parliamentary and administrative structures. In Hungary, the deterioration of **civil society space** and pressure on NGOs reduces the effectiveness of societal oversight and whistleblowing channels. In Malta, the UN Human Rights Committee requested improvements to whistleblower protection in August 2024, and a law on Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs) was enacted in July 2024, though critics note it does not cover domestic lawsuits. In Poland, the whistleblower protection law, enforced largely since September 2024, is fully active in the 2025 context, providing a stronger operational baseline for internal reporting compared to its neighbors, though operational challenges persist. In Romania, weak protection for whistleblowers in public bodies remains a threat, limiting internal detection capabilities. In Slovakia, the **Office for the Protection of Whistleblowers** demonstrated operational effectiveness by fining the Ministry of Interior for procedural violations in October 2024, indicating that the mechanism can function, though its broader impact is constrained by the hostile environment for journalists and NGOs.

### 3. Judicial Independence and Efficiency
*   **Judicial Independence**:
    *   *France*: Despite the 2023 reform of the status of magistrates, perceived judicial independence remains average. Magistrates face severe personal attacks and political pressure, particularly regarding election-related cases. The **Superior Council of the Magistracy (CSM)** actively defends judicial autonomy.
    *   *Belgium*: The judiciary must remain free from executive pressure. Allegations of political interference and information leaks in sensitive investigations (e.g., CDBC-OCRC cases) threaten independence.
    *   *Hungary*: Judicial independence is severely compromised. The **National Judicial Council** faced significant challenges, including the resignation of its President in December 2024 following protests against judicial reforms. The Council withdrew from a four-party agreement on judicial reforms in January 2025. The **Kúria** (Supreme Court) continues to enforce uniform case law, but undue pressure on judges regarding internal debates and appointments persists.
    *   *Malta*: Perceived judicial independence remains high among the general public (69%) and companies (63%). A major strength is the ongoing constitutional reform (tabled May 2025) to involve the judiciary in the appointment of the Chief Justice, reducing executive influence. However, concerns persist regarding the independence of the Immigration Appeals Board, as highlighted by an ECtHR judgment in October 2024.
    *   *Poland*: The judicial landscape is undergoing significant stabilization efforts following years of crisis. The government has prepared legislation to address the status of ~1,600 judges appointed by the post-2017 **National Council for the Judiciary (NCJ)**, involving a classification system and potential removal, pending Venice Commission consultation. The structure of the **Supreme Court** is being amended, including the dismantling of the Chamber of Extraordinary Control and Public Affairs. However, a constitutional crisis persists regarding the **Constitutional Tribunal**, with the government continuing to refuse publication of judgments, asserting the body lacks legitimacy. This creates a dual-track legal environment that undermines predictability for investors.
    *   *Romania*: Significant progress was made in completing the process to implement **Venice Commission** recommendations on the Justice Laws, addressing concerns over the appointment of judges and prosecutors. The Ministry of Justice has shown openness to ensuring judicial independence in the reform of the IT system for courts. However, perceived judicial independence has decreased among the general public and companies. Transparency in judicial promotions and disciplinary sanctions continues to face scrutiny.
    *   *Slovakia*: Perceived judicial independence is critically low, with only 32% of the public and 34% of companies expressing confidence in the judiciary. The **Judicial Council** faces governance issues, notably the lack of sufficient safeguards for the dismissal of its members, particularly those not elected by judges. Amendments to judges' laws introduced bonuses and evaluation changes that raise concerns about potential political influence. The expanded role of the Judicial Council in disciplinary proceedings poses further risks to independence. The Prosecutor General’s ability to annul final decisions in high-profile cases directly undermines judicial finality and independence.
*   **Efficiency and Digitalization**:
    *   *France*: Digitalization efforts (e.g., the Portalis tool) face implementation delays. Case resolution times remain a concern (1,335 days for bribery cases), prompting "flash missions" to improve efficiency.
    *   *Belgium*: Digitalization of justice processes represents a long-term strength for transparency, though current shortcomings and fraud risks in these projects pose immediate fiscal threats.
    *   *Hungary*: The judiciary demonstrates high overall efficiency in resolving cases and ongoing digitalization. However, this efficiency is undermined by a lack of transparency in **case allocation** in lower-instance courts, where discretion allowed to court managers prevents verification of impartiality.
    *   *Malta*: Judicial efficiency is a critical vulnerability. Despite opening new Commercial and Family Courts, the length of proceedings is excessive. In 2023, disposition time for first-instance civil/commercial cases was 454 days, and criminal cases took 713 days. Administrative justice saw a deterioration to 1,350 days. Backlogs continue to increase, with clearance rates falling below new case intake, adversely affecting business confidence and legal certainty.
    *   *Poland*: Digitalization of court procedures is a stated strength, aiming to improve access and speed. However, the backlog of pending leading judgments from the **European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR)** increased to 54 as of June 2025, signaling unresolved systemic legal issues that can delay enforcement and create uncertainty for foreign investors.
    *   *Romania*: Digitalization of the public procurement process is ongoing, aiming to improve transparency and reduce opportunities for corruption in tendering. However, judicial efficiency is challenged by the volume of cases resulting from shifts in statute of limitations law.
    *   *Slovakia*: The justice system faces severe efficiency challenges, particularly in administrative cases where resolution time rose sharply to 1,040 days in 2023. Logistical and specialization issues persist despite ongoing reforms to the judicial map. Furthermore, Slovakia had 31 leading judgments from the **European Court of Human Rights** pending implementation as of January 2025, rising to 33 by June 2025, indicating a weak enforcement of international judicial decisions that creates legal uncertainty for investors.

### 4. Civil Society and Media Pluralism as Oversight Mechanisms
*   **Civil Society Space**: A vibrant civic space is crucial for external detection of corruption.
    *   *France*: Budgetary constraints impacting civil society and the "narrowed" civic space (with intimidation of NGOs) signal a weakening of informal checks and balances.
    *   *Hungary*: The environment for civil society organizations (CSOs) has deteriorated significantly. The **Sovereignty Protection Office** has launched investigations into anti-corruption CSOs and those receiving EU funds. A draft law on "transparency in public life" (May 2025) aims to restrict foreign funding for entities deemed to endanger sovereignty, threatening critical voices and external oversight.
    *   *Malta*: Civil society space is assessed as "narrowed." Draft regulatory frameworks for NGOs are under preparation, raising concerns about potential constraints. The **Daphne Caruana Galizia Foundation** remains a key actor in advocating for transparency and justice following the 2017 assassination of the investigative journalist. No progress has been made in establishing a formal framework for public participation in legislative processes, though a Public Consultation Department is planned.
    *   *Poland*: The civic space was re-rated from "obstructed" to "narrowed" by CIVICUS in March 2025. The government has established working groups with CSOs and launched new programs for NGOs. However, challenges remain, including negative rhetoric against NGOs and operational restrictions. This improved rating suggests a slightly better environment for corruption oversight compared to Hungary, but still below optimal standards for robust independent auditing.
    *   *Romania*: Civil society space is considered "narrowed," with CSOs facing administrative barriers and attacks. The **Romanian Institute for Human Rights** faces severe budget cuts and staffing shortages, risking its functionality. The accreditation process for National Human Rights Institutions is stalled due to questions from the Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions (GANHRI) regarding independence and resources. However, the adoption of the **Strategy for Open Government 2025-2030** in April 2025 represents a positive step to address challenges for civil society and enhance public participation.
    *   *Slovakia*: Civil society faces increased pressure through new reporting obligations for NGOs and reduced funding for some human rights advocates. The law-making process suffers from limited stakeholder involvement, with 28% of laws in 2024 adopted via fast-track procedure, bypassing meaningful consultation. While civil society and stakeholders actively monitor and report issues, the hostile political rhetoric and administrative burdens constrain their ability to provide effective oversight.
*   **Media Freedom**:
    *   *Hungary*: Media pluralism is threatened by the disproportionate channeling of state advertising to pro-government outlets and the lack of functional independence of the **National Media and Infocommunications Authority (NMHH)**. Journalists face smear campaigns and investigations for alleged "foreign influence," reducing media's role as a watchdog against corruption.
    *   *Malta*: Limited progress has been made in safeguarding the independent governance of public service media, which remains at "very high risk" for political bias according to the Media Pluralism Monitor. Legislation to improve the working environment for journalists and access to official documents remains pending. However, Malta is preparing legislation to align with the European Media Freedom Act, which may enhance the media regulator’s competences.
    *   *Poland*: Media pluralism shows mixed trends. Politically biased content in public service media has decreased, and state advertising is more evenly distributed. However, concerns remain about the independence of the media regulator (**KRRiT**) and the governance of public broadcasters, which are currently in liquidation. A planned media law reform aims to implement the European Media Freedom Act. The improved distribution of state advertising reduces one avenue for political favoritism, potentially mitigating corruption risks in media procurement.
    *   *Romania*: Media freedom and pluralism face ongoing threats. No progress was made in enhancing the independent governance and editorial independence of public service media. Untransparent political advertising and the financing of private media by political parties have increased, affecting media independence. Journalists continue to face harassment, threats, and political pressure. The **National Audiovisual Council (CNA)** lacks sufficient human resources and an updated IT system, impacting its effectiveness as a regulator.
    *   *Slovakia*: Media freedom is under significant pressure. The new governance structure of **Slovak Television and Radio (STVR)**, established in July 2024, raises concerns about politicization. Amendments to the **Freedom of Information Act** introduced fees and longer response times, facing constitutional challenge, thereby restricting access to public data essential for investigative journalism. The safety of journalists remains a concern, with an increasingly hostile environment and no legislative progress on defamation reform. Political polarization and attacks on NGOs and media by politicians, including Prime Minister Robert Fico, further degrade the media's capacity to act as an anti-corruption watchdog.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit Ratings and Country Risk

The state of the anti-corruption framework directly influences sovereign credit rating factors, particularly under the categories of **Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength**, **Geopolitical and Political Stability**, and **Accountability**.

In Poland, the constitutional crisis regarding the Constitutional Tribunal and the refusal to publish judgments creates a unique legal ambiguity that threatens policy credibility and long-term stability. *   **Strengths**:

Malta’s transfer of prosecutorial powers to the Attorney General and ongoing constitutional reforms to enhance judicial appointment independence represent significant institutional strengthening. Poland’s separation of the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General and the full operation of the EPPO mark significant steps toward depoliticizing prosecution and enhancing enforcement capacity. Romania’s DNA maintains a positive record in prosecuting high-level corruption, and the completion of Venice Commission recommendations on Justice Laws strengthens the institutional framework. In Slovakia, the alignment of criminal codes with the EU PIF Directive and the adoption of New Legislative Rules by the Government offer some procedural improvements, and the Whistleblower Office’s ability to sanction government bodies (e.g., Ministry of Interior) demonstrates functional oversight capabilities.
    *   **Judicial Efficiency**: Hungary’s judiciary is highly efficient in case resolution, though this is offset by independence concerns. Malta faces severe inefficiency issues, but high perceived judicial independence among businesses provides some counterbalance. Poland’s digitalization of courts and mandatory impact assessments for draft laws improve procedural predictability, despite the broader constitutional uncertainties. Romania is progressing with digitalization of public procurement. Slovakia’s judicial map reforms aim to improve access, though efficiency remains a critical weakness.

## Key Vulnerabilities and Recent Developments

The **2025 Rule of Law Reports** for Belgium, France, Hungary, Malta, Poland, Romania, and Slovakia illustrate critical vulnerabilities that serve as early warning indicators for country risk analysts:

1.  **Legislative and Transparency Loopholes**:
    *   *France*: Exclusion of top-executive officials from lobbying disclosure.
    *   *Belgium*: Failure to adopt binding lobbying rules and revolving door regulations.
    *   *Hungary*: Absence of lobbying/revolving door legislation; weak asset declaration oversight; lack of transparency in political party financing.
    *   *Malta*: Unpublished ministers' asset declarations; MPs not covered by conflict of interest rules; lack of formal framework for public participation in legislation.
    *   *Poland*: Lack of comprehensive anti-corruption strategy; absence of lobbying rules; broad immunities for top executives; limited transparency in political party financing.
    *   *Romania*: No lobbying rules for MPs; pending legislation on political party financing; Constitutional Court ruling weakening asset declaration requirements for spouses/children.
    *   *Slovakia*: Consideration of restrictions on public access to asset disclosures; no progress in regulating lobbying; frequent use of fast-track legislative procedures bypassing consultation.
2.  **Judicial Pressure and Independence**:
    *   *France*: Magistrates face political pressure and personal attacks.
    *   *Belgium*: Allegations of political interference in investigations.
    *   *Hungary*: Resignation of National Judicial Council President; withdrawal from reform agreements; lack of transparency in case allocation in lower courts; pressure on judges regarding internal debates.
    *   *Malta*: High perceived independence, but concerns over the independence of the Immigration Appeals Board (ECtHR judgment) and slow law-making processes.
    *   *Poland*: Constitutional crisis regarding the legitimacy of the Constitutional Tribunal; refusal to publish judgments; disputed appointment of judges by the post-2017 NCJ; dismantling of Supreme Court chambers to restore independence.
    *   *Romania*: Perceived judicial independence has decreased; transparency in promotions and disciplinary sanctions faces scrutiny; HCCJ case-law on *lex mitior* creates uncertainty.
    *   *Slovakia*: Low perceived judicial independence (32% public); lack of dismissal safeguards for Judicial Council members; Prosecutor General’s power to annul final decisions; expanded role of Judicial Council in disciplinary proceedings.
3.  **Implementation Challenges**:
    *   *France*: Digitalization of judicial procedures (Portalis) faces technical issues.
    *   *Belgium*: Disparate integrity policies across 181 local police zones.
    *   *Hungary*: Integrity Authority faces obstacles in data access; emergency powers override parliamentary legislation.
    *   *Malta*: PCAC lacks resources and operational readiness; judicial backlog is critical (713 days for criminal cases); slow implementation of the National Anti-Fraud and Corruption Strategy.
    *   *Poland*: Transition from CBA to new Central Office; slow pace of media licensing decisions; 54 pending leading ECtHR judgments; legislative instability affecting legal certainty.
    *   *Romania*: Frequent use of GEOs (199 in 2024) causes legislative unpredictability; public consultations are often ineffective "box-ticking" exercises; CNA lacks resources.
    *   *Slovakia*: Dissolution of NAKA and SPO led to a drop in corruption cases investigated; 33 pending leading ECtHR judgments (as of June 2025); sharp rise in administrative case resolution times (1,040 days).
4.  **Civic Space and Media Constraints**:
    *   *France*: Narrowed civic space and budgetary restrictions for NGOs.
    *   *Hungary*: Deteriorating environment for CSOs; investigations by Sovereignty Protection Office; draft law restricting foreign funding; lack of media pluralism and independent media regulator.
    *   *Malta*: "Narrowed" civil society space with pending NGO regulatory frameworks; public service media at very high risk of political bias; pending legislation for journalist protection and access to official documents.
    *   *Poland*: Civic space "narrowed" (improved from "obstructed"); negative rhetoric against NGOs; concerns over independence of media regulator (KRRiT); public broadcasters in liquidation.
    *   *Romania*: Civic space "narrowed" with administrative barriers; untransparent political advertising and financing of private media by political parties; journalists face harassment and political pressure; human rights institutions face budget cuts and accreditation stalls.
    *   *Slovakia*: Increased pressure on NGOs through new reporting obligations and reduced funding; politicization of public service media (STVR); amendments to Freedom of Information Act restricting access; hostile environment for journalists with no defamation reform.

## Synthesis for Credit Analysts

When assessing the **anti-corruption framework** for sovereign credit purposes, analysts should prioritize:
*   **Independence**: Are investigative bodies (PNF in France, CDBC-OCRC in Belgium, PCAC in Malta, Integrity Authority in Hungary, new Central Office in Poland, DNA in Romania, and UBOK in Slovakia) and judicial councils (CSM, National Judicial Council, Constitutional Court in Malta, NCJ in Poland, HCCJ in Romania, Judicial Council in Slovakia) shielded from political pressure? *Note: Hungary’s judicial independence is significantly compromised; Poland is actively reforming the NCJ and Supreme Court but faces a constitutional crisis regarding the Tribunal; Malta’s is high in perception but faces specific institutional challenges; France/Belgium have strong but pressured systems; Romania has made progress via Venice Commission recommendations but perceptions have decreased; Slovakia’s judicial independence is critically low, with the Prosecutor General’s powers and the lack of Judicial Council safeguards posing severe risks.*
*   **Transparency**: Are asset declarations subject to independent verification? Are lobbying activities, especially at the executive level, fully disclosed? *Note: Hungary and Malta lack effective verification and lobbying regulations for high-level officials; France excludes top executives; Belgium lacks binding rules; Poland lacks lobbying rules entirely and has broad executive immunities; Romania’s asset declaration system was weakened by a Constitutional Court ruling, and no lobbying rules exist for MPs; Slovakia faces potential restrictions on public access to asset disclosures and lacks lobbying regulations.*
*   **Consistency**: Is there a unified legal and enforcement strategy? *Note: Belgium faces fragmentation at the local level; Hungary faces systemic inconsistencies via emergency decrees; Malta faces inconsistencies between strong prosecutorial reform and weak PCAC oversight; Poland is dissolving its main anti-corruption bureau (CBA) and lacks a comprehensive strategy, creating strategic incoherence; Romania’s strategy is on track, but judicial case-law on statute of limitations creates inconsistency in case outcomes; Slovakia’s framework is fragmented due to the dissolution of NAKA/SPO and the lack of a comprehensive anti-corruption strategy implementation plan.*
*   **Effectiveness**: Are new structures adequately resourced? How efficient is the judicial process? *Note: Hungary has high efficiency but low impartiality/oversight; France/Belgium struggle with case durations; Malta suffers from severe judicial backlog (700+ days); Poland has pending ECtHR judgments but is digitalizing courts; Romania faces challenges with judicial perceptions and media regulator resources; Slovakia faces severe inefficiencies in administrative justice (1,040 days) and a high backlog of ECtHR judgments.*
*   **Civic Oversight**: Is there a vibrant civic space and effective whistleblower protection? *Note: Hungary’s civic space is severely constrained; Poland’s has improved to "narrowed" with an active whistleblower law; Malta’s is "narrowed"; France faces budgetary constraints; Belgium has strengthened protections; Romania’s civic space is "narrowed" with weak whistleblower protections and under-resourced human rights institutions; Slovakia’s civic space is under pressure with new NGO reporting burdens and a hostile media environment, though the Whistleblower Office remains functional.*

Weaknesses in these areas signal elevated **institutional risk**, which can negatively impact **policy credibility**, increase the cost of capital, and constrain **fiscal flexibility**. While France, Belgium, and Malta exhibit mature or transitioning frameworks with specific vulnerabilities, Hungary presents a higher risk profile due to systemic erosion of checks and balances. Poland presents a complex, transitioning risk profile: significant progress in separating prosecutorial powers and operationalizing the EPPO offers structural improvements, but the constitutional crisis, lack of lobbying regulations, and vulnerabilities in public procurement sustain medium-term risks to investment protection and fiscal efficiency. Malta’s recent CJEU ruling and judicial efficiency challenges highlight that even with strong independence perceptions, operational failures and transparency gaps can sustain credit risk premia. Romania presents a mixed picture: while the DNA is effective and Venice Commission recommendations are being implemented, the weakening of asset declaration transparency, legislative unpredictability via GEOs, and judicial case-law risks regarding statute of limitations pose medium-term risks to policy credibility and investor confidence. **Slovakia** presents a deteriorating risk profile: the dissolution of specialized anti-corruption bodies, the low perceived judicial independence, the hostile environment for media and civil society, and the high backlog of ECtHR judgments signal a significant weakening of institutional integrity, which threatens to increase the country risk premium and deter foreign investment.
