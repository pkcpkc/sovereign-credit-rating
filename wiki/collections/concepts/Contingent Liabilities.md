# Contingent liabilities

**Contingent liabilities** represent potential obligations that a sovereign government may be forced to assume, depending on the outcome of uncertain future events. These liabilities are not explicitly recorded in standard government debt metrics but can significantly impact **debt sustainability**, fiscal flexibility, and sovereign credit ratings if realized. They primarily arise from three major sources: explicit or implicit guarantees on **State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs)**, the financial sector (banks), and off-balance-sheet financing vehicles.

In the context of sovereign credit rating methodologies, contingent liabilities are critical components for determining a sovereign's true fiscal burden. Major rating agencies, including **S&P Global Ratings** and **Fitch Ratings**, as well as the **IMF**, integrate these risks into their assessments to ensure that sovereigns with large, risky banking sectors or significant implicit guarantees are not overrated based solely on explicit debt figures. The analysis of contingent liabilities is particularly central to the **Fiscal Assessment** pillar, acting as a bridge between explicit debt stocks and potential future flows.

## Role in Sovereign Rating Methodology

### S&P Global Ratings Framework
In the S&P Global Ratings framework, contingent liabilities are assessed as a distinct component within the broader **Fiscal Assessment**, which is often described as the "heart" of the rating process. This assessment is split into "flows" (fiscal performance/flexibility) and "stocks" (debt burden/structure), with contingent liabilities analyzed separately to capture risks not reflected in headline debt metrics.

*   **Banking Sector Contingent Liabilities**: The most prominent source analyzed is the **banking sector**. S&P utilizes the **Banking Industry Country Risk Assessment (BICRA)** in conjunction with the relative size of the banking system to estimate the potential fiscal cost of banking sector stress.
    *   **Assessment Mechanism**: Combines inherent banking industry risk (BICRA) with sector size relative to the economy.
    *   **Categorization**: Risks are categorized into four levels: Limited, Moderate, High, and Very High.
    *   **Rating Impact**: High or very high levels lead to negative adjustments in the sovereign's debt burden assessment, potentially lowering the fiscal assessment score by up to **three categories**. This reflects the probability of capital injections, bailouts, or liquidity support required during a crisis, thereby increasing the effective **net general government debt**.

*   **Non-Financial Public Enterprises and Guarantees**: Beyond banking, S&P explicitly analyzes contingent liabilities stemming from non-financial public enterprises (SOEs) and off-budget guarantees. These are evaluated for their likelihood of crystallization and the magnitude of the fiscal cost.

*   **Discretion and Supplemental Adjustments**: While the methodology provides structured tables for assessing these risks, S&P allows for significant analyst discretion. "Supplemental adjustment factors" can be applied to the final rating if contingent liabilities pose extreme risks (e.g., extremely high fiscal debt or event risk) that are not fully captured by the standard indicative rating. This discretion highlights the subjective nature of evaluating implicit liabilities.

### Integration with Fiscal Assessment
Contingent liabilities are integrated into the broader fiscal assessment, which focuses on the change in **net general government debt** (gross debt minus liquid financial assets) as a percentage of GDP. The contingent liabilities analysis acts as a qualitative and quantitative adjuster, ensuring that sovereigns with large, risky banking sectors or significant implicit guarantees (such as those on SOEs) are not overrated based solely on explicit debt figures.

### Fitch Ratings Framework (Effective September 2025)
Fitch Ratings incorporates contingent liabilities primarily through its **Qualitative Overlay (QO)** mechanism, adjusting the quantitative output of the **Sovereign Rating Model (SRM)**.

*   **Public Finances Assessment**: While the SRM quantitatively assesses variables like gross general government debt/GDP and interest payments/revenue, the QO evaluates **fiscal financing flexibility** and **debt sustainability** using Fitch’s proprietary **Debt Dynamics Model**.
*   **Crystallization Risk**: Within the QO for Public Finances, contingent liabilities—specifically banking sector support and SOE debts—are assessed for their potential to crystallize into explicit debt. This assessment considers the probability of activation and the magnitude of the fiscal cost.
*   **Macro-Prudential Indicators**: Fitch enhances its monitoring of financial sector risks, which drive contingent liabilities, using the **Macro-Prudential Indicator (MPI)** and the **Banking Systemic Indicator (BSI)**. These tools help quantify the likelihood of financial sector stress that would necessitate sovereign intervention.
*   **Adjustment Mechanism**: The QO allows for notch adjustments of ±2 per pillar. If contingent liabilities pose a significant unquantified risk to fiscal sustainability, they can negatively influence the Public Finances pillar, contributing to the overall ±3 notch adjustment cap from the SRM output.

## Emerging Risks: SOEs and Off-Balance-Sheet Financing

Recent assessments, particularly the **IMF’s 2025 Article IV Consultation for Hungary** and **Italy**, highlight the growing significance of contingent liabilities stemming from the state sector beyond traditional banking risks.

### State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs) and Guarantees
SOEs represent a major source of contingent liabilities due to the expectation that governments will bail out loss-making entities or honor guarantees.
*   **Magnitude**: In the case of Hungary, contingent liabilities from SOEs and related government guarantees were estimated at **14% of GDP** in 2024.
*   **Transparency Issues**: Limited transparency in off-budget financing vehicles (foundations, Special Purpose Vehicles/SPVs) and SOE performance creates implicit fiscal risks. The rapid growth of SOE liabilities exacerbates this concern.
*   **Monitoring**: While authorities may begin monitoring SOE performance, the lack of comprehensive data often leads to underestimation of the true fiscal risk.

### Off-Balance-Sheet Financing
The use of off-balance-sheet vehicles allows governments to finance expenditures without immediately impacting headline debt metrics. However, these vehicles often carry implicit sovereign backing, creating significant **contingent liabilities** that can crystallize into direct debt during periods of stress.

### Public Guarantees: The Italy Case Study
The **IMF’s 2025 Article IV Consultation for Italy** provides a detailed contemporary analysis of how public guarantees function as contingent liabilities in high-debt environments.
*   **Stock of Guarantees**: Italy held an elevated stock of public guarantees amounting to **€294 billion**, or **13.4% of GDP**. This represents a significant contingent liability that could materialize if guarantee calls increase.
*   **Risk Dynamics**: While the stock of guarantees has seen a gradual decline, the potential for spillovers from bank recapitalization or new guarantee issuances remains a key threat to fiscal sustainability.
*   **Mitigation Factors**: The IMF notes strengths in the Italian framework, including:
    *   **Improved Private-Sector Risk Sharing**: New guarantees increasingly involve private sector co-financing, reducing the pure fiscal burden on the sovereign.
    *   **Centralized Monitoring**: Authorities have implemented centralized monitoring of exposures, enhancing transparency and early warning capabilities.
    *   **Fiscal Space Constraints**: Given Italy’s high public debt ratio (135.3% of GDP in 2024), the capacity to absorb sudden shocks from contingent liabilities is limited, making the management of these exposures critical for maintaining market confidence.

## The Sovereign-Bank Nexus

The interconnection between sovereign creditworthiness and banking sector health, known as the **sovereign-bank nexus**, is a key driver of contingent liability risks.

*   **Feedback Loop**: Banks hold significant amounts of government debt. If sovereign creditworthiness deteriorates, bank balance sheets are impaired, potentially requiring sovereign bailouts. Conversely, banking crises require sovereign funds, worsening the fiscal position.
*   **Case Study (Hungary)**: In Hungary, banks' growing exposure to sovereign debt amplifies macro-financial risks. While the banking sector remains well-capitalized (CAR 20.5%) and liquid, the IMF notes that this linkage remains a vulnerability.
*   **Case Study (Italy)**:
    *   **Persisting Linkages**: Despite improvements in banking sector profitability and capital buffers, the sovereign-bank nexus in Italy remains **sizable**. Banks retain significant exposure to sovereign debt, and the IMF identifies potential deterioration of asset quality due to trade shocks as a trigger for sovereign support.
    *   **Vulnerabilities**: Weaknesses among some **Less Significant Institutions (LSIs)** in Italy pose a risk of requiring fiscal support, thereby activating contingent liabilities.
    *   **Recapitalization Risk**: Although current recapitalization risk may be low in stable periods, the **sovereign-bank nexus** ensures that any shock to the corporate or housing sector (e.g., via non-performing loans) could quickly translate into a sovereign contingent liability if systemic stability is threatened. The activation of the **Systemic Risk Buffer (SyRB)** is a preventive measure, but its sufficiency under severe stress remains a subject of surveillance.

## Strategic Significance for Sovereigns

Managing contingent liabilities is essential for maintaining a strong credit profile and ensuring long-term fiscal sustainability.

*   **Fiscal Flexibility**: High contingent liabilities reduce fiscal flexibility, as governments must reserve budgetary space for potential emergency expenditures rather than productive investment. In Italy, limited fiscal space due to high debt levels makes the containment of contingent liabilities (like public guarantees) vital for implementing structural reforms.
*   **Debt Payment Culture**: The willingness of a sovereign to honor implicit guarantees is part of the broader **institutional assessment**, specifically regarding **debt payment culture**. A history of honoring implicit liabilities may strengthen institutional scores, while defaulting on them (or refusing to rescue systemically important banks) may lead to higher future costs and reputational damage. In S&P’s methodology, a weak debt payment culture can cap the **Institutional Assessment** score, indirectly pressuring the final rating.
*   **External Liquidity**: Large contingent liabilities can threaten **external liquidity** if a crisis triggers capital flight or requires substantial foreign currency interventions to stabilize the financial sector. For Italy, sizable **TARGET2 liabilities** and large refinancing needs heighten the importance of managing contingent risks to avoid external sector vulnerabilities.
*   **Market Confidence**: High risk premia and widening bond spreads often reflect market concerns regarding hidden risks like contingent liabilities from SOEs and the sovereign-bank nexus. Transparent monitoring and private-sector risk-sharing mechanisms (as seen in Italy's recent policy adjustments) help mitigate these concerns.
