# Macroeconomic imbalance

**Macroeconomic imbalance** refers to significant deviations in key economic aggregates—such as current account deficits, real effective exchange rates, house price growth, and government debt—that pose risks to financial stability and sustainable growth. Within the framework of the **European Semester** and EU economic governance, these imbalances are monitored through the **Macroeconomic Imbalance Procedure (MIP)** to prevent crises and ensure convergence toward stable economic performance.

As of 2024-2025, **Romania** serves as a primary case study for how **fiscal policy** errors can rapidly translate into external imbalances, threatening **debt sustainability** and sovereign credit ratings. The interaction between loose fiscal stances, weak structural reforms, and external vulnerabilities creates a feedback loop that undermines **investment climate** confidence and long-term growth prospects.

## Key Dimensions of Macroeconomic Imbalance

### 1. Current Account and External Vulnerabilities
A widening **current account deficit** is a primary indicator of macroeconomic imbalance, signaling that domestic investment exceeds domestic savings, requiring financing from abroad.
*   **Romania Case (2024)**: The current account deficit widened significantly to **8.4% of GDP**. This deterioration was driven by:
    *   High import demand fueled by fiscal expansion and robust private consumption.
    *   Weak external competitiveness.
    *   A negative **net international investment position (NIIP)** of -41.5% of GDP, indicating that foreign liabilities exceed assets.
*   **Sovereign Credit Impact**: Persistent external deficits increase reliance on foreign capital inflows. If investor sentiment shifts, this can lead to **currency depreciation** pressure (managed float against the Euro) and higher **funding costs** for sovereign debt. The high share of banking sector claims on government (25% of assets) creates a "doom loop" risk where fiscal stress impacts bank stability.

### 2. Fiscal Imbalances and Debt Sustainability
Fiscal imbalance occurs when government expenditures exceed revenues over sustained periods, leading to rising public debt. Under the reformed EU **Fiscal Policy** framework, member states are required to adhere to binding expenditure limits.
*   **Romania Case (2024-2025)**:
    *   **Deficit Surge**: The government deficit reached **9.3% of GDP** in 2024, driven by generous public sector wage increases and higher public investment spending.
    *   **Expenditure Growth**: Net expenditure growth hit **19.9%**, far exceeding recommended maximums.
    *   **Debt Trajectory**: Public debt rose rapidly to **54.8% of GDP** in 2024, with projections reaching 63.3% by 2026.
*   **Policy Response**: The **European Commission** endorsed Romania's **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP)**, extending the adjustment period to 2031. Immediate consolidation measures (wage/pension freezes, tax reforms) aim to save 2.0% of GDP in 2025. However, the **Romanian Fiscal Council** and international institutions (World Bank, OECD) warn that without growth-enhancing reforms, fiscal adjustment remains costly and risks stalling growth.

### 3. Structural Weaknesses and Growth Imbalances
Macroeconomic imbalances are often symptomatic of deeper structural issues that hinder productivity and innovation.
*   **Investment Climate & Regulatory Uncertainty**: Frequent changes in top political decision-makers, use of government emergency ordinances, and volatility in tax rules create a hostile environment for long-term investment. **90% of Romanian firms** cite regulatory uncertainty as the top barrier to investment.
*   **Productivity Gap**: Low **R&D intensity** (0.52% of GDP, lowest in the EU) and outdated infrastructure limit the economy's ability to transition to a high-value growth model. This reliance on low-productivity, consumption-driven growth exacerbates external deficits.
*   **Labor Market Mismatches**: Despite an improved employment rate, low labor force participation (particularly among women and youth) and severe skills shortages in STEM/digital sectors constrain potential output.

## Strategic Implications for Stakeholders

1.  **For Investors**: Monitor the implementation of the **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan**. Delays in structural reforms (tax framework review, SOE governance) could trigger rating downgrades or increased sovereign spreads.
2.  **For Policymakers**: Priority must be given to enhancing **public administration efficiency** and reducing the **VAT gap** to improve revenue collection without raising tax rates. Accelerating **green transition** investments via EU funds is critical for long-term competitiveness.
3.  **For Credit Analysts**: The convergence of **fiscal consolidation** and **structural reform** is the key determinant of risk trajectory. While debt levels remain below the EU average (81.6%), the velocity of debt accumulation and the negative NIIP pose significant tail risks if external conditions worsen (e.g., US tariffs, energy price shocks).
