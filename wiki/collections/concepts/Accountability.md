# Accountability

**Accountability** in the context of sovereign credit analysis and rule of law assessment refers to the mechanisms, institutions, and legal frameworks that ensure public officials, institutions, and state-owned enterprises are answerable for their actions, decisions, and use of public resources. It encompasses transparency, oversight, anti-corruption measures, and the ability of civil society and media to hold power to account.

Recent assessments, including the **2025 Rule of Law Reports** on France, Poland, and Romania, illustrate divergent trajectories in accountability implementation. While France demonstrates strong legal frameworks with practical implementation gaps regarding political elite transparency, and Poland shows legislative efforts to address historical rule of law concerns amidst persistent executive immunities and constitutional crises, Romania presents a mixed picture of institutional progress in judicial independence alongside systemic risks arising from judicial interpretations of statute of limitations and legislative unpredictability. These dynamics directly impact **[[Policy Credibility]]**, **[[Institutional Strength]]**, and ultimately **[[Sovereign Credit]]** ratings.

## Sovereign Credit & Macroeconomic Impact

Accountability is a critical determinant of **[[Policy Credibility]]** and **[[Governance Quality]]**, directly influencing sovereign credit ratings through several channels:

### 1. Fiscal Sustainability and Debt Burden
- **Resource Allocation Efficiency:** Effective accountability mechanisms reduce corruption and mismanagement in public procurement. In Poland, public procurement remains highly vulnerable to corruption with significant single-bid rates. In France, bodies like the **National Financial Prosecutor (PNF)** and **Anti-Corruption Agency (AFA)** combat financial crimes. In Romania, while the digitalization of public procurement is ongoing, the regulatory burden and frequent use of **Government Emergency Ordinances (GEOs)** create an unpredictable environment that deters efficient investment.
- **Contingent Liabilities:** Weak accountability can lead to hidden contingent liabilities. Poland faces specific risks from state-owned enterprises (SOEs). France notes risks in SOEs impacting long-term fiscal sustainability. In Romania, the annulment of convictions and discontinuation of corruption cases due to *lex mitior* interpretations raise risks regarding the recovery of misused funds and the effective prosecution of high-level corruption, potentially affecting fiscal integrity.
- **Recovery of Public Funds:** Strong accountability allows for the recovery of misused funds (e.g., EUR 6 million recovered from MPs in France). In Romania, the credibility of the asset declaration system has been weakened by a Constitutional Court ruling that deemed the requirement to declare spouses’ and children’s assets unconstitutional, limiting the transparency and effectiveness of asset recovery mechanisms.

### 2. Investment Climate and Legal Uncertainty
- **Contract Enforcement & Legal Certainty:** High confidence in investment protection by law and courts enhances **[[Economic Strength]]**. In Poland, low confidence in investment protection persists due to legislative instability. Conversely, France maintains high confidence in contract enforcement. In Romania, over half of companies express confidence in investment protection; however, legislative unpredictability cited by businesses—driven by 199 GEOs adopted in 2024—remains a primary concern. The frequent changes in fiscal code via GEOs affect micro-enterprise classification thresholds, creating operational uncertainty.
- **Corruption Risks:** Perception of corruption affects foreign direct investment (FDI). Poland’s lack of lobbying rules hinders high-level corruption prosecutions. France faces gaps in lobbying disclosure for top executives. In Romania, while the **National Anti-Corruption Directorate (DNA)** maintains a positive record in prosecuting high-level cases, systemic risks arise from the High Court of Cassation and Justice’s (HCCJ) interpretation of *lex mitior*, which led to the discontinuation of numerous corruption cases and annulment of convictions. This raises concerns about impunity and EU law compatibility, deterring investors who fear an uneven playing field.

### 3. Institutional Stability and Political Risk
- **Judicial Independence:** An independent judiciary is vital for holding the executive accountable. Poland’s ongoing constitutional crisis regarding the legitimacy of the Constitutional Tribunal creates significant political risk. In France, judicial autonomy is defended by the Superior Council of the Magistracy, though political attacks persist. In Romania, significant progress was made in completing the process to implement Venice Commission recommendations on the Justice Laws. The Ministry of Justice has shown openness to ensuring judicial independence in IT system reforms. However, perceived judicial independence has decreased among the general public and companies. The HCCJ’s recent case-law on statute of limitations, extending beyond the 2018-2022 legal void, has raised concerns about the effectiveness of anti-corruption efforts.
- **Civic Space:** A narrowed civic space weakens external oversight. Poland’s civic space was re-rated from "obstructed" to "narrowed" in 2025. In France, budgetary restrictions and intimidation of civil society weaken this layer of accountability. In Romania, civil society space is considered narrowed, with CSOs facing administrative barriers and attacks. Although a **Strategy for Open Government 2025-2030** was adopted to address these challenges, the accreditation process for National Human Rights Institutions (Romanian Institute for Human Rights and People's Advocate) remains stalled due to questions regarding independence and resources. The Romanian Institute for Human Rights faces severe budget cuts, risking its functionality as a watchdog.
- **Media Pluralism:** Free and pluralistic media are essential for exposing corruption. Poland has seen decreased political bias in public service media but concerns remain regarding the regulator. France’s **[[ARCOM]]** is expanding its regulatory role. In Romania, media freedom faces ongoing threats, including untransparent political advertising, harassment of journalists, and political pressure on editorial independence. The **National Audiovisual Council (CNA)** lacks sufficient human resources and an updated IT system, impacting its effectiveness. No progress has been made in enhancing the independent governance of public service media.

## Key Mechanisms and Institutions

Accountability is operationalized through a network of independent bodies, legal frameworks, and societal actors:

### 1. Anti-Corruption and Transparency Frameworks
- **Institutional Capacity:** 
  - **Poland:** The **European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO)** is fully operational. A new Central Office for Combating Corruption is being established within the police.
  - **France:** Strengthening bodies like the **[[Anti-Corruption Agency]] (AFA)** and **[[High Authority for Transparency in Public Life]] (HATVP)** is crucial. AFA was reorganized, and HATVP increased staff to handle rising asset declarations.
  - **Romania:** The **National Anti-Corruption Directorate (DNA)** maintains a stable occupancy rate despite vacancies and continues to secure convictions in high-level cases. The implementation of the 2021-2025 National Anti-Corruption Strategy is on track, with a new strategy under preparation based on ex-post evaluation. However, the **National Integrity Agency (ANI)**’s asset declaration system faces challenges after a Constitutional Court ruling deemed the requirement to declare spouses’ and children’s assets unconstitutional, potentially weakening transparency.
- **Lobbying Transparency:** A significant gap remains in all three jurisdictions. Poland has made no progress in introducing lobbying rules. In France, lobbying disclosure requirements often do not apply to top-executive level officials. In Romania, no progress has been made on introducing lobbying rules for Members of Parliament, limiting transparency of political influence and posing risks to **[[Checks and Balances]]**.
- **Whistleblower Protection:** 
  - **Poland:** An operational whistleblower protection law has been enforced largely since September 2024.
  - **France:** Effective whistleblowing mechanisms are essential, though insufficient public funding can hinder them.
  - **Romania:** Protection for whistleblowers in public bodies remains weak, identified as a threat to accountability.

### 2. Judicial and Legislative Oversight
- **[[Judicial Independence]]:** 
  - **Poland:** Significant efforts have been made to implement the Action Plan on the Rule of Law, including proposals to address the status of judges appointed by the post-2017 National Council for the Judiciary (NCJ). However, the refusal to publish Constitutional Tribunal judgments continues to fuel a constitutional crisis.
  - **France:** The **Superior Council of the Magistracy (CSM)** actively defends judicial autonomy, though political interference and attacks on magistrates remain concerns.
  - **Romania:** The process to take into account Venice Commission recommendations on Justice Laws has been completed. Legislative steps are being explored to extend the mandates of high-ranking prosecutors from three to five years to strengthen safeguards. The HCCJ has sought clarification from the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) regarding *lex mitior* applications (Case C-280/25 Lin II).
- **Prosecution Service Independence:** 
  - **Poland:** Major progress has been made in separating the office of the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General.
  - **France:** The **European Public Prosecutor Office (EPPO)** collaboration enhances anti-corruption efforts.
  - **Romania:** The DNA maintains stable occupancy. However, the annulment of convictions due to expired limitation periods raises questions about the long-term sustainability of high-level corruption prosecutions.
- **Legislative Processes:** 
  - **Poland:** Strengthened rules for legislative consultations, including mandatory impact assessments, improve policy design. However, frequent legislative changes affect legal certainty.
  - **France:** Accelerated legislative procedures and reduced citizen consultation can undermine legislative accountability.
  - **Romania:** Frequent use of **Government Emergency Ordinances (GEOs)** contributes to legislative unpredictability. Public consultations are often criticized as ineffective "box-ticking" exercises. Changes in fiscal code via GEOs affect micro-enterprise classification thresholds.
- **Auditing and Control:** Independent auditing bodies contribute to transparency. Poland’s Supreme Audit Office and France’s Court of Audit are active. In Romania, the **Court of Accounts** plays a role, but legislative instability adds to unpredictability in fiscal oversight.

### 3. Media and Civic Oversight
- **[[Media Pluralism]] and Freedom:** 
  - **Poland:** Politically biased content in public service media has decreased. A planned media law reform aims to address the independence of the regulator (KRRiT).
  - **France:** Progress in media ownership transparency, but challenges in practical access to documents persist.
  - **Romania:** No progress was made in enhancing the independent governance and editorial independence of public service media. Untransparent political advertising and financing of private media by political parties have increased, affecting media independence. The CNA lacks sufficient resources.
- **Civil Society:** 
  - **Poland:** The Government established working groups with NGOs, but operational restrictions and negative rhetoric persist.
  - **France:** Civil society acts as a watchdog, but budgetary restrictions and intimidation narrow the civic space.
  - **Romania:** The accreditation process for National Human Rights Institutions is stalled. The Romanian Institute for Human Rights faces severe budget cuts and staffing shortages. A **Strategy for Open Government 2025-2030** was adopted to address challenges for civil society, but administrative barriers and attacks on CSOs remain.

### 4. International and European Mechanisms
- **[[EU Rule of Law Mechanism]]:** Provides external oversight. Poland is engaged in an ongoing infringement procedure regarding the Constitutional Tribunal.
- **European Court of Human Rights (ECHR):** Implementation of judgments is a key metric. Poland has 54 pending leading judgments as of June 2025. France has seen a decrease in pending judgments. In Romania, the number of ECHR judgments pending implementation has decreased, indicating improved compliance with international human rights obligations.
- **Venice Commission:** Collaboration enhances standards. Poland consults with the Venice Commission on NCJ and Supreme Court reforms. Romania has completed the process to implement Venice Commission recommendations on the Justice Laws.

## Risks and Threats to Accountability

1. **Executive Immunity and Gaps:** Broad immunities for top executives in Poland hinder criminal accountability. In France, exclusions of top executives from lobbying disclosure create opacity. In Romania, the weakening of the asset declaration system due to Constitutional Court rulings limits transparency for high-ranking officials.
2. **Constitutional and Legal Crises:** Poland’s refusal to publish Constitutional Tribunal judgments undermines legal accountability. In Romania, the HCCJ’s interpretation of *lex mitior* leading to the annulment of convictions creates systemic risks regarding impunity and raises concerns about compatibility with EU law.
3. **Legislative Instability:** Frequent changes in legislation (Poland) and accelerated procedures with reduced consultation (France) lead to legal uncertainty. In Romania, the frequent use of GEOs (199 in 2024) is cited as a primary concern for businesses, contributing to regulatory unpredictability and affecting **[[Policy Credibility]]**.
4. **Corruption in Public Procurement and Judiciary:** Systemic transparency issues in Poland. In Romania, the discontinuation of corruption cases due to statute of limitations interpretations threatens the deterrent effect of anti-corruption frameworks.
5. **Resource Constraints:** Budgetary restrictions impact the effectiveness of anti-corruption bodies and civil society organizations in both France and Poland. In Romania, severe budget cuts to the Romanian Institute for Human Rights risk its functionality.
6. **Narrowing Civic Space:** Intimidation of civil society and negative rhetoric against NGOs reduce external oversight in Poland. In France, budgetary restrictions narrow the civic space. In Romania, civil society faces administrative barriers and attacks, with the accreditation of human rights institutions stalled.
7. **Media Independence Threats:** In Poland, concerns remain regarding the media regulator. In France, access to documents is challenged. In Romania, untransparent political advertising, harassment of journalists, and lack of progress in public service media governance threaten media pluralism and its role in accountability.

## Strengths in Accountability Frameworks

1. **Independent Regulatory Bodies:** 
   - **Poland:** High level of independence of the national competition authority.
   - **France:** Credible institutions like ARCOM, HATVP, and AFA provide robust oversight.
   - **Romania:** The DNA maintains a stable occupancy rate and continues to secure convictions in high-level cases. The National Integrity Agency maintains results in managing conflicts of interest despite recent legal challenges to asset declarations.
2. **Institutional Reforms:** 
   - **Poland:** Separation of Minister of Justice from Prosecutor General; establishment of EPPO; mandatory impact assessments for draft laws.
   - **France:** Strong ethical codes for Parliament and Government; digitalization of judicial procedures.
   - **Romania:** Completion of the process to implement Venice Commission recommendations on Justice Laws. Legislative steps to extend mandates of high-ranking prosecutors. Openness to adapt IT system reforms to guarantee judicial independence.
3. **Effective Oversight Mechanisms:** 
   - **Poland:** Active Ombudsperson fulfilling checks and balances role; Supreme Audit Office controls.
   - **France:** Defender of Rights and independent auditing by the Court of Audit.
   - **Romania:** Improved transparency of case-law on disciplinary sanctions in the judiciary. The European Public Prosecutor’s Office (EPPO) collaboration enhances anti-corruption efforts.
4. **Whistleblower Protection:** Poland has an operational whistleblower protection law. (Note: Romania has weak protection).
5. **International Cooperation:** Collaboration with bodies like EPPO, Venice Commission, and GRECO enhances anti-corruption efforts and rule of law standards. Romania’s cooperation in implementing Venice Commission recommendations is a key strength.

## Synthesis

Accountability is a dynamic system requiring continuous reinforcement of institutions, resources, and political will. The 2025 Rule of Law Reports for France, Poland, and Romania highlight that while all three nations possess strong foundational institutions, the *practical implementation* and *political will* to uphold accountability vary significantly, impacting sovereign credit profiles differently.

In **Poland**, accountability is currently strained by a constitutional crisis, executive immunities, and the lack of lobbying regulations, despite positive steps in judicial reform, prosecution independence, and whistleblower protection. These deficits pose elevated risks to **[[Policy Credibility]]** and **[[Investment Climate]]**, potentially impacting sovereign credit ratings through increased political risk and legal uncertainty.

In **France**, accountability frameworks are robust, but gaps in political transparency (lobbying) and civic space constraints pose moderate risks. The strength of institutions like the AFA and PNF mitigates some fiscal risks, but continued erosion of civic oversight could undermine long-term **[[Governance Quality]]**.

In **Romania**, accountability presents a complex duality. On one hand, there is significant progress in judicial independence reforms, adherence to Venice Commission recommendations, and the operational stability of the DNA. On the other hand, systemic risks are emerging from the judiciary’s interpretation of *lex mitior*, which threatens the efficacy of anti-corruption prosecutions by leading to the annulment of convictions. Furthermore, legislative unpredictability driven by frequent GEOs and weaknesses in political financing transparency, lobbying rules, and media independence constrain **[[Policy Credibility]]**. The narrowing of civic space and resource constraints on human rights institutions further weaken external oversight. For sovereign credit analysis, Romania’s trajectory involves balancing institutional gains in judicial independence against procedural and interpretive risks in the justice system that could lead to perceptions of impunity and regulatory uncertainty. Close monitoring of the CJEU’s clarification on *lex mitior*, the implementation of the new Anti-Corruption Strategy, and the effectiveness of the Open Government Strategy is essential to assess the impact on **[[Fiscal Sustainability]]** and **[[Economic Strength]]**.
