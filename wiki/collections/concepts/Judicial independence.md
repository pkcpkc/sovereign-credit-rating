# Judicial Independence

**Judicial independence** refers to the principle that the judiciary must be free from external pressures, particularly from the executive and legislative branches of government, to ensure impartial and fair adjudication. In the context of sovereign credit ratings and macroeconomic stability, judicial independence is a critical component of **[[Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength]]**. High perceived independence correlates with stronger investor confidence, robust contract enforcement, and lower country risk, while structural weaknesses in judicial appointments, prosecutorial autonomy, resource management, safety of magistrates, or case allocation transparency can introduce political risk and deter long-term foreign direct investment.

Recent European Commission Rule of Law Reports (2025) highlight diverging and deteriorating paths among EU member states. **Poland** remains a significant case of transitional reform amidst deep-seated structural crises. **Hungary** continues as an outlier of systemic deterioration. **Austria**, **Belgium**, **France**, **Italy**, **Malta**, and **Romania** face varying degrees of structural or operational challenges. **Slovakia** presents a concerning profile where limited progress in addressing rule of law concerns coexists with low perceived judicial independence, weak safeguards for judicial council members, and fragmented anti-corruption frameworks.

## Relevance to Sovereign Credit Ratings and Country Risk

Judicial independence serves as a foundational pillar for **[[Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength]]**. For credit analysts, it mitigates political risk by ensuring that contracts are enforced impartially and that corruption does not distort market outcomes. Weaknesses in this area can lead to downgrades in governance scores, increased risk premiums, and reduced access to capital. Specifically, the safety of magistrates, the transparency of lobbying affecting judicial outcomes, the efficiency of case resolution, and the independence of case allocation mechanisms directly impact the **Accountability** and **Economic Strength** rating factors.

In extreme cases, such as **Hungary**, the erosion of judicial independence through the override of parliamentary legislation by emergency decrees and the lack of judicial review over "strategic importance" decrees creates a severe risk of arbitrary asset nationalization and discriminatory regulatory pressure. This directly threatens **Growth Prospects** and **Economic Strength and Tax Base**, as foreign companies face legal uncertainty and potential de-investment. Similarly, in **Italy**, the frequent use of urgency decrees and the stalled progress on lobbying transparency introduce governance volatility, while the ongoing debate over separating judicial and prosecutorial careers creates institutional friction that may impact **Policymaking Stability**. In **Malta**, despite high public trust, the inability to secure final judgments in high-level corruption cases and the recent CJEU ruling against the investor citizenship scheme highlight risks to **Financial Sector Health** and **Contingent Liabilities**, as regulatory loopholes and weak enforcement mechanisms can expose the state to legal and reputational risks.

In **Poland**, the sovereign credit profile is heavily influenced by the ongoing constitutional crisis and the legitimacy dispute surrounding the Constitutional Tribunal and the NCJ. The refusal to publish Constitutional Tribunal judgments and the unresolved status of judges appointed by the post-2017 NCJ create significant **legal uncertainty** for investors. While the government has taken steps to dismantle the Chamber of Extraordinary Control and separate the Prosecutor General from the Ministry of Justice—positive signals for **Institutional Strength**—the lack of a comprehensive anti-corruption strategy and broad executive immunities hinder accountability. These factors contribute to low corporate confidence in investment protection due to legislative instability, impacting **Growth Prospects** and increasing the risk premium associated with Poland’s sovereign debt.

In **Romania**, judicial independence is characterized by a tension between structural reforms and operational risks. While Romania has completed the process to implement Venice Commission recommendations on Justice Laws and shown openness to safeguard judicial independence in IT system reforms, the perception of judicial independence has decreased. The most significant credit risk stems from the High Court of Cassation and Justice’s (HCCJ) interpretation of *lex mitior* regarding the statute of limitations, which has led to the discontinuation of numerous corruption cases. This creates a risk of impunity for high-level officials, undermining **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]** effectiveness and increasing **Contingent Liabilities** for the state. Furthermore, the weakening of the asset declaration system by a Constitutional Court ruling reduces transparency, negatively impacting **Policy Credibility**.

**Slovakia** presents a distinct risk profile characterized by low perceived judicial independence (32% among the public, 34% among companies) and structural vulnerabilities in judicial governance. The lack of sufficient safeguards for the dismissal of Judicial Council members, particularly those not elected by judges, raises concerns about potential executive influence over judicial appointments and discipline. The broad powers retained by the Prosecutor General to annul final decisions and interfere in high-level corruption cases further undermine the finality of judicial decisions, creating **legal uncertainty** for investors. Additionally, the dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities (NAKA and SPO) has led to a drop in investigated corruption cases and operational inefficiencies, weakening the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**. The poor business climate perception, driven by these rule of law risks and lengthy administrative proceedings, negatively impacts **Growth Prospects** and signals higher country risk.

### Case Study: Austria – Perception vs. Structural Integrity

According to the **EU Commission 2025 Rule of Law Report** on Austria, judicial independence remains a key strength in the country's sovereign credit profile, yet structural vulnerabilities persist that require monitoring.

#### Strengths: Perception and Efficiency
*   **High Public Perception:** Austria maintains a high level of perceived judicial independence among the general population (86% in 2025, up from 82% in 2024). Corporate perception remains strong at 71%, though it decreased slightly from 77% in 2024. This high perception supports the rating factor of **Stable democratic framework**.
*   **System Efficiency:** The justice system is highly efficient, with civil and commercial cases resolved in an average of 141 days. Digitalization initiatives, such as "Justice 3.0," aim for 90% digital case management by the end of 2025, further enhancing transparency and speed.
*   **Transparency of Judgments:** As of January 1, 2025, the compulsory online publication of judgments was expanded to Higher Regional Courts, enhancing legal certainty and public scrutiny.

#### Threats: Structural Deficiencies and Politicization
Despite strong perceptions, specific structural issues pose risks to the long-term sustainability of judicial independence:

1.  **Judicial Appointments for Administrative Courts:**
    *   **Issue:** There has been no progress in introducing systematic judicial involvement in the appointment of administrative court (vice-)presidents. Current varied procedures lack consistent judicial input, diverging from European standards.
    *   **Impact:** This raises concerns regarding the **Checks and balances** mechanism within the judiciary, potentially undermining the neutrality of administrative justice.

2.  **Prosecutorial Autonomy:**
    *   **Issue:** Reform to establish an independent Federal Prosecution Office has seen limited progress. The Minister of Justice retains the power to issue instructions in individual cases (31 cases examined in 2024). An independent investigative commission in July 2024 confirmed past instances of political influence on prosecutorial actions.
    *   **Impact:** Weak **[[Prosecutorial autonomy]]** threatens the integrity of anti-corruption enforcement and the rule of law, a key pillar for **Policy credibility and institutional strength**.

3.  **Access to Justice and Court Fees:**
    *   **Issue:** Court fees increased by approximately 23% on April 1, 2025, raising concerns about barriers to access to justice. However, the Constitutional Court ruled in October 2024 that restrictions on legal aid in the Administrative Procedural Code were unconstitutional, mandating legal aid for all administrative court proceedings.
    *   **Impact:** Ensuring equal access to courts is essential for the legitimacy of the judicial system.

### Case Study: Belgium – Institutional Safeguards and Compliance Challenges

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Belgium** reveals a system where institutional safeguards are strong, but operational efficiency and compliance with judicial decisions remain significant risks to governance stability.

#### Strengths: Institutional Protection and Investment Confidence
*   **Budgetary Independence:** The Belgian Government has exempted the judiciary from budget cuts and intends to transfer budgetary management powers for justice administration from the executive to the judiciary. This is a critical strength for **Institutional Strength**, signaling protection of the judiciary from fiscal politicization.
*   **High Corporate Confidence:** 60% of companies express confidence in the effectiveness of investment protection by law and courts, supporting the **Economic Strength and Tax Base** rating factor.
*   **Judicial Accountability Mechanisms:** The joint memorandum by the three highest courts requiring the Government to respect court judgments represents a significant check on executive power.

#### Threats: Efficiency, Integrity, and Compliance
*   **Political Fragmentation and Interference:** Public criticism of the judiciary by politicians during the caretaker government period (June 2024 – February 2025) poses a threat to perceived neutrality. Allegations of information leaks and undue political influence in sensitive corruption investigations handled by the Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC) undermine trust.
*   **Compliance with Court Rulings:** Limited progress has been made in ensuring public authorities comply with final rulings. Belgium remains under supervision by the Council of Europe for structural issues identified in *Camara v. Belgium*. As of January 1, 2025, 17 leading judgments of the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) were pending implementation. This non-compliance is a direct negative indicator for **Rule of Law** scores.
*   **Resource and Efficiency Shortages:** Persistent backlogs, particularly in the Court of Appeal of Brussels, and unfilled vacancies due to unattractive working conditions threaten the timely delivery of justice. Poor management of justice digitalization raises fraud risks.
*   **Integrity Framework Gaps:** The system of asset and interest declarations for politicians lacks adequate verification and transparency (sealed declarations). Limited progress on lobbying legislative reform and "revolving door" rules weakens the **Anti-corruption framework**.

### Case Study: France – Reform Amidst Political Pressure

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on France** highlights a complex landscape where judicial independence is structurally supported by recent reforms but operationally tested by political polarization and efficiency challenges.

#### Strengths: Institutional Frameworks and Anti-Corruption Capacity
*   **Magistrate Status Reforms:** The implementation of the 2023 law reforming the status of magistrates aims to enhance judicial independence and diversity. The Superior Council of the Magistracy (CSM) actively defends judicial autonomy, and the President of the Republic has reaffirmed support for the judiciary.
*   **Strong Anti-Corruption Institutions:** France maintains a relatively low corruption perception score. The Anti-Corruption Agency (AFA) has been reorganized, and the National Financial Prosecutor (PNF) is well-capitalized and independent, effectively combating financial crimes. This supports the **Financial Sector Health** and **Policy Credibility** rating factors.
*   **Independent Regulatory Bodies:** High perceived independence of national competition authority and other regulators (ARCOM, HATVP) supports institutional strength.

#### Threats: Political Polarization, Efficiency, and Civic Space
*   **Attacks on Magistrates and Political Pressure:** Despite legal protections, magistrates face severe personal attacks and threats, particularly after rulings on national elections. The Superior Council of the Magistracy reiterated concerns in March 2025 regarding this climate. This erosion of safety and perceived neutrality impacts **Geopolitical and Political Stability**.
*   **Judicial Efficiency and Digitalization Delays:** Progress in digitalizing judicial procedures (Portalis tool) faces implementation delays and technical issues. Case resolution times remain high (354 days for civil/commercial cases; 1,335 days for bribery cases). Inefficiency increases the cost of doing business and legal uncertainty for SMEs, negatively affecting **Economic Strength**.
*   **Lobbying Transparency Gaps:** A significant gap remains in lobbying transparency, as disclosure rules still do not apply to top-executive officials. This lack of transparency at the highest level of government can lead to perceptions of undue influence on judicial and legislative outcomes, weakening **Accountability**.
*   **Narrowing Civic Space:** The civic space is rated as "narrowed," with civil society organizations facing budgetary constraints and intimidation. Civil society acts as a watchdog for judicial integrity; its weakening reduces external oversight on the rule of law.
*   **ECHR Implementation Backlog:** Although the number of pending leading ECHR judgments decreased to 22 by June 2025, historical backlogs remain unresolved, indicating systemic challenges in aligning domestic practice with international human rights standards.

### Case Study: Hungary – Systemic Erosion and Legal Uncertainty

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Hungary** depicts a severe deterioration of judicial independence, posing existential risks to the sovereign credit profile through the breakdown of legal certainty and the expropriation of foreign capital.

#### Strengths: Limited Operational Efficiency
*   **Case Resolution Speed:** Despite systemic flaws, the judiciary maintains high overall efficiency in resolving cases, and digitalization initiatives are ongoing. This superficial metric masks deeper structural deficiencies.

#### Threats: Political Control, Emergency Powers, and Investor Flight
*   **Emergency Powers Overriding Legislation:** The government extensively uses emergency powers to override Acts of Parliament, bypassing judicial review. Decrees of "strategic importance" cannot be challenged in court, creating a zone of state action immune to judicial oversight. This directly undermines **Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength** and increases **Contingent Liabilities** for investors facing arbitrary regulatory changes.
*   **Pressure on Judicial Bodies:** The National Judicial Council (NJC) faced internal turmoil, with its President resigning in December 2024 following protests against reforms that lacked adequate consultation. The NJC subsequently withdrew from a four-party agreement on judicial reforms, signaling a breakdown in institutional self-governance. The *Kúria* (Curia) continues to enforce uniform case law, but undue pressure on judges regarding internal debates persists.
*   **Case Allocation Opacity:** A critical deficiency remains the lack of transparency in case allocation at lower-instance courts. Discretion allowed to court managers and frequent rule changes prevent verification of impartiality, raising concerns about "court shopping" and political interference in high-profile cases. This is a direct threat to **Accountability**.
*   **Judicial Age and Appointment Reforms:** New rules raised the minimum age for judges to 35 and allowed service until age 70, implemented without effective consultation. These changes, coupled with the resignation of the NJC President, have created a "chilling effect" within the judiciary, as noted by the Council of Europe Committee of Ministers in March 2025.
*   **Impact on Foreign Investment:** Foreign companies, particularly from other EU Member States, face intensified regulatory pressure through special taxes, export restrictions, and discriminatory practices. The legal uncertainty arising from the lack of judicial oversight on strategic decrees is a primary driver of de-investment, negatively impacting **Economic Strength and Tax Base** and **Growth Prospects**.

### Case Study: Italy – Institutional Tensions and Reform Challenges

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Italy** presents a scenario where high perceived independence is counterbalanced by structural inefficiencies, legislative opacity, and contentious constitutional reforms.

#### Strengths: Perception and Digitalization
*   **High Public and Corporate Perception:** Perceptions of judicial independence among the general public and companies have increased, reflecting resilience in the judiciary's core mandate. This supports **Stable democratic framework** ratings.
*   **Digital Case Management:** The mandatory use of the digital case management system (*APP*) in criminal courts since January 1, 2025, aims to streamline processes and enhance transparency, although technical challenges have necessitated derogations for some courts.
*   **Anti-Corruption Institutional Strength:** The National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC) has strengthened its human resources and issued new guidelines on "revolving doors" and whistleblowing, supporting the **Anti-corruption strategy** and **Policy Credibility**.

#### Threats: Constitutional Reform, Staff Shortages, and Executive Overreach
*   **Draft Constitutional Reform on Career Separation:** A draft constitutional reform to separate the careers of judges and prosecutors was approved by the Chamber of Deputies in January 2025. However, it faces strong opposition from the High Council for the Judiciary and magistrates’ associations, who argue it undermines judicial autonomy and representation. This dispute introduces **Policymaking Stability** risks and potential fragmentation within the judicial branch.
*   **Persistent Staff Shortages:** Despite recruitment efforts, persistent shortages of administrative staff and magistrates (aiming to close a 17% shortage) threaten the system's capacity. The High Council for the Judiciary approved directives for out-of-tenure appointments to mitigate this, but resource constraints remain a bottleneck for **Judicial Efficiency**.
*   **Executive Overreach and Urgency Decrees:** The government’s frequent resort to urgency decrees (87 in the current legislature) and the use of confidence motions raise concerns about the separation of powers. This practice can bypass parliamentary scrutiny and judicial oversight, affecting **Policy Credibility**.
*   **Legislative Gaps in Transparency:** No concrete progress has been made on establishing an operational lobbying register or addressing the practice of channeling political donations through foundations. Additionally, draft laws on lobbying and political party financing have not advanced. These gaps weaken **Accountability** and increase the risk of undue influence on judicial and legislative processes.
*   **Access to Judicial Information:** Rules regulating the flow of certain judicial information remain a source of concern for stakeholders, potentially hindering media scrutiny and public oversight of judicial conduct.

### Case Study: Malta – Efficiency Gaps and Accountability Concerns

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Malta** illustrates a jurisdiction where perceived judicial independence is high, but functional independence is undermined by procedural inefficiencies, weak final enforcement in corruption cases, and recent regulatory defeats.

#### Strengths: Prosecutorial Centralization and Public Trust
*   **High Perceived Independence:** Judicial independence is perceived as high by both the general public (69%) and companies (63%). This perception supports the **Stable democratic framework** and **Economic Strength and Tax Base** rating factors, with over two-thirds of companies expressing confidence in investment protection.
*   **Prosecutorial Autonomy:** The transfer of prosecutorial powers for serious offenses to the Office of the Attorney General was completed in September 2024. This centralization enhances **[[Prosecutorial autonomy]]** and strengthens the institutional framework for independent prosecution, a key pillar for **Policy Credibility**.
*   **Constitutional Reform Progress:** A constitutional reform tabled in May 2025 aims to involve the judiciary in the appointment of the Chief Justice, reducing executive influence over judicial leadership.

#### Threats: Efficiency, Corruption Enforcement, and Regulatory Risks
*   **Judicial Efficiency Bottlenecks:** Despite opening new courts, excessive length of proceedings remains a critical issue. First-instance civil/commercial cases took 454 days and criminal cases 713 days in 2023. Administrative justice saw a deterioration to 1,350 days. Backlogs increase, with clearance rates falling below intake, directly impacting **Growth Prospects** and increasing the cost of doing business.
*   **Weak Track Record in Corruption Cases:** While investigative bodies have increased resources, there is no robust track record of final judgments in high-level corruption cases. The Permanent Commission Against Corruption (PCAC) has never referred a case for prosecution, citing insufficient resources. This gap undermines the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]** and creates uncertainty for investors regarding the enforcement of fair competition.
*   **Investor Citizenship Scheme Ruling:** On April 29, 2025, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) ruled that Malta’s investor citizenship scheme violates EU Law. This ruling highlights past regulatory weaknesses and potential **Contingent Liabilities** for the state, impacting **Financial Sector Health** and **Policy Credibility**.
*   **Integrity of High-Level Officials:** Ministers' asset declarations remain unpublished, and MPs are not covered by public administration conflict of interest rules. This lack of transparency at the highest level of government weakens **Accountability** and fuels perceptions of corruption, which 51% of companies identify as a problem in doing business.
*   **Accountability Mechanisms:** A controversial reform in April 2025 increased the threshold for citizens to trigger magisterial inquiries, raising concerns about reduced accountability for high-level offenses. This change, combined with a "narrowed" civic space, reduces external oversight on judicial and executive conduct.

### Case Study: Poland – Transitional Reform Amidst Constitutional Crisis

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Poland** outlines a complex environment where the government is actively pursuing reforms to restore judicial independence, yet significant structural and constitutional tensions persist.

#### Strengths: Legislative Engagement and Structural Separation
*   **Reform of Judicial Appointments:** The government is preparing legislation to address the status of approximately 1,600 judges appointed by the post-2017 National Council for the Judiciary (NCJ). A proposed classification system and potential removal process, pending Venice Commission consultation, aim to resolve the legitimacy crisis surrounding these appointments.
*   **Supreme Court Restructuring:** Draft laws propose dismantling the Chamber of Extraordinary Control and Public Affairs within the Supreme Court, a step widely viewed as enhancing judicial independence and aligning with EU standards.
*   **Separation of Prosecutorial Power:** Significant progress has been made in separating the office of the Minister of Justice from that of the Prosecutor General. This draft law, approved by the Cabinet Standing Committee in late 2024, incorporates Venice Commission recommendations and aims to ensure the functional independence of the National Prosecution Service.
*   **Operational Anti-Corruption Bodies:** The European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO) is fully operational in Poland, and a new Central Office for Combating Corruption is being established within the police, replacing the Central Anti-Corruption Bureau. This strengthens the capacity for investigating cross-border and high-level corruption.

#### Threats: Constitutional Crisis and Governance Volatility
*   **Constitutional Tribunal Legitimacy Dispute:** The government continues to refuse to publish judgments of the Constitutional Tribunal, asserting the body lacks legitimacy due to past appointments. Legislation addressing the NCJ and Constitutional Tribunal has been adopted by Parliament but remains pending before the Tribunal itself due to a presidential referral. This stalemate creates a constitutional vacuum and severe **legal uncertainty** for investors.
*   **Low Investment Confidence:** Corporate confidence in investment protection is low due to legislative instability and the perceived politicization of justice. This directly negatively impacts **Growth Prospects** and increases the country risk premium.
*   **Accountability Gaps:** Broad immunities for top executives and the absence of a comprehensive anti-corruption strategy or lobbying rules hinder effective accountability. Impunity clauses in legislation and limited transparency in political party financing further weaken **Accountability**.
*   **ECHR Compliance Backlog:** As of June 2025, 54 leading judgments of the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) were pending implementation, highlighting systemic challenges in aligning domestic judicial practices with international human rights standards. This non-compliance poses a risk to **Policy Credibility** and potential financial penalties via EU mechanisms.
*   **Media and Civic Space Pressures:** While civic space has improved from "obstructed" to "narrowed," negative rhetoric against NGOs and operational challenges persist. The independence of the media regulator (KRRiT) and governance of public broadcasters remain concerns, reducing external oversight on judicial and executive actions.

### Case Study: Romania – Structural Progress vs. Case-Law Risks

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Romania** highlights a dichotomy where significant progress in implementing Venice Commission recommendations on justice laws is offset by severe operational risks arising from controversial judicial interpretations of the statute of limitations and declining public trust.

#### Strengths: Institutional Reform and Strategic Alignment
*   **Venice Commission Recommendations:** Romania has completed the process to take into account Venice Commission recommendations on the Justice Laws. This structural alignment enhances the formal framework for **[[Institutional Strength]]** and supports **Policy Credibility** by demonstrating a commitment to EU rule of law standards.
*   **IT System Independence:** The Ministry of Justice has shown openness to ensuring judicial independence in the reform of the IT system for courts, addressing previous concerns about centralized data access and potential executive interference.
*   **Anti-Corruption Strategy Implementation:** The implementation of the 2021-2025 National Anti-Corruption Strategy is on track. The National Anti-Corruption Directorate (DNA) maintains a stable occupancy rate and continues to secure convictions in high-level cases, supporting the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]** pillar of the credit profile.
*   **Prosecutorial Stability:** Legislative steps are being explored to extend the mandates of high-ranking prosecutors from three to five years, aiming to reduce political vulnerability and enhance long-term planning capacity.

#### Threats: Statute of Limitations, Perception Decline, and Transparency Gaps
*   **Statute of Limitations and *Lex Mitior*:** A critical threat to judicial independence and anti-corruption efficacy stems from the High Court of Cassation and Justice’s (HCCJ) interpretation of *lex mitior*. This case-law has led to the discontinuation of numerous corruption cases and the annulment of convictions due to expired limitation periods, extending beyond the 2018-2022 legal void. This creates a systemic risk of impunity for high-level officials, undermining **Policy Credibility** and increasing **Contingent Liabilities**. The HCCJ has referred a question to the CJEU (Case C-280/25 Lin II), but the interim uncertainty damages investor confidence in legal finality.
*   **Declining Perceived Independence:** Despite structural reforms, the perception of judicial independence has decreased among both the general public and companies. This erosion of trust impacts the **Stable democratic framework** rating factor and signals potential operational friction that may affect **Economic Strength**.
*   **Weakened Asset Declaration System:** A Constitutional Court ruling in May 2025 deemed the requirement to declare spouses’ and children’s assets unconstitutional. This significantly weakens the National Integrity Agency’s (ANI) asset declaration system, reducing transparency and accountability for high-level officials. This gap in **[[Accountability]]** increases the risk of undisclosed conflicts of interest, negatively impacting **Policy Credibility**.
*   **Legislative Unpredictability:** The frequent use of Government Emergency Ordinances (GEOs) (199 adopted in 2024) contributes to legislative unpredictability, which is cited as a primary concern for businesses. This volatility affects **Policymaking Stability** and deters long-term investment, as legal frameworks become unstable and difficult to navigate.
*   **Transparency and Lobbying Gaps:** No progress has been made on introducing lobbying rules for Members of Parliament, and legislation to improve transparency in political party financing remains pending. This lack of transparency at the legislative level increases the risk of undue influence on judicial and legislative outcomes, weakening the overall rule of law ecosystem.

### Case Study: Slovakia – Low Perception and Structural Vulnerabilities

The **2025 Rule of Law Report on Slovakia** highlights a jurisdiction with low perceived judicial independence and significant structural weaknesses that pose risks to **Policy Credibility** and **Growth Prospects**.

#### Strengths: Legislative Alignment
*   **Alignment with EU Directives:** Slovakia made legislative amendments to align its criminal codes with the EU Directive on the Protection of the Union’s Financial Interests (PIF), including additional adjustments in December 2024. This supports **Policy Credibility** by demonstrating adherence to EU legal standards.
*   **Whistleblower Protection Enforcement:** The Office for the Protection of Whistleblowers fined the Ministry of Interior in October 2024 for procedural violations, indicating some functional capacity within oversight mechanisms.

#### Threats: Judicial Governance, Prosecutorial Power, and Anti-Corruption Fragmentation
*   **Low Perceived Independence:** Judicial independence is perceived as low (32% among the public, 34% among companies in 2025). This low confidence directly impacts the **Stable democratic framework** rating factor and signals higher political risk for investors.
*   **Weak Safeguards for Judicial Council:** No progress was made in establishing sufficient safeguards for the dismissal of Judicial Council members, particularly those not elected by judges. This raises concerns about the independence of judicial self-governance and potential executive influence over judicial appointments and discipline.
*   **Prosecutorial Interference:** The Prosecutor General retains broad powers to annul final decisions and interfere in high-level corruption cases. This undermines the finality of judicial decisions and creates **legal uncertainty** regarding the enforcement of anti-corruption measures, a key component of **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**.
*   **Dissolution of Anti-Corruption Entities:** The dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities, including the National Crime Agency (NAKA) and the Special Prosecutor’s Office (SPO), has led to a drop in investigated corruption cases and operational inefficiencies. The lack of specialized investigators weakens the state’s capacity to enforce laws against high-level corruption, increasing **Contingent Liabilities** and reputational risk.
*   **Inefficiency in Administrative Justice:** The justice system faces efficiency challenges, particularly in administrative cases where resolution time rose sharply to 1,040 days in 2023. Lengthy proceedings hinder business permits and increase the cost of doing business, negatively affecting **Growth Prospects**.
*   **Pending ECtHR Judgments:** Slovakia had 33 pending leading judgments from the European Court of Human Rights as of June 2025, indicating weak enforcement of international judicial decisions and raising risks related to **Policy Credibility** and potential financial penalties.

*   **Hungarian National Judicial Council (NJC):** Key self-governing body for the judiciary. Its ability to function independently was severely tested in 2024-2025, leading to the resignation of its President and withdrawal from reform agreements, highlighting the fragility of institutional checks in Hungary.
*   **Hungarian Kúria (Curia):** The supreme court in Hungary. While it enforces uniform case law, it operates under a system where lower court case allocation is opaque and emergency decrees limit judicial review scope.
*   **Italian High Council for the Judiciary (CSM):** The primary self-governing body for the Italian judiciary. It has taken forward tasks under the comprehensive justice reform legislation but issued a critical opinion on the draft constitutional reform separating judicial and prosecutorial careers, highlighting tensions between executive ambition and judicial autonomy.
*   **Italian National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC):** Strengthened resources and updated the National Anti-Corruption Plan, playing a key role in enforcing integrity standards, though legislative gaps in lobbying transparency limit its broader impact.
*   **Malta Office of the Attorney General:** Received prosecutorial powers for serious offenses in 2024, centralizing authority and enhancing independence from executive influence in serious crime prosecution.
*   **Malta Permanent Commission Against Corruption (PCAC):** An anti-corruption body with limited operational capacity and no record of referring cases for prosecution, highlighting a gap in effective oversight mechanisms.
*   **Polish National Council for the Judiciary (NCJ):** The body responsible for safeguarding judicial independence. Its post-2017 composition has been the subject of intense dispute and CJEU rulings, leading to a legitimacy crisis that the current government is attempting to resolve through new classification and removal legislation for appointed judges.
*   **Polish Constitutional Tribunal:** The highest judicial body in Poland. Its legitimacy is contested by the government, which refuses to publish its judgments, creating a constitutional crisis that undermines the finality of judicial decisions and rule of law stability.
*   **Polish Supreme Court:** Undergoing structural reforms, including the proposed dismantling of the Chamber of Extraordinary Control and Public Affairs, to enhance independence and align with EU standards.
*   **Polish Ministry of Justice:** Has taken steps to separate the role of the Minister from the Prosecutor General, a key recommendation for ensuring prosecutorial independence. However, it remains central to the ongoing constitutional disputes.
*   **Romanian High Court of Cassation and Justice (HCCJ):** The highest judicial body in Romania. Its recent case-law on *lex mitior* and the statute of limitations has raised significant concerns regarding impunity and EU law compatibility, posing a major risk to **Policy Credibility**.
*   **Romanian National Anti-Corruption Directorate (DNA):** Maintains a positive record in prosecuting high-level corruption and stable occupancy rates, supporting the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**. However, its effectiveness is threatened by the HCCJ’s interpretations of limitation periods.
*   **Romanian National Integrity Agency (ANI):** Responsible for asset declarations. Its oversight capacity was weakened by a 2025 Constitutional Court ruling that deemed the declaration of spouses’ and children’s assets unconstitutional, creating a transparency gap.
*   **Romanian Ministry of Justice:** Demonstrated openness to ensuring judicial independence in IT system reforms, a positive signal for **Institutional Strength**.
*   **Slovak Judicial Council:** The key self-governing body for the judiciary in Slovakia. Its independence is weakened by the lack of sufficient safeguards for the dismissal of its members, raising concerns about potential executive influence.
*   **Slovak Prosecutor General’s Office:** Retains broad powers to annul final decisions and interfere in high-level corruption cases, undermining judicial finality and **[[Prosecutorial autonomy]]**.
*   **Slovak National Crime Agency (NAKA):** A specialized anti-corruption entity that was dissolved, leading to a drop in investigated corruption cases and operational inefficiencies, weakening the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**.
*   **Slovak Special Prosecutor’s Office (SPO):** Another specialized anti-corruption entity dissolved, further fragmenting the anti-corruption framework and reducing oversight capacity.
*   **European Commission:** Through the Rule of Law Mechanism, it monitors and reports on judicial independence, linking it directly to the disbursement of funds and overall rule of law assessment.
*   **Council of Europe:** Highlighted the need for independent prosecutorial structures free from political influence (Austria) and supervises compliance with ECtHR rulings (Belgium, France). In Hungary, it addressed the "chilling effect" on judges. In Italy, it registered alerts concerning journalist safety and potential spyware use, impacting the broader civic environment supporting judicial independence. In Poland, it continues to assess the compatibility of judicial reforms with European standards. In Romania, it provided recommendations on Justice Laws that have been implemented. In Slovakia, it monitors compliance with ECtHR judgments.
*   **Ministry of Justice (Austria):** Responsible for implementing reforms but currently retains significant control over prosecutorial instructions, a point of contention for independence advocates.
*   **High Council for Justice (Belgium):** Monitors judicial efficiency and resource allocation, publishing reports on backlogs and recommending digitalization improvements.
*   **Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC) (Belgium):** Key anti-corruption body facing scrutiny over potential political interference and information leaks.
*   **French High Authority for Transparency in Public Life (HATVP):** Increased staff to handle asset declarations; however, gaps remain in lobbying disclosure for top-executive officials.
*   **Hungarian Integrity Authority:** Faces obstacles in accessing data and fulfilling oversight tasks, with a weak asset declaration system lacking systematic follow-up for non-compliance.
*   **Sovereignty Protection Office (Hungary):** A body launched to investigate alleged "foreign interests," which has targeted journalists and civil society organizations, indirectly pressuring the judicial and civic environment.
*   **Malta Data Protection and Information Commissioner:** Provides a reliable review mechanism for access to official documents, supporting transparency.
*   **Malta Public Service Commission:** Involved in appointments and integrity measures, though faced with criticism regarding coverage of high-level politicians.
*   **Venice Commission:** Provided critical recommendations on Justice Laws in Romania, which have been implemented, serving as a benchmark for judicial independence reforms.
*   **National Audiovisual Council (Romania):** Lacks sufficient human resources and an updated IT system, impacting its effectiveness in monitoring media independence, which acts as a secondary check on judicial and executive actions.

## Interrelated Concepts

*   **[[Prosecutorial autonomy]]**: The ability of prosecutors to act without political interference is closely linked to judicial independence. In Austria, the lack of progress here is a specific threat to the rule of law score. In Belgium, concerns over leaks in high-profile cases handled by CDBC-OCRC reflect similar risks. In France, the PNF is viewed as a strong, independent actor, though broader lobbying gaps affect overall integrity. In Hungary, the limited accountability of the prosecution service and potential political influence are key threats to policy credibility. In **Italy**, the proposed separation of judicial and prosecutorial careers aims to redefine this autonomy but risks creating institutional friction and perceptions of reduced judicial leverage. In **Malta**, the completion of the transfer of prosecutorial powers to the Office of the Attorney General is a significant strength, enhancing **[[Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength]]** by centralizing authority and reducing executive fragmentation in serious crime prosecution. In **Poland**, the significant progress in separating the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General is a crucial step toward restoring prosecutorial independence, directly addressing long-standing EU concerns and supporting **Policy Credibility**. In **Romania**, legislative steps to extend prosecutor mandates and safeguard high-ranking prosecutors support autonomy, but the broader judicial environment is strained by controversial case-law interpretations. In **Slovakia**, the Prosecutor General retains broad powers to annul final decisions and interfere in high-level corruption cases, significantly undermining **[[Prosecutorial autonomy]]** and creating legal uncertainty for investors.
*   **[[Checks and balances]]**: Judicial independence is a core check on executive power. Weaknesses in appointment procedures for court presidents (Austria), non-compliance with court rulings by the executive (Belgium), or the use of accelerated legislative procedures bypassing consultation (France) weaken this balance. In Hungary, the extensive use of emergency powers to override parliamentary legislation represents a near-total suspension of this balance. In **Italy**, the frequent use of urgency decrees and confidence motions by the government strains the separation of powers, potentially allowing executive overreach that bypasses standard legislative and judicial scrutiny. In **Malta**, the increase in the threshold for magisterial inquiries and the lack of formal public participation frameworks weaken citizen-led checks on executive power, while the unpublished asset declarations of ministers reduce financial accountability. In **Poland**, the constitutional crisis regarding the Tribunal's legitimacy and the government's refusal to publish its judgments effectively suspend the check of constitutional review on executive action, severely undermining **Checks and balances** and increasing **Policy Credibility** risks. In **Romania**, the frequent use of Government Emergency Ordinances (GEOs) weakens legislative scrutiny and judicial oversight, contributing to legislative unpredictability that harms **Policymaking Stability**. In **Slovakia**, the frequent use of fast-track legislative procedures (28% of laws in 2024) and limited stakeholder involvement weaken legislative scrutiny and democratic accountability, further straining the balance of power.
*   **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**: An independent judiciary is essential for effective anti-corruption enforcement. The politicization of prosecutorial instructions (Austria), opaque asset declaration systems (Belgium), and incomplete lobbying transparency (France) undermine national anti-corruption efforts. In Hungary, the high perception of corruption (CPI 41/100) and the lack of convictions in high-level cases are exacerbated by the Integrity Authority's inability to function effectively and the judiciary's lack of independence. In **Italy**, ANAC’s strengthened role is positive, but the lack of progress on lobbying registers and political financing transparency leaves gaps in the anti-corruption framework. In **Malta**, despite high perceived independence, the weakness of the PCAC and the lack of final judgments in high-level corruption cases undermine the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**, contributing to a CPI score of 46/100 and affecting **Growth Prospects** due to perceived risks in public procurement and business conduct. In **Poland**, while the EPPO is operational and a new Central Office for Combating Corruption is being established, the absence of a comprehensive anti-corruption strategy and broad executive immunities hinder high-level accountability. Corruption risks in public procurement, characterized by high single-bid rates and lack of lobbying transparency, continue to threaten **Economic Strength** and investor confidence. In **Romania**, while the DNA maintains a strong track record, the HCCJ’s *lex mitior* rulings have led to the annulment of convictions, creating a risk of impunity that severely undermines the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]** and increases **Contingent Liabilities**. In **Slovakia**, the dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities (NAKA, SPO) has led to a drop in investigated corruption cases and operational inefficiencies, significantly weakening the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]** and increasing reputational risk.
*   **[[Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength]]**: Austria's structural gaps, Belgium's compliance issues, France's political pressure on magistrates, and Hungary's systemic erosion of legal oversight directly impact this sovereign credit rating factor. Hungary's specific risks include the arbitrary use of state power to target foreign investors, which destroys investor confidence. In **Italy**, the debate over constitutional reforms and the reliance on urgency decrees introduce uncertainty into the policymaking process, potentially affecting long-term **Policy Credibility**. In **Malta**, the CJEU ruling on the investor citizenship scheme and the weak track record of corruption prosecutions introduce specific risks to **Financial Sector Health** and **Policy Credibility**, as they signal potential regulatory failures and enforcement gaps. In **Poland**, the constitutional crisis and the legitimacy dispute over the Constitutional Tribunal and NCJ are the primary drivers of **Policy Credibility** risk. The low corporate confidence in investment protection due to legislative instability and the pending nature of key judicial reforms mean that Poland’s institutional strength is currently fragile, requiring successful implementation of the Action Plan on the Rule of Law to restore full creditworthiness. In **Romania**, the weakening of the asset declaration system and the uncertainty surrounding the statute of limitations case-law pose significant risks to **Policy Credibility**, as they suggest that legal frameworks may not be applied consistently or fairly, deterring long-term investment. In **Slovakia**, low perceived judicial independence, weak safeguards for judicial council members, and the broad powers of the Prosecutor General undermine **Policy Credibility** and **Institutional Strength**, signaling higher political risk and legal uncertainty for investors.
*   **[[Media pluralism]]**: While distinct, media independence acts as a secondary check on judicial and executive actions. In Belgium and France, debates over public broadcasting governance and journalist safety highlight the fragility of this check. In Hungary, the state's control over media and the targeting of journalists further isolates the judiciary from public scrutiny and accountability. In **Italy**, concerns over RAI’s governance independence, restrictions on access to judicial information, and allegations of spyware targeting journalists (Paragon) weaken the external oversight necessary for maintaining judicial integrity. In **Malta**, the lack of progress on independent governance of public service media and pending legislation on journalist safety create a "very high risk" environment for political bias, reducing media's ability to act as a watchdog for judicial and executive accountability. In **Poland**, while politically biased content in public service media has decreased, concerns persist regarding the independence of the media regulator (KRRiT) and the governance of public broadcasters (currently in liquidation). The planned media law reform aims to align with the European Media Freedom Act, which is critical for restoring independent oversight of judicial proceedings. In **Romania**, untransparent political advertising and financing of private media by political parties, along with harassment of journalists, undermine media freedom, reducing external oversight on judicial independence and increasing risks to **Geopolitical and Political Stability**. In **Slovakia**, media freedom remains under pressure, with concerns over the politicization of public service media (STVR governance structure) and a hostile environment for journalists, further reducing external oversight on judicial and executive conduct.
*   **[[Civic space]]**: In France, the "narrowed" civic space and budgetary constraints on civil society reduce the external monitoring of judicial conduct. In Hungary, the environment for civil society organizations has deteriorated, with investigations by the Sovereignty Protection Office and proposed laws restricting foreign funding, effectively removing external watchdogs on judicial and executive conduct. In **Italy**, the adoption of the new Security Law in June 2025, which increases penalties for protest-related activities, raises concerns about the constriction of civic space, potentially reducing civil society's ability to advocate for judicial reforms and transparency. In **Malta**, civil society space is assessed as "narrowed," with draft regulatory frameworks for NGOs under preparation and increased thresholds for magisterial inquiries limiting citizen-led accountability mechanisms. In **Poland**, civic space has improved from "obstructed" to "narrowed" in 2025, with new government working groups and NGO programs. However, negative rhetoric and operational restrictions persist, limiting civil society's capacity to effectively monitor judicial independence and corruption. In **Romania**, civil society space is considered "narrowed," with CSOs facing administrative barriers and attacks. The stalled accreditation of National Human Rights Institutions and budget cuts to the Romanian Institute for Human Rights further weaken the civic infrastructure necessary for overseeing judicial independence. In **Slovakia**, civil society faces increased pressure through new reporting obligations for NGOs and reduced funding for some human rights advocates, weakening the civic infrastructure necessary for external oversight of judicial independence.
*   **[[Case Allocation Transparency]]**: A specific technical aspect of judicial independence. While Austria and France focus on appointment and procedural fairness, Hungary faces a critical deficiency here. The lack of transparent case allocation in lower courts allows for potential manipulation of which judge hears which case, undermining impartiality even if the judges themselves are technically independent. In **Italy**, while digitalization aims to improve transparency, persistent staff shortages and out-of-tenure appointments may complicate the consistent and transparent allocation of cases. In **Malta**, while not explicitly cited as a case allocation issue, the severe backlogs and excessive length of proceedings suggest systemic inefficiencies that may obscure transparent case management. In **Poland**, the legitimacy of judges appointed by the disputed NCJ raises broader concerns about the impartiality of case outcomes, as the selection process itself lacked transparent, independent judicial input. In **Romania**, while IT system reforms aim to enhance independence, the lack of specific details on case allocation transparency in the summary suggests it may remain a latent area of concern for **Accountability**. In **Slovakia**, amendments to judges' laws introduced bonuses and evaluation changes, raising concerns about potential influence on case handling, though some efficiency measures were welcomed.
*   **[[Judicial efficiency]]**: While distinct from independence, efficiency is a prerequisite for practical independence. Backlogs in Belgium, France, and Italy create pressure that can be exploited for political interference or lead to "denial of justice." High efficiency in Austria and Hungary (superficially) supports stability, but if coupled with lack of independence (Hungary), it becomes a tool for arbitrary state action rather than fair dispute resolution. In **Malta**, excessive length of proceedings (454 days for civil cases, 713 for criminal) is a critical threat to **Economic Strength** and **Growth Prospects**, as delays undermine legal certainty and deter investment, even in the absence of overt political interference. In **Poland**, digitalization of court procedures is a strength, but the broader constitutional uncertainty and potential for arbitrary judicial changes can negate efficiency gains by creating unpredictable legal environments for businesses. In **Romania**, while not highlighted as a primary efficiency crisis, the frequent use of GEOs and legislative unpredictability create operational inefficiencies for businesses, impacting **Growth Prospects**. In **Slovakia**, the justice system faces efficiency challenges, particularly in administrative cases where resolution time rose sharply to 1,040 days in 2023, hindering business permits and increasing the cost of doing business, which negatively impacts **Growth Prospects**.

## Conclusion

For sovereign credit analysis, judicial independence in Austria, Belgium, France, Hungary, Italy, Malta, Poland, **Romania**, and **Slovakia** presents distinct and increasingly polarized risk profiles. **Austria** benefits from high public trust and procedural efficiency, but its sovereign credit profile is exposed to structural risks regarding **judicial appointment reforms** and **prosecutorial independence**. These gaps threaten the long-term **Policy credibility** required for maintaining top-tier ratings.

**Belgium**, while protecting the judiciary from budgetary cuts and maintaining high corporate confidence in investment protection, faces material risks related to **compliance with court rulings** and **efficiency backlogs**. The lack of progress in implementing ECtHR judgments and the opaque nature of political integrity frameworks (asset declarations, lobbying) introduce governance volatility. Credit analysts must weigh Belgium's strong institutional safeguards against its operational deficiencies in executing judicial mandates, as non-compliance undermines the core promise of the rule of law to investors.

**France** presents a scenario of strong institutional frameworks being tested by **political polarization** and **efficiency bottlenecks**. While the structural independence of the judiciary is being reinforced through magistrate status reforms and strong anti-corruption bodies like the PNF, the practical exercise of independence is threatened by attacks on magistrates and political pressure. Additionally, significant delays in case resolution and gaps in lobbying transparency for top executives create legal uncertainty for businesses. For credit analysts, France’s profile is characterized by high baseline institutional strength offset by emerging risks in **political stability** and **accountability**.

**Hungary** represents a severe deviation from EU norms, where judicial independence is systematically undermined by **emergency powers**, **opaque case allocation**, and **political pressure on judicial bodies**. The inability of courts to review decrees of "strategic importance" and the hostile environment for civil society and foreign investors create profound **legal uncertainty**. For sovereign credit purposes, Hungary’s judicial deficiencies are not merely governance issues but direct drivers of **economic weakness**, as they facilitate the expropriation of foreign assets and deter investment. The erosion of **[[Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength]]** in Hungary is total in key sectors, leading to a significant premium in country risk and limiting access to favorable capital markets.

**Italy** offers a nuanced case where high perceived independence masks underlying structural and political tensions. The judiciary benefits from digitalization efforts and strong anti-corruption institutions like ANAC. However, the **draft constitutional reform** separating judicial and prosecutorial careers introduces institutional friction and uncertainty regarding judicial autonomy. Combined with the government’s frequent use of **urgency decrees** and stalled progress on **lobbying transparency**, Italy’s profile carries risks related to **Policymaking Stability** and **Accountability**. While not facing the systemic erosion seen in Hungary, Italy’s governance volatility requires careful monitoring, as the balance between executive power and judicial independence remains contested and potentially fragile.

**Malta** presents a unique challenge where high perceived independence and successful prosecutorial centralization are undermined by **judicial inefficiency** and **weak enforcement of anti-corruption measures**. The excessive length of proceedings creates legal uncertainty that hampers **Economic Strength** and **Growth Prospects**. Furthermore, the recent CJEU ruling against the investor citizenship scheme and the lack of final judgments in high-level corruption cases highlight vulnerabilities in **Financial Sector Health** and **Policy Credibility**. For credit analysts, Malta’s profile requires monitoring of whether constitutional reforms to enhance judicial appointments can translate into tangible improvements in case resolution speeds and corruption enforcement, thereby restoring full confidence in the legal framework's integrity and efficiency.

**Poland** stands out for its active engagement in rule of law reforms amidst a persistent constitutional crisis. The government’s efforts to address the status of NCJ-appointed judges, dismantle the Supreme Court Chamber of Extraordinary Control, and separate the Prosecutor General from the Ministry of Justice are positive steps toward restoring **Institutional Strength**. However, the refusal to publish Constitutional Tribunal judgments and the ongoing legitimacy dispute create severe **legal uncertainty** and low corporate confidence in investment protection. For credit analysts, Poland’s trajectory hinges on the successful implementation of these reforms and the resolution of the constitutional stalemate. Until then, the country faces elevated **Policy Credibility** risks and higher country risk premiums due to the fragility of its judicial framework and the associated implications for **Growth Prospects** and **Accountability**.

**Romania** presents a mixed profile where structural progress in implementing Venice Commission recommendations is undermined by severe operational risks. The **High Court of Cassation and Justice’s** interpretation of *lex mitior* has led to the discontinuation of corruption cases, creating a risk of impunity that threatens **Policy Credibility** and increases **Contingent Liabilities**. Additionally, the weakening of the asset declaration system and frequent use of Government Emergency Ordinances contribute to legislative unpredictability and reduced transparency. For credit analysts, Romania’s profile requires close monitoring of the CJEU’s ruling on the statute of limitations and the government’s ability to maintain judicial independence amidst these case-law challenges, as these factors directly impact **Institutional Strength** and investor confidence in legal finality.

**Slovakia** adds a new dimension of risk characterized by **low perceived judicial independence** (32% public, 34% corporate) and **structural vulnerabilities** in judicial governance. The lack of safeguards for Judicial Council members and the broad powers of the Prosecutor General to annul final decisions create significant **legal uncertainty** and undermine **Policy Credibility**. The dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities has weakened the **[[Anti-corruption strategy]]**, leading to a drop in investigated cases and operational inefficiencies. For credit analysts, Slovakia’s profile signals higher political risk and legal uncertainty, particularly for investors sensitive to rule of law standards. The poor business climate perception and lengthy administrative proceedings further detract from **Growth Prospects**, suggesting that improvements in judicial independence and anti-corruption enforcement are critical for restoring investor confidence and stabilizing the sovereign credit outlook.
