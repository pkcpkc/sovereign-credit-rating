# Constitutional Court

The **Constitutional Court** in Hungary serves as the primary judicial body for constitutional review, yet its institutional integrity has become a critical variable in sovereign risk assessments and country risk analysis. According to the 2025 Rule of Law Report issued by the European Commission, the Court is characterized as "politicized" and lacking functional independence, which directly impacts **Legal Certainty** and investment protection confidence.

## Judicial Independence and Case Allocation

A central concern highlighted in recent assessments is the lack of transparent case allocation mechanisms within the Constitutional Court. The absence of clear, objective criteria for assigning cases creates an environment susceptible to political influence, undermining the predictability required for stable economic governance. This politicization affects the Court's ability to act as an impartial check on executive power, a function essential for maintaining **Judicial Independence**.

The erosion of independence is further exacerbated by structural reforms, including the Fourteenth Amendment to the Fundamental Law, which altered judicial appointment criteria and extended retirement ages. These changes, combined with undue pressure on judges and public criticism from the President of the **Kúria** (Supreme Court), signal a systemic weakening of the judiciary's autonomy. For sovereign credit rating agencies, this represents a significant **Institutional and Governance** threat, as it suggests that legal remedies for disputes involving state interests may be compromised.

## Interaction with Emergency Powers and Legal Certainty

The Constitutional Court's role is increasingly constrained by the government's extensive use of emergency powers, specifically the "state of danger." The extension of this state until November 2025 has allowed the government to override parliamentary acts and adopt emergency decrees that bypass standard legislative scrutiny. While the Fifteenth Amendment (effective 2026) introduces a requirement for parliamentary authorization to extend the state of danger, critics argue it does not fully resolve the legal uncertainty created by current emergency decrees.

This dynamic creates a conflict where the Constitutional Court's traditional role in reviewing the constitutionality of legislation is undermined by the political realities of emergency governance. The inability to effectively check executive overreach during a "state of danger" contributes to **Legal Uncertainty**, a key factor in determining a country's creditworthiness and attractiveness for foreign direct investment.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit and Financial Stability

The status of the Constitutional Court directly influences several factors in sovereign credit rating models:

1.  **Institutional and Governance Strength**: The politicization of the Court is listed as a primary threat. A judiciary that lacks independence cannot reliably enforce contracts or protect property rights, leading to higher risk premiums for sovereign debt.
2.  **Policy Predictability and Reform Continuity**: The Court's inability to provide a robust check on frequent legislative changes and emergency decrees reduces policy predictability. This volatility affects **Fiscal Sustainability** by creating an unpredictable regulatory environment for state finances and public procurement.
3.  **Contingent Liabilities**: There are risks of state liability arising from non-compliance with EU court rulings, a situation exacerbated if the Constitutional Court fails to align national law with European Union Law. This can lead to financial penalties and suspension of EU funds, impacting **External Position and Liquidity**.
4.  **Investment Protection**: The lack of functional independence in the broader judicial system, including the Constitutional Court, hinders **Investment Protection Confidence**. Foreign investors face heightened risks due to potential political interference in legal disputes, contributing to de-investment patterns in strategic sectors.

## Conclusion

The Constitutional Court's current operational framework in Hungary, marked by politicization and lack of case allocation transparency, poses a significant risk to the rule of law. Its compromised independence undermines the checks and balances necessary for fiscal discipline and legal certainty, directly impacting sovereign credit ratings and macroeconomic stability. Monitoring the Court's evolving role in response to emergency powers and judicial reforms is essential for accurate country risk analysis.
