# ageing costs

**Ageing costs** refer to the fiscal pressure exerted on public finances by an aging population, primarily manifested through rising expenditures on [[pensions]] and [[long-term care]]. In the context of sovereign credit analysis and macroeconomic policy, ageing costs represent a structural drag on fiscal sustainability, increasing primary deficits and limiting the state's ability to absorb economic shocks or invest in growth-enhancing reforms.

## Key Characteristics and Drivers

Ageing costs are driven by demographic shifts (increasing life expectancy, declining fertility rates) and the design of social protection systems. Key components include:

*   **Pension Expenditure:** Direct social transfers to retirees, which are often index-linked to wages or prices, creating automatic stabilizers that expand deficits during inflationary periods.
*   **Long-Term Care (LTC):** Healthcare and social support for the elderly, which requires significant upfront infrastructure investment and recurrent operational funding.
*   **Labor Market Impacts:** Aging populations can reduce labor force participation if not managed through active labor market policies, indirectly affecting the tax base required to fund these costs.

## Case Study: Belgium (2025 Assessment)

The [[Belgium]] 2025 Country Report highlights ageing costs as a critical threat to [[fiscal sustainability]] and [[debt sustainability]]. Belgium faces one of the steepest projected increases in ageing-related spending in the European Union.

### Projected Fiscal Impact
According to Commission ageing reports cited in the 2025 surveillance framework:
*   **Total Increase:** By 2070, age-related expenditure in Belgium is projected to rise by **5.1 percentage points of GDP**, significantly higher than the EU average increase of 1.4 percentage points.
*   **Pensions:** Projected to increase by **3.5% of GDP** by 2070. This is the primary driver of the fiscal burden, exacerbated by historical assimilated periods and early retirement options.
*   **Long-Term Care:** Projected to increase by **1.7% of GDP** by 2070, reflecting the growing demand for care services among the elderly population.

### Impact on Sovereign Credit and Fiscal Policy
The structural nature of these costs severely constrains Belgium's fiscal space:
1.  **Debt Trajectory:** Rising ageing-related costs are a primary driver of the projected public debt surge from 104.7% of GDP (2024) to **126.4% by 2035**. This trajectory undermines [[debt_payment_culture]] and increases refinancing risks.
2.  **Primary Deficits:** The gap between rising rigid expenditures (pensions/LTC) and a stable revenue base (~50% of GDP) leads to persistent primary deficits. This was evident in the widening general government deficit to 4.5% of GDP in 2024 and projected 5.4% in 2025.
3.  **Policy Response:** The Belgian government's [[Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan]] (2025–2029) includes specific structural reforms to mitigate these costs, such as:
    *   Implementation of a **bonus-malus pension system** to encourage later retirement.
    *   Reduction of **assimilated periods** (periods counted towards pension rights without contributions) to improve actuarial fairness.
    *   However, the effectiveness of these measures is hampered by [[institutional fragmentation]] between federal and regional entities, which complicates coordinated budgetary monitoring.

## Implications for Country Risk Analysis

For sovereign credit rating agencies and investors, ageing costs serve as a key indicator of long-term fiscal health:

*   **Fiscal Flexibility:** High ageing costs reduce the discretionary fiscal space available for counter-cyclical stimulus during downturns or for strategic investments (e.g., [[energy transition]], defense).
*   **Structural Reform Credibility:** Markets closely monitor the implementation of pension and LTC reforms. Delays or watered-down reforms (as seen in past Belgian coalition negotiations) signal higher future default risks.
*   **Competitiveness Link:** To fund ageing costs without raising distortionary taxes, countries must boost [[productivity growth]]. In Belgium, weak productivity growth and high [[tax-wedge on labour]] create a vicious cycle where the cost of living rises (via indexation) without corresponding real income growth, further straining the pension system.
