# sovereign credit risk

**Sovereign credit risk** refers to the risk that a sovereign government (issuer) or monetary authority will fail to meet its financial obligations to nonofficial (commercial) creditors. This risk encompasses both the ability and the willingness of the sovereign to service its debt. The assessment of this risk is formalized through **Sovereign Credit Rating** methodologies, such as those published by **S&P Global Ratings**, which evaluate a country's creditworthiness based on a comprehensive framework of institutional, economic, external, fiscal, and monetary factors.

## Concept Definition and Scope

Sovereign credit risk is distinct from corporate credit risk due to the unique powers and constraints of sovereign entities. While a sovereign has the power to print money (in its local currency) and collect taxes, it faces constraints such as political limits on taxation, external exchange rate risks, and the potential for financial repression or default.

The core components of sovereign credit risk analysis include:
- **Default Risk**: The probability of a sovereign failing to make scheduled principal or interest payments.
- **Downgrade Risk**: The likelihood of a reduction in credit quality, leading to higher borrowing costs.
- **Contingent Liabilities**: Potential future obligations arising from the banking sector, pension systems, or state-owned enterprises.

## S&P Global Ratings Sovereign Rating Methodology (2017)

The primary framework for assessing sovereign credit risk, as defined by **S&P Global Ratings** in its 2017 methodology (effective December 18, 2017), utilizes a five-pillar assessment model. This methodology replaced the 2014 version and integrated the 2013 Monetary Authorities methodology to provide a standardized approach to rating sovereigns and monetary unions.

### The Five-Pillar Assessment Framework

The indicative rating level is determined by scoring five key areas on a scale of 1 (strongest) to 6 (weakest). These pillars are:

1. **Institutional Assessment**
   - Evaluates the effectiveness of policymaking, transparency, and the **debt payment culture**.
   - A sovereign with a weak debt payment culture (e.g., sustained arrears, odious debt discourse) receives the weakest assessment ('6').
   - Adjusts for **external security risks** (e.g., war), which can lower the assessment by one or two categories if the risk is not imminent within two to three years.

2. **Economic Assessment**
   - Focuses on GDP per capita, growth prospects, and economic diversity.
   - Higher income levels and diversified economies generally correlate with lower sovereign credit risk due to greater resilience to shocks.

3. **External Assessment**
   - Measures **external liquidity** and the external position.
   - Metrics include the status of the currency (reserve vs. actively traded) and external debt ratios.
   - Updates in the 2017 methodology include using **Current Account Payments (CAP)** in the denominator for certain ratios when external liquid assets exceed external debt, providing a more accurate measure of external indebtedness.

4. **Fiscal Assessment**
   - Analyzes fiscal performance, flexibility, and the **debt burden**.
   - **Net General Government Debt**: The 2017 methodology shifted the primary metric to the change in *net* general government debt (gross debt minus liquid financial assets) as a percentage of GDP. This accounts for government assets that can be deployed during stress, offering a clearer view of the true fiscal stance.
   - **Contingent Liabilities**: Integrates the **Banking Industry Country Risk Assessment (BICRA)** to evaluate risks from the banking sector. High contingent liabilities can lead to negative adjustments in the debt burden assessment.

5. **Monetary Assessment**
   - Evaluates the exchange rate regime and **monetary policy credibility**.
   - For monetary authorities within monetary unions (e.g., ECB), ratings are determined by a weighted average of member sovereign ratings, adjusted by the authority's monetary assessment.

### Key Methodological Updates and Implications

The 2017 update introduced several critical changes that directly impact the quantification of sovereign credit risk:

- **Local vs. Foreign Currency Rating Cap**: The uplift for local-currency ratings is now capped at **one notch** above foreign-currency ratings (previously two). This reflects a more conservative view on the distinction between local and external debt servicing risks, acknowledging that severe institutional failures can impair local currency debt servicing as well.
- **Supplemental Adjustments**: Beyond the five-pillar matrix, specific factors can adjust the final rating.
  - *Negative Adjustments*: Applied for extremely weak external liquidity, extremely high fiscal debt burdens, very high institutional risk combined with high debt, or imminent event risks (war, political conflict).
  - *Positive Adjustments*: Possible for sovereigns with exceptionally large liquid financial assets (net asset position >100% of GDP).

## Impact on Financial Stability and Country Risk

Sovereign credit risk is a central determinant of **Country Risk Analysis**. High sovereign credit risk leads to:
- **Higher Borrowing Costs**: Increased yields on government bonds, which spill over to corporate and financial sector borrowing costs.
- **Capital Flight**: Reduced foreign direct investment and portfolio investment due to perceived instability.
- **Banking Sector Vulnerability**: As sovereigns and banks are often linked ("doom loop"), high sovereign risk can weaken domestic banks holding significant government debt.

The integration of **contingent liabilities** and **banking sector risk** (via BICRA) into the fiscal assessment underscores the interdependence between sovereign creditworthiness and financial stability. A sovereign with a weak banking sector faces higher implicit debt burdens, thereby increasing its overall credit risk.
