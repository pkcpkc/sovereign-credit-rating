# Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB)

The **Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB)** is a macroprudential policy tool designed to ensure that the banking sector maintains sufficient capital to absorb losses during periods of economic downturn. It functions by requiring banks to build up capital reserves during periods of high credit growth and economic expansion (the "boom"), which can then be released or utilized during periods of economic contraction (the "bust").

## Key Functions and Mechanics
*   **Countercyclicality:** The primary goal is to dampen pro-cyclicality in the financial system—the tendency of credit expansion to accelerate during booms and contract sharply during recessions.
*   **Capital Accumulation:** By requiring higher capital ratios during growth phases, the CCyB prevents excessive leverage and ensures a "cushion" is available to maintain lending when demand is weak.
*   **Stability Integration:** It works in tandem with other buffers, such as the **[[Systemic Risk Buffer (SyRB)]]**, to provide a multi-layered defense against systemic shocks.

## Contextual Application: Italy (2025)
In the context of the 2025 IMF Article IV Consultation for Italy, the banking sector is characterized as sound with high profitability and adequate capital buffers. While the specific CCyB requirements are part of the broader regulatory framework, the IMF highlights the importance of these buffers in maintaining **[[Financial Sector Stability]]** amidst global uncertainties and the ongoing monitoring of the sovereign-bank nexus.

## Related Concepts
*   **[[Systemic Risk Buffer (SyRB)]]**: A specific buffer (noted as 1% in the Italian context) used to bolster resilience against systemic risks.
*   **[[Sovereign Risk]]**: The broader risk profile of a country's debt, which influences the stability of the domestic banking sector and the necessity of capital buffers.
*   **Macroprudential Policy**: The overarching regulatory framework that includes CCyB to mitigate systemic risk across the entire financial system.
