# Analytical discretion

**Analytical discretion** refers to the professional judgment and subjective decision-making power exercised by credit rating analysts within structured methodological frameworks. In the context of sovereign credit ratings, particularly within the [[S&P Global Ratings]] methodology, analytical discretion allows analysts to deviate from mechanically derived indicative ratings based on qualitative assessments, event-specific risks, and holistic views of a country's creditworthiness.

## Role in S&P Sovereign Credit Rating Methodology

According to the 2017 S&P methodology framework, as analyzed in academic contexts such as the 2024 presentation by Alex Stomper at HU Berlin, analytical discretion is a critical component that introduces flexibility but also reduces the strict replicability of ratings. The methodology is built on five pillars: [[Institutional assessment]], [[Economic assessment]], [[External assessment]], [[Fiscal assessment]], and [[Monetary assessment]]. While these pillars provide indicative ratings through quantitative and qualitative indicators, the final rating is not purely algorithmic.

### Supplemental Adjustment Factors
Analytical discretion is formally exercised through "supplemental adjustment factors." These are applied when specific conditions warrant a deviation from the baseline indicative rating. Key areas where discretion is applied include:

*   **Event Risk**: Analysts may adjust ratings based on imminent political, social, or economic events that are not fully captured by historical data.
*   **Extreme Liquidity Weakness**: Situations where external liquidity is critically constrained may lead to downward adjustments despite other favorable indicators.
*   **Extreme Fiscal Debt**: Specific structural issues with debt that do not fit standard models may trigger discretionary downgrades.
*   **Institutional Nuances**: Factors such as [[Debt payment culture]] and the effectiveness of policymaking are inherently subjective. For instance, a weak debt payment culture can cap the institutional assessment, requiring analyst interpretation of what constitutes "weak" versus "strong" culture.

### Deviation from Indicative Ratings
The final sovereign credit rating can be overridden by analysts if they deem the indicative rating (derived from the five pillars) to be misleading. This override mechanism is the primary vehicle for analytical discretion, allowing for the incorporation of forward-looking views and qualitative judgments that standard metrics may miss.

## Implications for Replicability and Transparency

The existence of significant analytical discretion has led to critiques regarding the **replicability** of sovereign credit ratings. Because factors like "cohesive civil society," "transparency," and "accountability" are subjective, different analysts might reach different conclusions for the same country under the same framework.

*   **Non-Replicability**: The subjective nature of discretionary adjustments means that the rating process is not fully replicable by third parties without access to the internal deliberations and specific judgments of the rating agency's analysts.
*   **Citizen Science Initiative**: To address this opacity, initiatives have been proposed to apply the S&P methodology to European countries in a "Citizen Science" project. The goal is to document discrepancies between independent applications of the methodology and official ratings, thereby quantifying the impact of analytical discretion and testing the transparency of the rating process.

## Relevance to Country Risk Analysis

In broader [[Banking Industry Country Risk Analysis (BICRA)]] and macroeconomic policy contexts, analytical discretion highlights the limitations of purely quantitative risk models. It underscores that sovereign risk is not just a function of debt-to-GDP ratios or inflation rates, but also of institutional quality, political stability, and the credibility of policy institutions.

*   **Fiscal Sustainability**: Discretion allows analysts to account for contingent liabilities and off-budget guarantees that may not be immediately visible in standard fiscal metrics.
*   **Financial Stability**: By incorporating event risk and liquidity concerns, discretionary judgments help capture emerging threats to financial stability that historical data might not yet reflect.
