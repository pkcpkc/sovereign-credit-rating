# Labour Market

The [[Poland]] labour market is characterized by a significant structural paradox: exceptionally tight conditions with historically low unemployment coexisting with severe demographic headwinds and persistent skills shortages. In 2024, the unemployment rate remained at a historic low of 2.9%, driven by strong private consumption and robust economic growth (2.9% GDP growth in 2024). However, this tightness has begun to ease slightly due to labour market constraints.

### Key Indicators and Demographics
*   **Employment Rate:** Poland’s employment rate reached 78.4% in 2024, surpassing its 2030 [[European Union]] target.
*   **Demographic Pressure:** The working-age population is shrinking due to an ageing society, which puts upward pressure on wages and limits labour supply. Statutory retirement ages remain at 60 for women and 65 for men, contributing to early exit from the workforce.
*   **Unemployment:** Despite high employment, the pool of available workers is shrinking, leading to labour shortages that hinder business expansion.

### Structural Challenges and Skills Mismatch
A primary threat to macroeconomic stability and creditworthiness is the disconnect between labour supply demands and workforce capabilities.
*   **Skills Shortages:** Business surveys identify a shortage of skilled staff as a key barrier to private investment (cited by 87% of firms). This is exacerbated by persistently low enrolment in STEM study programmes and a decline in basic skills among 15-year-olds, particularly in vocational tracks.
*   **Digital and Green Transition:** There are significant gaps in digital and green skills, limiting Poland's ability to capitalize on its status as a clean tech hub (producing 60% of European-made lithium batteries).
*   **Adult Learning:** Participation in adult learning is declining, widening the skills gap and hampering the digital and green transitions.

### Inclusion and Demographic Gaps
While overall employment is high, significant disparities exist among underrepresented groups, indicating inefficiencies in labour market integration.
*   **Gender Gap:** The gender employment gap decreased to 11.6 percentage points (pps) in 2024 but remains above the EU average. This is largely due to insufficient childcare and long-term care services, which disproportionately affect female labour force participation.
*   **Disability Gap:** The disability employment gap widened to 35.6% in 2024, one of the largest in the EU. This is attributed to a lack of supported employment mechanisms and insufficient employer knowledge regarding inclusive hiring practices.

### Institutional and Regulatory Environment
*   **Social Dialogue:** Social dialogue has yielded limited tangible results, with collective bargaining coverage being very low at 13.4%. Regular violations of labour rights have been noted, suggesting structural rigidities in how workers and employers interact.
*   **High Unit Labour Costs:** Rising wages, driven by labour shortages and minimum wage policies, have led to high unit labour costs, impacting competitiveness.

### Impact on Sovereign Credit and Fiscal Policy
The state of the labour market directly influences Poland's fiscal sustainability and sovereign credit rating factors:
1.  **Tax Base Efficiency:** Low labour productivity and skills mismatches contribute to a weak tax base efficiency, complicating efforts to reduce the general government deficit (5.3% of GDP in 2023).
2.  **Pension Sustainability:** The combination of an ageing population and shrinking workforce increases pressure on the pension system. With low uptake of private pension schemes (PPE, IKZE), only ~30% of the eligible population participates, increasing reliance on state pension spending.
3.  **Growth Prospects:** Skills shortages and a shrinking working-age population are identified as key threats to future economic growth, potentially slowing the convergence with Western European income levels.

### Conclusion
The [[Poland]] labour market is currently defined by full employment constraints rather than cyclical unemployment. The critical risk factor is not joblessness, but the *quality* and *availability* of labour. Addressing these issues requires structural reforms in education (particularly STEM and vocational training), improved childcare infrastructure to boost female participation, and pension reforms to mitigate demographic pressures. Failure to address these structural bottlenecks risks stifling productivity growth and undermining fiscal consolidation efforts aimed at reducing the deficit below 3% of GDP by 2028.
