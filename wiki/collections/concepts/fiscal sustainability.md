# fiscal sustainability

**Fiscal sustainability** refers to the ability of a government to maintain its current spending, tax, and debt policies without resorting to excessive borrowing, inflation, or default in the medium to long term. It is a critical component of sovereign credit rating analysis, reflecting the structural integrity of a nation's public finances and its capacity to service debt obligations under various economic scenarios.

Sustainability is not merely a measure of the annual budget deficit but a dynamic assessment of the trajectory of public debt relative to GDP growth, interest rates, and structural expenditure drivers. In the context of the **European Semester** and the reformed EU Economic Governance Framework (Regulation (EU) 2024/1263), fiscal sustainability is evaluated through **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plans (MTFSPs)**. These plans assess compliance with the Stability and Growth Pact while accommodating specific policy needs. Mechanisms such as the **National Escape Clause** allow for temporary deviations for strategic investments (e.g., defense, green transition), while the **Excessive Deficit Procedure** remains a corrective tool for countries with persistently high deficits.

Comparative analysis of **Austria**, **Belgium**, **France**, **Hungary**, and **Malta** in 2025-2026 highlights divergent pathways in managing fiscal sustainability amidst shared challenges of aging populations, weak productivity, and high debt burdens, alongside unique structural risks such as aggressive tax planning and institutional fragmentation. While Western European nations like France and Belgium grapple with deficit persistence and demographic pressures, **Hungary** presents a distinct case where fiscal sustainability is threatened by low growth, high implicit interest costs, significant state intervention distorting markets, and institutional weaknesses related to rule of law. Conversely, **Malta** demonstrates that even countries with low current debt levels and strong growth face severe long-term sustainability threats from demographic aging and narrow tax bases reliant on volatile corporate revenues. These cases illustrate how political structures, demographic profiles, administrative efficiency, institutional credibility, and revenue quality critically influence fiscal trajectories and sovereign risk.

## Definition and Core Components

Fiscal sustainability is assessed through four primary dimensions:

1.  **Structural Fiscal Balance**: The underlying fiscal position adjusted for the economic cycle and one-off measures, providing a clearer view of fiscal stance than nominal deficits.
2.  **Debt Sustainability**: The ability to service existing and projected debt without compromising economic growth or requiring drastic austerity. This involves analyzing the primary balance, interest rates, and GDP growth differentials.
3.  **Long-Term Fiscal Projections**: Assessments of age-related spending pressures (pensions, healthcare, long-term care) over a 50-year horizon, identifying the gap between projected revenues and mandatory expenditures.
4.  **Institutional Credibility & Governance**: The strength of fiscal frameworks, independent fiscal institutions (IFIs), and the coherence of budgetary coordination across federal and regional entities. This includes the efficacy of spending reviews, the political commitment to medium-term adjustment paths, and the stability of regulatory environments.

## Comparative Contextual Analysis: Austria, Belgium, France, Hungary, and Malta (2025-2026)

### Case Study: Austria (2025)
According to the European Commission's 2025 assessment, Austria faces significant challenges to its fiscal sustainability, characterized by widening deficits and structural rigidities.

*   **Current Fiscal Pressures**: Austria’s government deficit is projected to exceed the EU treaty threshold of 3% of GDP, reaching **4.4% in 2025** and **4.2% in 2026**. This is driven by weak economic activity (third consecutive year of recession), high inflation indexation of expenditures, and rising costs related to aging demographics.
*   **Debt Trajectory**: Gross government debt is projected to rise from **81.8% of GDP in 2024 to 84.0% in 2025**. High interest expenditure burdens strain fiscal space, reducing flexibility to respond to future shocks.
*   **Long-Term Structural Challenges**: Age-related public spending is projected to increase from **28% of GDP in 2024 to 30% by 2070**, primarily driven by healthcare and long-term care costs. Declining labor productivity and a shrinking working-age population exacerbate the tax-base-to-spending ratio imbalance.
*   **Institutional Context**: The split of independent fiscal institution (IFI) tasks between **WIFO** and **FISK** may obscure clear reference points, potentially undermining policy credibility.

### Case Study: Belgium (2025)
The 2025 Country Report for Belgium outlines a trajectory of severe public finance deterioration, with unique institutional and demographic pressures impacting fiscal sustainability.

*   **Fiscal Deterioration & Debt Trajectory**: The general government deficit expanded to **4.5% of GDP in 2024** and is forecast to rise further to **5.4% in 2025**. Public debt, already high at **104.7% of GDP**, is on an unsustainable upward trajectory, projected to reach **126.4% by 2035** without intervention.
*   **Use of National Escape Clause**: Belgium successfully utilized the **National Escape Clause** within the reformed EU governance framework to legally exceed net expenditure limits for **defence spending** (projected to rise to 1.7% of GDP in 2025). This mechanism allows deviation while maintaining compliance with the broader fiscal roadmap, provided reforms are delivered.
*   **Severe Ageing-Related Fiscal Pressure**: Belgium faces one of the steepest projected increases in ageing-related spending in the EU. By 2070, age-related expenditure is projected to rise by **5.1 percentage points of GDP** (vs. EU average of 1.4), driven by pensions (+3.5 pps) and long-term care (+1.7 pps).
*   **Institutional Fragmentation**: Fiscal sustainability is threatened by weak budgetary coordination between federal and regional entities and an under-resourced **High Council of Finance**. The lack of a fully developed multiannual fiscal planning framework at the national level historically hindered compliance, though the submitted MTFSP aims to address this.

### Case Study: France (2025-2026)
France’s 2025 Country Report highlights a precarious fiscal situation marked by persistently high deficits, rising debt, and political instability that complicates medium-term planning.

*   **High Deficit and Debt Burden**: France recorded a general government deficit of **5.8% of GDP in 2024**, with public debt rising to **113% of GDP**. The deficit is projected to remain stubbornly high at **5.6% in 2025** and **5.7% in 2026**. Public debt is forecast to reach **118.4% of GDP by 2026**, driven by sluggish tax revenues, high interest payments (projected at **2.9% of GDP in 2026**), and rigid expenditures.
*   **Regulatory Context (Excessive Deficit Procedure)**: Following the Council’s endorsement of France’s **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** in January 2025, the excessive deficit procedure was formally ended. However, the Commission emphasizes that adherence to the seven-year adjustment path is critical. Failure to implement necessary spending reviews and reforms could reignite procedural risks.
*   **Political and Institutional Fragility**: The fragmented political landscape post-2024 elections has led to unprecedented delays in budget adoption and reduced fiscal ambitions. This instability undermines policy credibility. While the **High Council of Public Finance** provides independent oversight, its recommendations are often contested, and the complexity of administrative procedures hinders efficient resource allocation.
*   **Structural and Economic Headwinds**:
    *   **Competitiveness**: High taxes on production, high corporate indebtedness, and a complex business environment hinder competitiveness. Corporate bankruptcies reached record highs in 2024.
    *   **Labor Market**: Employment growth turned negative in 2025, with unemployment rising to 7.9%. Skills mismatches and low business R&D intensity limit productivity gains.
    *   **Energy Transition**: While France has a low-carbon electricity mix, delays in renewable deployment and building decarbonization due to administrative barriers pose long-term fiscal risks if not addressed.
*   **Contingent Liabilities**: Risks associated with state-owned enterprises, particularly **EDF** (Électricité de France), represent a potential contingent liability that could further strain public finances if operational or financial difficulties arise.

### Case Study: Hungary (2025-2026)
The 2025 Country Report for Hungary reveals a fiscal sustainability profile characterized by high medium-term risks, driven by low growth potential, elevated debt servicing costs, and significant institutional distortions.

*   **Fiscal Deficit and Debt Dynamics**: Public debt rose to **73.5% of GDP in 2024**. Although the government deficit decreased from 6.7% in 2023 to **4.9% of GDP in 2024**, it is projected to remain elevated at **4.6% in 2025** and **4.7% in 2026**. The Commission identifies **high medium-term sustainability risks** due to persistent primary deficits and rigid spending commitments.
*   **High Implicit Interest Costs**: A critical threat to Hungary’s fiscal sustainability is the high cost of servicing debt. The implicit interest rate on government debt is close to **6%**, significantly higher than market benchmarks, driven by a large share of domestic debt held by banks and non-bank financial institutions at preferential rates. This erodes primary surplus capacity and limits fiscal flexibility.
*   **Institutional Credibility and Policy Predictability**: Fiscal sustainability is severely compromised by weak institutional frameworks. The **Hungarian Fiscal Council** has limited independence and capacity. The frequent use of **emergency powers** ("state of danger"), regulatory volatility, and discretionary state intervention in business transactions reduce policy predictability and undermine the reliability of medium-term budgetary frameworks. Budgets are often adopted early in the year with frequent revisions, reducing transparency.
*   **State Intervention and Market Distortions**: Heavy state intervention distorts the economic landscape, affecting the tax base and efficiency of public spending. This includes subsidized lending schemes (38.5% of new SME loans), administratively controlled mortgage rates, and high sector-specific taxes (banking, retail, energy). These measures crowd out private actors, limit competition, and create contingent liabilities.
*   **Growth Constraints**: Subdued GDP growth (0.5% in 2024, forecast 0.8% in 2025) limits the denominator effect on debt reduction. Growth is projected to pick up to 2.5% in 2026, driven by EV battery manufacturing, but this is constrained by **low labour productivity** (stagnant for 15 years), skills shortages, and high energy import dependence.
*   **EU Funds and Rule of Law**: The implementation of the **Recovery and Resilience Plan (RRP)** has stalled, with 0% of milestones achieved as of mid-2025, due to anti-corruption deficiencies and weak control mechanisms. This limits access to crucial fiscal support and signals poor governance, impacting long-term fiscal health.
*   **Demographic Pressures**: Similar to its EU peers, Hungary faces aging population pressures, with pension spending projected to rise by **4.3 percentage points of GDP by 2070**, adding to the long-term fiscal gap.

### Case Study: Malta (2025)
Malta presents a contrasting profile where current fiscal indicators appear robust, yet long-term sustainability is threatened by demographic shifts and structural revenue weaknesses.

*   **Current Fiscal Position & Consolidation**: Malta’s general government deficit is projected to decline from **3.7% of GDP in 2024 to 2.8% in 2026**. The debt-to-GDP ratio is low at **47.4% in 2024** and is expected to stabilize below 50% by 2026. This positions Malta favorably in the short term compared to peers like France and Belgium.
*   **Net Expenditure Deviations**: Despite improving deficits, Malta exceeded Council recommendations for net expenditure growth in 2024 (**13.9% growth**), driven by energy subsidies and capital expenditures (e.g., national airline). This highlights the tension between short-term fiscal stimulus and medium-term discipline under the new EU framework.
*   **Severe Long-Term Demographic Risks**: Malta faces the **second-largest projected increase in age-related expenditure in the EU**. Total age-related spending is projected to rise from **17% of GDP in 2024 to 26% by 2070**. Specifically, pension spending is projected to double from 6% to 10.5% of GDP, and healthcare from 5.1% to 7.2% of GDP. This poses a significant long-term sustainability threat despite current low debt levels.
*   **Revenue Quality & Tax Base Vulnerabilities**:
    *   **Low Tax Revenue**: Tax revenue is only **26.6% of GDP** (vs. EU average of 39%), heavily reliant on corporate income tax. This creates vulnerability to global shocks and aggressive tax planning.
    *   **Compliance Gaps**: A significant **VAT compliance gap of 25.9%** (vs. EU average of 7%) indicates substantial revenue leakage.
    *   **Aggressive Tax Planning**: Risks associated with outbound payments and non-domiciled companies remain a concern for institutional credibility and long-term revenue stability, despite some ongoing reforms.
*   **Institutional & Governance Challenges**:
    *   **Judicial Inefficiency**: Lengthy judicial proceedings (454 days for civil/commercial cases) hamper business confidence and enforcement, indirectly affecting tax collection and contract reliability.
    *   **IFIs**: The **Malta Fiscal Advisory Council (MFAC)** faces concerns regarding limited independence and effectiveness.
    *   **Corruption Perceptions**: Corruption perception index scores (46/100) lag behind the EU average, posing risks to institutional credibility and the effective management of public funds, including RRF disbursements.
*   **Contingent Liabilities**: Potential costs associated with state-owned enterprise subsidies (e.g., **Enemalta**) and energy sector interventions, along with unmet healthcare needs, represent contingent liabilities that could strain public finances.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit Ratings and Country Risk

Fiscal sustainability is a primary determinant of sovereign credit ratings. For Austria, Belgium, France, Hungary, and Malta, current trajectories present specific risks and strengths:

### Threats to Rating Stability
*   **Fiscal Slack Erosion**: High debt ratios (France >113%, Belgium >104%, Austria ~84%, Hungary ~73%) and elevated interest costs reduce fiscal space for counter-cyclical policy. Hungary’s high implicit interest costs (6%) and France’s persistent deficit above 5% pose significant long-term refinancing risks. **Malta**, while having low debt, faces risks from its narrow tax base and vulnerability to corporate tax volatility.
*   **Demographic Drag**: The "ageing cost" acts as a structural deficit driver. **Belgium** (+5.1% GDP by 2070) and **Malta** (+9% GDP by 2070, second largest in EU) face steeper burdens than **Austria** (+2% GDP by 2070). France and Hungary face similar medium-term risks, though Hungary’s lower baseline pension spending offers some cushion, albeit eroded by low growth.
*   **Institutional Complexity & Political Stability**:
    *   **Hungary**: Weak rule of law, limited IFI independence, regulatory volatility, and frequent emergency decrees severely undermine policy credibility. Discretionary state intervention creates opaque contingent liabilities.
    *   **France**: Political fragmentation and budgetary delays reduce policy credibility and predictability.
    *   **Belgium**: Federal structure leads to coordination failures and opaque off-budget obligations.
    *   **Austria**: Split IFI mandates may reduce transparency and policy credibility.
    *   **Malta**: Concerns regarding corruption, judicial inefficiency, and the independence of the MFAC pose risks to governance scores and long-term institutional credibility.
*   **Structural Rigidity & Productivity**:
    *   **Hungary**: Stagnant productivity for 15 years, heavy reliance on automotive exports, and skills mismatches limit organic revenue growth and debt sustainability.
    *   **France**: High tax wedges, low business dynamism, and stagnant R&D intensity.
    *   **Belgium**: Low business dynamism ("zombie firms") and weak productivity growth.
    *   **Austria**: Structural declines in total factor productivity and complex regulations hinder labor market flexibility.
    *   **Malta**: Very low R&D intensity (0.64% of GDP) and high regulatory burden (OECD PMR indicator second weakest in EU) constrain long-term growth potential and tax base expansion.
*   **External Vulnerabilities**:
    *   **Hungary**: High dependence on Russian fossil fuels creates supply security risks and energy import bill volatility, impacting the trade balance and inflation, which in turn affects real fiscal balance.
    *   **Malta**: High exposure to global capital flows (Gross external assets/liabilities ~3000% of GDP) and dependence on tourism/services sectors make the tax base vulnerable to external shocks.

### Strengths Supporting Sustainability
*   **Strong Institutional Frameworks**:
    *   **France**: Commitment to the MTFSP and the presence of the **High Council of Public Finance** provide analytical grounding.
    *   **Belgium**: Submission and positive assessment of the MTFSP demonstrates commitment to medium-term consolidation.
    *   **Austria**: Comprehensive national fiscal frameworks and the submission of an MTFSP signal alignment with EU governance rules.
    *   **Hungary**: Despite weaknesses, the submission and Council endorsement of an MTFSP provides a nominal anchor, though implementation risks are high.
    *   **Malta**: Endorsed MTFSP provides a clear fiscal trajectory, and strong integration into EU governance structures supports policy credibility.
*   **Banking Sector Resilience**: All five countries have well-capitalized banking sectors. **Malta** shows particularly strong capital adequacy (CET1 20.2%) and low NPLs (1.8%). However, in **Hungary**, the high concentration of government bonds in bank assets creates systemic risk and distorts credit allocation, whereas in **Malta**, high concentration in the top 5 MFIs (>75% of assets) poses idiosyncratic risks.
*   **Eurozone Membership (Austria, Belgium, France, Malta)**: Access to ECB liquidity and deep capital markets provides funding stability and reduces sovereign risk premiums. **Hungary**, as a non-euro member, faces currency risk and higher financing costs, relying on significant foreign exchange reserves for stability. **Malta** benefits from this stability and has a strong net international investment position.
*   **Economic Diversification & Investment**:
    *   **France**: A diversified industrial base including nuclear, aerospace, and agriculture provides a robust tax base.
    *   **Hungary**: Emerging leadership in electric vehicle battery manufacturing and high FDI levels offer growth prospects, though the economy remains heavily reliant on automotive exports.
    *   **Malta**: Diversified service exports (tourism, iGaming, financial services) and strong short-to-medium term growth forecasts (4.1% in 2025) provide a strong current revenue base, though the lack of industrial depth remains a structural risk.

## Policy Implications and Recommendations

To enhance fiscal sustainability, European Commission assessments emphasize targeted structural and fiscal measures:

1.  **Accelerated Structural Reforms**:
    *   **France**: Simplify administrative procedures, reduce regulatory restrictions in services and retail, and improve the efficiency of R&D schemes.
    *   **Belgium**: Simplify tax benefits, reduce administrative burdens, and boost innovation diffusion. Address "zombie firms" to free up resources.
    *   **Austria**: Improve access to risk capital and reduce regulatory barriers to boost potential output.
    *   **Hungary**: Reduce state intervention in the economy (subsidized loans, price controls) to improve market efficiency. Strengthen competition policy and reduce regulatory volatility to attract sustainable investment. Address skills shortages and improve education outcomes to boost productivity.
    *   **Malta**: Reduce the high regulatory burden (OECD PMR), improve judicial efficiency to shorten case resolution times, and enhance enforcement of anti-money laundering and aggressive tax planning regulations to broaden the effective tax base.
2.  **Labor Market Modernization**:
    *   **France**: Address skills shortages, improve educational outcomes, and enhance the attractiveness of the teaching profession.
    *   **Belgium**: Link unemployment benefits to income, limit duration for younger workers, and raise effective retirement ages.
    *   **Austria**: Address skills shortages in ICT and care sectors; tighten early retirement access.
    *   **Hungary**: Improve the quality of education and vocational training to address skills mismatches. Enhance labor market flexibility and incentives for older workers.
    *   **Malta**: Address severe skills mismatches and labor shortages by improving education outcomes and vocational training. Enhance labor market inclusion for non-EU nationals and reduce the gender employment gap.
3.  **Fiscal Consolidation & Governance**:
    *   **France**: Implement credible spending reviews as committed in the MTFSP. Strengthen coordination between political parties to ensure timely budget adoption.
    *   **Belgium**: Strengthen budgetary coordination between federal and regional levels. Empower the High Council of Finance.
    *   **Austria**: Implement credible deficit reduction measures (e.g., EUR 6.4 billion savings target for 2025) to stabilize the debt-to-GDP ratio.
    *   **Hungary**: Enhance the independence and capacity of the **Hungarian Fiscal Council**. Improve budget transparency and reliability by reducing emergency decrees and discretionary interventions. Ensure realistic medium-term budgetary frameworks. Address off-budget liabilities and improve oversight of public interest trusts and state-owned enterprises.
    *   **Malta**: Strengthen the independence and effectiveness of the **Malta Fiscal Advisory Council (MFAC)**. Broaden the tax base by reducing reliance on corporate income tax, closing the VAT compliance gap, and implementing withholding taxes on outbound payments. Manage net expenditure growth carefully to adhere to Council recommendations.
4.  **Long-Term Ageing Mitigation**:
    *   All five nations must continue to implement pension reforms and invest in preventive healthcare to curb the projected surge in long-term care and pension expenditures. **Malta**’s projected doubling of pension spending as a share of GDP requires urgent structural reforms. France’s 2023 pension reform is a key step. Hungary needs to ensure pension systems remain sustainable amidst an aging population and low growth.
5.  **Green & Digital Transition Integration**:
    *   **France**: Accelerate the deployment of renewable energies and building decarbonization.
    *   **Hungary**: Accelerate the energy transition away from Russian fossil fuels to improve energy security and reduce external vulnerabilities. Improve grid flexibility and renewable integration. Ensure EU funds are utilized effectively for green and digital investments, addressing rule of law concerns to unlock RRF funding.
    *   **Malta**: Accelerate renewable energy deployment (currently 17% of electricity mix) and reduce fossil fuel subsidies. Improve waste management and recycling rates. Utilize RRF funds effectively for digitalization and energy efficiency.
    *   **All**: Utilize mechanisms like the **National Escape Clause** to accommodate necessary spending on defense and energy transition without derailing overall fiscal consolidation paths.

## Related Concepts

*   [[Debt Sustainability]]: The long-term ability to service debt obligations without distress.
*   [[Macroeconomic Stability]]: The broader economic context in which fiscal policies operate.
*   [[Structural Reforms]]: Policies aimed at improving the efficiency and competitiveness of the economy.
*   [[Aging Population]]: A demographic trend with profound implications for public spending.
*   [[European Semester]]: The EU framework for coordinating economic and fiscal policies.
*   [[Stability and Growth Pact]]: The EU rules governing fiscal discipline among member states.
*   [[National Escape Clause]]: A provision in EU fiscal rules allowing temporary deviations from expenditure limits for specific investments like defense.
*   [[Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan]]: National plans outlining fiscal and structural reforms to comply with EU economic governance.
*   [[Excessive Deficit Procedure]]: The EU mechanism to address countries with fiscal imbalances exceeding treaty limits.
*   [[Sovereign Credit Rating]]: An assessment of a government's creditworthiness by rating agencies.
*   [[Contingent Liabilities]]: Potential obligations that may arise from future events, such as support for state-owned enterprises or off-budget funds.
*   [[Institutional Credibility]]: The trust and predictability associated with a government's policy-making and fiscal framework.
*   [[Rule of Law]]: Legal and institutional frameworks that underpin economic stability and investment security.
*   [[Aggressive Tax Planning]]: Strategies used to minimize tax liability, impacting long-term revenue sustainability and international reputation.
*   [[Net Expenditure Growth]]: A key metric in the new EU fiscal framework for assessing compliance with fiscal rules.
*   [[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]: EU funding instrument supporting structural reforms and investments, impacting fiscal capacity.
