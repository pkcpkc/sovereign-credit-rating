# Climate Risk

**Climate Risk** refers to the potential for adverse financial, operational, or macroeconomic impacts resulting from physical climate changes (e.g., extreme weather events, rising sea levels) and the transition to a low-carbon economy (e.g., policy shifts, technological changes). In sovereign credit analysis, it is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of long-term **Debt Sustainability**, **Macroeconomic Stability**, and institutional resilience.

## Core Dimensions of Climate Risk

Climate risk is typically bifurcated into two primary categories, both of which influence sovereign credit profiles:

1.  **Physical Risk**: The direct economic and social damage caused by acute events (e.g., floods, hurricanes, heatwaves) and chronic shifts (e.g., rising sea levels, desertification). Physical risks directly impact **GDP** growth, infrastructure integrity, and fiscal capacity through reconstruction costs and reduced productivity.
2.  **Transition Risk**: The financial losses associated with the shift toward a lower-carbon economy. This includes policy risks (carbon taxes, regulatory changes), technological obsolescence of fossil-fuel-dependent assets, and shifts in market sentiment toward sustainable investments.

## Integration into Sovereign Credit Analysis

Modern rating methodologies, such as those employed by **Fitch Ratings** and the **IMF**, have integrated climate risk into standard credit assessments through specific models and qualitative overlays.

### Fitch Ratings: Climate Vulnerability Signal (Climate.VS)
Fitch Ratings utilizes a screening tool known as the **Climate Vulnerability Signal (Climate.VS)**, which assigns a score from 0 to 100 based on:
*   **Physical Risks**: Exposure to heatwaves, floods, and other natural disasters.
*   **Transition Risks**: Dependence on fossil fuels and the costs associated with the green energy transition.

Sovereigns with a **Climate.VS** score of 50 or higher in 2035 are subject to additional analytical scrutiny. This can lead to adjustments in the **Qualitative Overlay (QO)**, potentially resulting in a rating notch adjustment if the country is deemed vulnerable to long-term climate-related credit risks.

### IMF: Macro-Critical Climate Shocks
The **IMF** identifies macro-critical climate-related shocks as significant downside risks to global and national economic stability. In country reports (e.g., **IMF 2025 Italy Country Report**), climate risks are evaluated for their potential to:
*   Disrupt **Fiscal Policy** through unexpected expenditure on disaster relief and adaptation.
*   Impact **External Vulnerability** via trade disruptions in climate-sensitive sectors (e.g., agriculture, tourism).
*   Exacerbate **Sovereign Risk** by straining public finances, particularly in countries with high public debt levels or limited fiscal space.

## Impact on Financial Stability and Fiscal Sustainability

### Fiscal Pressure
Climate risks pose a direct threat to **Fiscal Sustainability** by increasing contingent liabilities. Governments may face higher costs for infrastructure repair, healthcare burdens from climate-related health issues, and social safety net expenditures. For countries with high **Public Debt**, such as Italy (with a debt-to-GDP ratio of 135.3% in 2025), climate-induced fiscal shocks can impede debt reduction trajectories and increase the **Interest-growth differential**.

### Banking Sector Vulnerability
Climate risks also affect the **Banking Sector** through exposure to climate-vulnerable assets. Banks with significant lending to high-risk industries (e.g., fossil fuels, coastal real estate) may face increased **Nonperforming Loans** or asset write-downs. This creates a feedback loop known as the **Sovereign-bank nexus**, where sovereign credit deterioration due to climate costs can weaken bank balance sheets, and vice versa.

### Structural Reforms and Adaptation
Mitigating climate risk requires robust **Structural Reform** strategies. These include:
*   Investing in climate-resilient infrastructure.
*   Implementing carbon pricing mechanisms.
*   Enhancing data quality for surveillance of climate-related financial risks.
*   Aligning fiscal plans (e.g., **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan**) with green transition goals.
