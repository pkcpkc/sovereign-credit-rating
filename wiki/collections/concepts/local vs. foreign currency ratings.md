# local vs. foreign currency ratings

**Local vs. Foreign Currency Ratings** refer to the distinct credit assessments assigned to a sovereign government's ability and willingness to service its financial obligations, categorized by the currency denomination of the debt. The **Foreign Currency Sovereign Rating (FCSR)** assesses the risk of default on debt issued in a currency other than the sovereign's domestic currency, while the **Local Currency Sovereign Rating (LCSR)** assesses the risk of default on debt issued in the domestic currency.

In the context of the **S&P Global Ratings Sovereign Rating Methodology (2017)**, the distinction between these two ratings is critical for understanding a sovereign's external versus internal liquidity constraints, monetary policy credibility, and institutional debt payment culture.

## Core Distinctions and Methodology

### 1. Assessment Basis
*   **Foreign Currency Rating (FCSR):** Primarily driven by external sector risks, including [[external liquidity]], [[debt sustainability]], and the availability of foreign exchange reserves. It reflects the sovereign's ability to generate foreign currency revenues to service external debt.
*   **Local Currency Rating (LCSR):** Driven by the sovereign's control over its own currency, including the ability to monetize debt (print money), the credibility of [[monetary policy]], and domestic [[fiscal assessment]] metrics. However, this advantage is constrained by inflation risks, currency depreciation, and the legal or institutional constraints on monetization.

### 2. The Rating Notch Difference (Uplift/Cap)
Historically, LCSR often exceeded FCSR because sovereigns with fiat currencies possess the ultimate backstop of monetary financing to avoid default on local-currency debt. However, this difference is not unlimited.

*   **2017 Methodology Update:** A significant change in the S&P Global Ratings 2017 methodology is the **limitation of the local-currency rating uplift**.
    *   **Previous Standard:** Local-currency ratings could be up to **two notches** higher than foreign-currency ratings.
    *   **Current Standard (Post-2017):** The local-currency rating is generally **capped at one notch** above the foreign-currency rating.
    *   **Rationale:** This change reflects a more conservative view on the distinction between local and external debt servicing risks, acknowledging that extreme fiscal imbalances or institutional failures can constrain even local-currency obligations (e.g., through inflationary finance or political refusal to monetize).

## Key Drivers of the Spread

The difference between LCSR and FCSR is determined by several interacting pillars within the **S&P Sovereign Rating Methodology**:

### Institutional Assessment
*   **Debt Payment Culture:** A sovereign with a weak [[debt payment culture]] (e.g., history of arrears, selective payment defaults) may face a compressed spread between LCSR and FCSR, or even a negative adjustment if institutional risks are extreme.
*   **Political Constraints:** Institutional frameworks that legally restrict the central bank from financing the government (fiscal dominance) can narrow the advantage of the LCSR, as the government cannot simply print money to meet obligations without violating legal mandates.

### Monetary Assessment
*   **Monetary Policy Credibility:** High credibility in [[monetary policy]] reduces the inflationary risk associated with monetizing local debt. If a central bank lacks independence or credibility, the LCSR may be downgraded to reflect the high inflation risk that could erode the real value of debt servicing.
*   **Exchange Rate Regime:** In countries with fixed exchange rate regimes or currency boards, the distinction between local and foreign currency ratings may diminish because the central bank is committed to maintaining parity, effectively linking local liquidity to foreign reserves.

### External and Fiscal Assessments
*   **External Liquidity:** Strong [[external liquidity]] (high foreign reserves relative to external debt) supports a higher FCSR, potentially narrowing the gap with LCSR.
*   **Contingent Liabilities:** Significant [[contingent liabilities]] (e.g., from the [[banking sector]]) assessed via [[Banking Industry Country Risk Assessment (BICRA)]] can negatively impact both ratings, but may weigh heavier on LCSR if the banking sector is domestically funded.

## Strategic Implications

### For Investors
*   **Arbitrage Opportunities:** The one-notch cap implies that investors should scrutinize sovereigns with large spreads. A wide historical spread that has now converged may indicate deteriorating [[institutional assessment]] or [[monetary policy credibility]].
*   **Default Risk Perception:** The FCSR represents the "hardest" constraint (ability to buy FX), while the LCSR represents the "softest" constraint (willingness/ability to print money). Investors in local currency bonds must assess whether the sovereign's [[monetary policy]] will allow for inflationary default rather than nominal default.

### For Policymakers
*   **Market Access:** A narrower spread between LCSR and FCSR may indicate to markets that the sovereign has strong [[external liquidity]] and institutional trust, reducing reliance on foreign capital.
*   **Policy Space:** Understanding the cap helps policymakers gauge the cost of debt. If LCSR is capped at one notch above FCSR, excessive fiscal expansion that threatens external stability will directly drag down domestic borrowing costs, limiting the benefit of issuing in local currency.
