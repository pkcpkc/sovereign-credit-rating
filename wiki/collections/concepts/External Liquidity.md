# External liquidity

**External liquidity** is a critical component of sovereign credit analysis, representing a country's ability to meet its external financial obligations (such as external debt payments and current account deficits) using its available liquid assets. In the context of sovereign rating methodologies, particularly that of **[[S&P Global Ratings]]**, external liquidity is evaluated as a core pillar of the **[[External Assessment]]**, directly influencing the sovereign's foreign-currency credit rating. It serves as a primary buffer against external shocks, determining whether a sovereign can service its [[debt sustainability]] requirements without resorting to emergency measures such as capital controls, disorderly currency depreciation, or external default.

## Definition and Scope

External liquidity refers to the stock of liquid external assets held by the sovereign government and its central bank that can be readily deployed to service external debt or cover current account payments. Key metrics used to assess external liquidity include:

- **Foreign Exchange Reserves**: The primary buffer against external shocks and a key indicator of immediate liquidity coverage.
- **Net International Investment Position (NIIP)**: The difference between a country's external financial assets and liabilities, reflecting the broader external asset/liability position.
- **Current Account Payments (CAP)**: The outflow of funds required to meet external obligations.

Adequate external liquidity ensures that a sovereign can service its external debt and finance current account deficits, particularly when facing volatile capital flows. It is intrinsically linked to the **[[Exchange rate regime]]**, as countries with fixed or managed exchange rates often require higher liquidity buffers to defend their currency pegs compared to those with flexible regimes.

## Role in Sovereign Credit Rating Methodologies

### S&P Global Ratings Framework (2017)

In the **[[S&P Global Ratings]]** Sovereign Rating Methodology, external liquidity is a distinct sub-factor within the **[[External Assessment]]** pillar. The assessment determines whether a sovereign has sufficient buffers to withstand external shocks and meet payment obligations.

1.  **Scoring and Pillar Interaction**:
    The External Assessment is scored on a scale of 1 to 6 (where 1 is strongest and 6 is weakest). This score is combined with scores from the [[Institutional Assessment]], [[Economic Assessment]], [[Fiscal Assessment]], and [[Monetary Assessment]] to determine the indicative rating level.

2.  **Metric Adjustments**:
    The 2017 methodology update introduced specific adjustments to how external liquidity ratios are calculated:
    - **Current Account Payments (CAP)**: S&P shifted from using Current Account Receipts (CAR) to using Current Account Payments (CAP) in the denominator for certain external ratios when a sovereign's external liquid assets exceed its external debt. This provides a more conservative and accurate measure of external indebtedness and liquidity coverage.
    - **Currency Status**: The classification of a currency as a "reserve currency" (>3% of global FX reserves) or an "actively traded currency" (>1% of global FX turnover) influences the baseline for external liquidity requirements. Reserve currency issuers typically face lower external liquidity constraints due to greater market depth and investor confidence.

3.  **Supplemental Adjustments and Analyst Discretion**:
    External liquidity can trigger direct adjustments to the final rating, often serving as a key area where **[[Analytical discretion]]** is applied:
    - **Negative Adjustments**: "Extremely weak external liquidity" is explicitly cited as a factor that can lead to downward rating adjustments (supplemental adjustments), independent of the core matrix score. This often applies to countries with low reserve buffers relative to short-term external debt or large current account deficits.
    - **Positive Adjustments**: Conversely, sovereigns with exceptionally large liquid financial assets (resulting in a net asset position >100% of GDP) may receive positive adjustments.
    - **Discretionary Overrides**: As noted in academic critiques of the methodology, the determination of "extreme" weakness is subject to analyst judgment. This subjectivity contributes to the debate on the **replicability of ratings**, as the threshold for what constitutes a liquidity crisis requiring a rating downgrade is not strictly quantifiable.

## Impact on Financial Stability and Risk Analysis

### Country Risk and Banking Sector Contingencies
External liquidity is intrinsically linked to the [[Banking Industry Country Risk Assessment (BICRA)]]. Weak external liquidity at the sovereign level can constrain the banking sector's ability to access foreign funding, increasing the risk of [[contingent liabilities]] if banks require government support during a crisis. Conversely, strong sovereign liquidity enhances the creditworthiness of domestic banks by ensuring a stable macroeconomic environment and credible lender-of-last-resort capacity.

### Interaction with Monetary Policy
The availability of external liquidity affects the **[[Monetary Assessment]]**. Central banks with high reserves can intervene more effectively in foreign exchange markets to smooth volatility, supporting [[monetary policy credibility]]. However, if external liquidity is depleted, the central bank may be forced to raise interest rates sharply to defend the currency, potentially stifling [[economic assessment]] metrics like growth and diversity. The effectiveness of the central bank's toolkit is thus heavily dependent on the stock of external liquidity.

### Local vs. Foreign Currency Ratings
While external liquidity primarily drives the foreign-currency rating, it indirectly affects the local-currency rating. The 2017 methodology caps the local-currency rating uplift at one notch above the foreign-currency rating. This reflects the reality that severe external liquidity crises often spill over into the domestic economy, limiting the government's ability to isolate local-currency obligations from external shocks.

## Key Indicators of Weak External Liquidity

Credit rating agencies typically flag external liquidity risks when:
- Foreign exchange reserves are insufficient to cover short-term external debt (e.g., reserves < 100% of short-term debt).
- The current account deficit is large and persistent, requiring continuous capital inflows that are vulnerable to reversal.
- The country lacks access to international capital markets due to high risk premiums or sovereign distress.
- There is a significant mismatch between the currency denomination of external assets and liabilities.
- **Analyst Judgment**: Specific instances of "extreme liquidity weakness" may be identified through discretionary analysis, particularly in emerging markets where market depth is limited.

## Related Concepts

- **[[External Assessment]]**: The broader pillar in sovereign rating methodologies that includes external liquidity, external position, and currency status.
- **[[Debt Sustainability]]**: The capacity of a sovereign to manage its debt stock and flow over time, heavily dependent on external liquidity to service external debt.
- **[[Contingent Liabilities]]**: Potential obligations (e.g., from the banking sector) that may materialize if external liquidity conditions deteriorate, impacting fiscal sustainability.
- **[[Monetary Policy Credibility]]**: The effectiveness of central bank policy, which relies on adequate reserves (liquidity) to maintain exchange rate stability and control inflation.
- **[[Banking Industry Country Risk Assessment (BICRA)]]**: A framework used to assess the risk posed by the banking sector to the sovereign, which is exacerbated by weak external liquidity.
- **[[Analytical discretion]]**: The role of subjective judgment in applying supplemental adjustments for factors like extreme external liquidity weakness, impacting the replicability of ratings.
