# Constitutional Tribunal

The **Constitutional Tribunal** is the highest judicial body in Poland responsible for reviewing the constitutionality of laws and international agreements. In the context of the 2025 Rule of Law Report, the Tribunal is central to a profound institutional crisis in Poland, characterized by a legitimacy dispute between the government and the Tribunal, which has significant implications for judicial independence, legal certainty, and sovereign credit risk.

## Institutional Crisis and Legitimacy Dispute

Since March 2024, the Polish government has refused to publish judgments issued by the Constitutional Tribunal, arguing that the body itself is illegitimate due to appointments made by the post-2017 National Council for the Judiciary (NCJ). This refusal to enforce or publish rulings has created a direct clash with European Union law, leading to ongoing infringement procedures before the Court of Justice of the EU.

Key developments in this crisis include:
- **Appointment Deadlock**: The Sejm (lower house of parliament) has ceased appointing new judges to the Tribunal. Conversely, the President of the Republic, Andrzej Duda, has appointed a new Vice-President to the Tribunal.
- **Constitutional Clash**: The Tribunal has issued rulings on the constitutionality of EU treaty provisions, asserting its authority over EU law supremacy in specific contexts. This has exacerbated tensions with the European Union and undermined the predictability of Poland's legal framework.
- **Pending Reforms**: While legislative efforts are underway to amend laws regarding the NCJ and the Constitutional Tribunal, key amendments remain pending referral to the Constitutional Tribunal itself, creating a legislative stalemate.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit and Country Risk

The status of the Constitutional Tribunal is a critical factor in assessing Poland's sovereign credit rating and institutional governance strength. The crisis introduces significant risks to **legal certainty** and **institutional checks and balances**, which are key components of sovereign risk analysis.

### Credit Risk Factors
- **Institutional and Governance Threats**: The Constitutional Tribunal's legitimacy crisis is identified as a major threat to Poland's institutional governance. The refusal to publish judgments undermines the rule of law, leading to **legal uncertainty** that deters foreign direct investment and complicates business operations.
- **Political Polarization**: The conflict reflects deep political polarization, threatening the stability of democratic processes and the independence of judicial institutions.
- **EU Financial Conditionality**: The Tribunal's rulings on EU law compatibility directly impact Poland's access to and utilization of EU funds, including the Recovery and Resilience Plan. Non-compliance with EU legal standards, driven by the Tribunal's stance, poses a threat to external liquidity and fiscal sustainability.

### Mitigating Factors
- **Ombudsperson Independence**: The independence of the Ombudsperson (Waldemar Kowalewski) serves as a counterbalance, maintaining some level of institutional integrity and civil society engagement.
- **Government Action Plan**: The current administration's Action Plan on Rule of Law implementation aims to address these crises, including drafting laws to regularize the status of judges and potentially resolve the Tribunal's composition issues.

## Key Personnel and Stakeholders

- **Bogdan Swieczkowski**: President of the Constitutional Tribunal. His leadership is central to the Tribunal's current stance on constitutional matters and its interaction with the executive branch.
- **Andrzej Duda**: President of the Republic, who has appointed members to the Tribunal, contributing to the legitimacy dispute with the legislature.
- **Donald Tusk**: Prime Minister, whose government has refused to publish Tribunal judgments, arguing the body is illegitimate.
