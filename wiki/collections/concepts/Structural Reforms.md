# Structural Reforms

**Structural Reforms** refer to comprehensive policy adjustments, institutional changes, and regulatory simplifications designed to improve the efficiency, resilience, and long-term growth potential of an economy. In the context of sovereign credit rating analysis, European Union economic governance, and International Monetary Fund (IMF) Article IV Consultations, these reforms are critical for addressing structural rigidities that hinder productivity, exacerbate fiscal vulnerabilities, and limit the effectiveness of monetary and fiscal policy.

Recent assessments from the European Commission's 2025 Country Reports and IMF Article IV Consultations highlight that structural reforms are no longer optional but essential for maintaining fiscal sustainability, ensuring macroeconomic stability, and restoring competitiveness. The lack of such reforms is increasingly identified as a primary threat to sovereign creditworthies, particularly in economies facing high public debt, demographic aging, productivity stagnation, and external imbalances.

From a credit rating perspective, particularly under frameworks such as the [[Fitch Ratings]] Sovereign Rating Criteria (2025-2026), structural reforms are a key determinant of [[Institutional Strength]] and [[Fiscal Policy]] flexibility. They serve as a critical input into the **Qualitative Overlay (QO)** applied to the [[Sovereign Rating Model]] (SRM). While quantitative models capture baseline economic variables, structural reforms influence the qualitative assessment of a government's "Willingness and Capacity to Pay" by signaling political feasibility, policy predictability, and the ability to navigate demographic or environmental shocks (e.g., via the [[Climate Vulnerability Signal]]).

## Key Dimensions of Structural Reforms

Structural reforms encompass a wide array of policy interventions targeting different facets of the economy. Based on recent country-specific assessments from Malta, Poland, Austria, Belgium, France, Hungary, Romania, and Slovakia, key dimensions include:

### 1. Labor Market, Human Capital, and Demographics
Reforms in this area aim to increase labor force participation, reduce skills mismatches, and improve wage flexibility, particularly in the face of demographic headwinds.
*   **Activation and Participation:** In [[Belgium]], reforms are needed to address low activity rates among low-skilled workers, migrants, and older individuals. High labor taxes and complex social benefits create disincentives to work, leading to high vacancy rates despite high employment levels [[Belgium]]. Similarly, [[Austria]] faces demographic aging and constrained labor supply, requiring reforms to boost female full-time work and elderly participation through expanded childcare and eldercare facilities [[Austria]]. In [[Poland]], despite a shrinking working-age population, employment rates have surpassed targets, yet significant gaps remain for women and persons with disabilities due to insufficient support services [[Poland]].
*   **Skills and Education:** In [[Malta]], severe labor shortages in ICT, construction, and healthcare are masked by high immigration but constrain competitiveness. Low R&D intensity (0.64% of GDP) further hampers innovation [[Malta]]. In [[Italy]], structural issues include low tertiary education attainment and significant skills mismatches, particularly in the Mezzogiorno (South) [[Italy]]. In [[France]], high youth unemployment and NEET rates necessitate reforms in training offers to match labor market needs [[France]]. In [[Poland]], declining basic skills among youth and low STEM enrollment exacerbate labor shortages, while adult learning participation is falling [[Poland]].
*   **Wage Indexation and Flexibility:** [[Belgium]]'s automatic wage indexation mechanism is cited as a structural rigidity that can lead to competitiveness losses, requiring reform to align wage growth with productivity [[Belgium]]. In [[Austria]], sticky services inflation driven by strong wage growth requires coordination with productivity gains to maintain competitiveness [[Austria]]. In [[Hungary]], persistent inflation above target is partly driven by core services inflation and wage pressures, with the IMF advising the phasing out of distortive price controls to allow market mechanisms to function [[Hungary]].

### 2. Public Finance, Pension Systems, and Social Protection
Reforms targeting public spending are crucial for long-term fiscal sustainability, particularly in the face of aging populations and rising health costs.
*   **Pension Sustainability:** [[Austria]] and [[Belgium]] are urged to implement pension reforms to address rising expenditure costs driven by demographic aging. In [[Austria]], pension expenditure is projected to rise from 14.2% of GDP in 2024 to 15% by 2030 [[Austria]]. In [[Poland]], the statutory retirement ages and annuity factor pose threats to sustainability, with low uptake of private pension schemes (only 30% participation) limiting resilience [[Poland]]. In [[Malta], rising age-related expenditure (pensions, healthcare, long-term care) threatens fiscal sustainability despite currently low pension spending relative to GDP [[Malta]]. In [[Slovakia]], adverse demographic trends and aging-related costs pose significant medium-term risks, with projections suggesting public debt could exceed 200% of GDP by 2050 without pension and healthcare reforms [[Slovakia]]. In [[Romania]], costly pension reforms contributed to a fiscal deficit of 8.7% of GDP in 2024, prompting a new consolidation package [[Romania]].
*   **Healthcare and Long-Term Care:** In [[Italy]], rising pension costs and high structural expenditure pose threats to fiscal sustainability, requiring reforms to ensure long-term solvency [[Italy]]. In [[Poland], there is a lack of formal long-term care capacity, leading to a heavy burden on informal carers, and poor health outcomes among working-age adults [[Poland]]. In [[Malta], social inequalities remain high for vulnerable groups, including persons with disabilities and non-EU nationals [[Malta]].
*   **Tax Structure and Efficiency:** [[Belgium]] calls for a shift in the tax burden away from labor to simplify regulations and improve incentives for work and investment [[Belgium]]. In [[Malta], the tax system relies heavily on corporate income taxes with a narrow labor taxation base and significant VAT compliance gaps, creating vulnerabilities [[Malta]]. In [[Poland], the complex tax framework hinders innovation and sustainable growth, with scope for simplification and elimination of numerous tax reliefs [[Poland]]. In [[Hungary]], the IMF recommends broadening the tax base and rationalizing energy subsidies to support fiscal adjustment [[Hungary]]. In [[Romania]], VAT rate hikes were implemented as part of a larger fiscal consolidation package to address high deficits [[Romania]]. In [[Slovakia]], measures include reducing reduced VAT rates and raising property taxes to support consolidation [[Slovakia]].

### 3. Business Environment, Regulatory Simplification, and Innovation
Reducing administrative burdens and improving the ease of doing business are central to fostering innovation, investment, and digital/green transitions.
*   **Regulatory Complexity and Burden:** [[France]] struggles with complex administrative procedures and a regulatory burden that hinders the implementation of renewable energy projects and business innovation [[France]]. In [[Malta], administrative burdens and lengthy judicial proceedings (454 days for civil cases) are significant barriers to investment [[Malta]]. In [[Poland], uncertainty about the future and high energy costs are key barriers to private investment, alongside underdeveloped grid interconnections [[Poland]].
*   **Simplification and Governance:** [[Austria]] highlights the need for regulatory simplification to restore competitiveness and reduce bureaucratic hurdles that stifle business dynamism [[Austria]]. In [[Hungary]], unpredictable regulatory changes, sector-specific taxes, and state intervention crowding out private actors are significant structural barriers [[Hungary]].
*   **Innovation and Digital/Green Transition:** [[France]] leverages its position in digital and green transitions, particularly through AI adoption, to boost productivity [[France]]. In [[Malta], low R&D investment and slow progress on climate targets are threats, though the establishment of Science Malta aims to streamline R&D governance [[Malta]]. In [[Poland], the country has emerged as a clean tech hub (particularly in battery manufacturing), but faces skill shortages and regulatory uncertainties regarding fossil fuel reliance and subsidies [[Poland]].

### 4. Institutional Strength, Governance, and Policy Predictability
The capacity of institutions to implement and sustain reforms is a key determinant of their success.
*   **Administrative Capacity and Judicial Efficiency:** [[Italy]] has made efforts to improve judicial efficiency and public administration through [[Recovery and Resilience Plan]] (RRP) support, though case backlogs remain a critical issue [[Italy]]. In [[Malta], lengthy judicial proceedings and perceived corruption risks are threats to institutional strength, despite strong digital public services infrastructure [[Malta]]. In [[Romania]], slow absorption of EU funds is attributed to administrative capacity constraints and governance issues [[Romania]]. In [[Slovakia]], concerns over governance, corruption risks, and perceived weakness in judicial independence have slowed EU funds disbursements [[Slovakia]].
*   **Coordination and Federalism:** [[Belgium]] lacks a fully developed multiannual fiscal planning framework, highlighting weaknesses in budgetary coordination between federal and regional entities [[Belgium]]. In [[France], complex multi-level government coordination and political fragmentation pose risks to reform continuity, despite the existence of a Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plan (MTFSP) [[France]].
*   **Rule of Law and Independence:** In [[Hungary]], weak institutions, poor rule of law, and corruption are identified as major threats to institutional strength, undermining policy predictability and reform continuity. The suspension of EU funds (€19 billion) due to rule of law concerns is a critical risk factor [[Hungary]]. In [[Poland], weak judicial independence concerns and a fast-changing regulatory framework create uncertainty, although the establishment of a Fiscal Council in 2024 aims to improve fiscal governance [[Poland]].

## Impact on Sovereign Credit Ratings and Fiscal Sustainability

Structural reforms have a direct and indirect impact on sovereign credit ratings through several channels, influencing both the quantitative [[Sovereign Rating Model]] (SRM) outputs and the qualitative adjustments made by rating agencies like [[Fitch Ratings]].

*   **Debt Sustainability:** Effective reforms can lower the debt-to-GDP ratio by boosting growth (numerator effect) and improving primary balances (denominator effect). For example, [[Italy]]'s return to a primary surplus is linked to fiscal consolidation measures, but long-term sustainability depends on structural reforms to manage rising debt-to-GDP ratios projected to reach 136.7% by 2025 [[Italy]]. In contrast, countries like [[Malta]] maintain low debt-to-GDP ratios (below 50%) but face long-term sustainability threats from rising age-related expenditures, necessitating proactive pension and healthcare reforms [[Malta]]. In [[Hungary]], public debt is projected to rise to 79% of GDP by 2030, driven by high interest expenses and significant contingent liabilities from State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs) guarantees (14% of GDP) [[Hungary]]. In [[Romania]], despite an investment-grade rating, doubts about the execution of fiscal consolidation and high current deficits pose downgrade risks [[Romania]]. In [[Slovakia]], without further consolidation, public debt is projected to rise to 74% of GDP by 2030 [[Slovakia]].

*   **Growth Prospects:** Structural reforms that improve productivity and innovation are critical for medium-to-long-term growth prospects. [[France]]'s weak total factor productivity and low business R&D intensity are cited as threats to growth, emphasizing the need for reforms in innovation support and business environment [[France]]. In [[Austria], sluggish productivity growth and demographic aging constrain medium-term prospects, requiring reforms to deepen the EU Single Market and boost labor supply [[Austria]]. In [[Hungary]], stagnant output over three years and productivity impediments are countered by investments in EV and battery production, yet low investment and weak business confidence remain challenges [[Hungary]]. In [[Poland]], robust convergence is supported by NGEU fund absorption, but demographic headwinds and low labor productivity growth pose threats [[Poland]].

*   **Fiscal Flexibility:** Countries with robust structural frameworks, such as [[Austria]], demonstrate stronger fiscal flexibility and credible budgeting, which enhances their ability to adjust taxes and spending during economic downturns [[Austria]]. However, even fiscally stable countries like [[Poland]] face risks from excessive deficit procedures and rising debt, requiring binding expenditure paths and improved public investment efficiency to maintain ratings [[Poland]]. In [[Hungary]], the suspension of EU funds affects fiscal capacity, highlighting the link between governance reforms and fiscal flexibility [[Hungary]]. In [[Slovakia]], ambitious fiscal consolidation targets are critical to stabilizing debt, but political polarization complicates implementation [[Slovakia]].

*   **Investment Climate:** A stable and reformed regulatory environment attracts foreign direct investment (FDI) and domestic capital. [[Hungary]]'s poor business environment, characterized by regulatory volatility, has led to a significant drop in investment, negatively impacting its credit profile [[Hungary]]. In [[Malta], high dependence on foreign direct investment and regulatory volatility pose risks, while in [[Poland], underdeveloped infrastructure and fossil fuel subsidies distort investment decisions [[Poland]]. In [[Romania]], slow structural reform progress limits the absorption of EU funds, constraining investment-led growth [[Romania]]. In [[Slovakia]], governance concerns and corruption risks hinder the effective utilization of EU funds and foreign investment [[Slovakia]].

*   **Qualitative Overlay (QO) Adjustments:** Under the [[Fitch Ratings]] framework, structural reforms directly influence the Qualitative Overlay applied to the SRM output. Strong institutional capacity, rule of law, and successful implementation of [[Recovery and Resilience Plan]] (RRP) projects can lead to positive QO adjustments (up to +3 notches), reflecting enhanced policy credibility and reduced political risk. Conversely, weak governance, corruption, or stalled reforms can trigger negative QO adjustments, even if quantitative indicators (like GDP growth or debt ratios) appear stable. This is particularly relevant for countries with high [[Climate Vulnerability Signal]] scores, where structural reforms related to the green transition are critical for long-term creditworthiness.

### France
*   **Priority Areas:** Simplification of economic life, renewable energy permitting, labor market segmentation, and digital/green transition support (AI) [[France]].
*   **Focus:** Addressing high corporate debt, rising bankruptcies, and skills shortages that hinder productivity, alongside implementing the Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plan (MTFSP) [[France]].

*   **Focus:** Improving the business environment by reducing state intervention, enhancing transparency, and strengthening the rule of law to unlock suspended EU funds. ### Italy
*   **Priority Areas:** Public administration and judicial efficiency, RRP implementation, and social fairness [[Italy]].
*   **Focus:** Addressing regional disparities, skills mismatches, and high tax evasion to ensure long-term debt sustainability and competitiveness [[Italy]].

### Romania

*   **Focus:** Unlocking EU funds through improved administrative capacity and governance, strengthening labor force participation, and ensuring long-term debt sustainability despite high current deficits [[Romania]].

### Slovakia
*   **Priority Areas:** Diversification of the economy away from automotive sector dependence, governance reforms, and judicial independence [[Slovakia]].
*   **Focus:** Addressing demographic challenges by increasing labor force participation (migrants, elderly), strengthening the Council for Budgetary Responsibility, and improving the investment climate to ensure effective EU fund absorption [[Slovakia]].

## Conclusion

Structural reforms are a cornerstone of modern economic policy, serving as the bridge between short-term stabilization and long-term prosperity. For sovereign credit analysts, the pace, depth, and political feasibility of these reforms are key variables in assessing country risk. The 2025 Country Reports and IMF Article IV Consultations underscore that without decisive action on labor markets, public finance, and institutional governance, economies remain vulnerable to external shocks, fiscal slippage, and stagnating productivity. Key trends include the urgent need to address demographic aging through pension and labor supply reforms, the critical role of digital and green transitions in boosting productivity, and the importance of institutional capacity to ensure policy predictability and continuity. Furthermore, the management of State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs) and contingent liabilities has emerged as a critical component of fiscal sustainability, particularly in countries like Hungary and Romania, where weak governance can lead to significant fiscal risks and rating downgrades.

In the context of advanced rating methodologies, such as those from [[Fitch Ratings]], structural reforms are no longer just background conditions but active variables in the **Qualitative Overlay**. They determine whether a country's quantitative economic metrics (GDP, Debt/GDP) translate into a favorable credit rating or are offset by political and institutional fragility. Therefore, monitoring the implementation of structural reforms is essential for accurate sovereign risk assessment.
