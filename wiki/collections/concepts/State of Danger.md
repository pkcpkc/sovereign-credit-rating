# State of Danger

**State of Danger** refers to a specific legal and constitutional mechanism in Hungary, established under the Fundamental Law, which grants the government expanded emergency powers to bypass normal parliamentary procedures. In the context of the 2025 [[Rule of Law Report]], this mechanism is identified as a primary driver of systemic legal uncertainty, posing significant risks to [[Legal Certainty]], fiscal sustainability, and sovereign creditworthiness.

## Definition and Operational Mechanism

The **State of Danger** allows the government to adopt decrees that have the force of law without immediate parliamentary approval, overriding standard legislative processes. While initially intended for humanitarian crises, its prolonged application has transformed it into a tool for routine governance. Key operational characteristics include:

*   **Override of Parliamentary Acts:** The government can adopt measures that contradict or bypass laws passed by the National Assembly.
*   **Extension of Duration:** The state can be extended indefinitely or for prolonged periods, as seen with the extension until November 2025.
*   **Scope Expansion:** Measures adopted under this state are not limited to crisis management but extend to economic regulations, such as price caps and tax exemptions.

## Impact on Legal Certainty and Sovereign Risk

The European Commission highlights that the extensive use of the **State of Danger** severely undermines **Legal Certainty**, a critical factor for sovereign credit ratings and investment confidence.

### Legal Uncertainty and Business Environment
*   **Legislative Instability:** Frequent use of emergency decrees creates a volatile regulatory environment, making it difficult for businesses to plan long-term investments.
*   **Bypassing Consultation:** Substantive amendments and new regulations are often implemented late-stage, bypassing public consultation and standard legislative scrutiny.
*   **Judicial Review Limitations:** Decrees issued during a state of danger have limited scope for judicial review, reducing checks and balances on executive power.

### Sovereign Credit Rating Factors
From a credit risk perspective, the **State of Danger** contributes to several negative rating factors:
1.  **Political and Geopolitical Risk:** Prolonged states of danger are viewed as a threat to democratic stability and adherence to EU norms, potentially triggering infringement procedures and tensions with EU institutions.
2.  **Policy Predictability:** The use of emergency decrees to implement fiscal and economic policies (e.g., budget amendments, tax changes) reduces policy predictability.
3.  **Fiscal Flexibility vs. Sustainability:** While emergency powers allow rapid fiscal response, they bypass parliamentary oversight, raising concerns about fiscal sustainability and transparency.

## Specific Measures Implemented Under State of Danger

In 2025, the Hungarian government utilized the extended **State of Danger** to implement various economic and social measures unrelated to a humanitarian crisis:
*   **Price Caps:** Implementation of price controls on essential goods and services.
*   **Tax Exemptions:** Introduction of VAT exemptions for specific sectors.
*   **Regulatory Changes:** Adoption of discriminatory regulations affecting foreign investors in strategic sectors, including retail and construction.

These measures have been criticized for distorting market competition and creating barriers to entry for foreign companies, leading to de-investment patterns and ongoing infringement procedures by the European Commission.

## Constitutional and Institutional Context

The **State of Danger** interacts critically with Hungary's constitutional framework, particularly regarding the role of the [[Constitutional Court]] and the [[Judicial Independence]].

*   **Constitutional Court Constraints:** The ability to bypass parliamentary scrutiny limits the Constitutional Court's traditional role in reviewing legislation for constitutionality, as emergency decrees often stand until challenged or repealed.
*   **Parliamentary Oversight:** The ordinary legislative process is circumvented, reducing the National Assembly's ability to debate and amend government proposals. The Fifteenth Amendment, effective 2026, introduces a requirement for parliamentary authorization to extend the state of danger, but this does not retroactively address the legal uncertainty created by current decrees.

## Recommendation for Risk Mitigation

To mitigate sovereign risk and improve credit ratings, stakeholders recommend:
1.  **Limiting Use:** Restricting the **State of Danger** to genuine emergencies and ensuring strict time limits.
2.  **Parliamentary Authorization:** Requiring robust parliamentary debate and approval for any extension or new measures adopted under emergency powers.
3.  **Legal Clarity:** Ensuring that emergency decrees do not undermine fundamental legal principles or create discriminatory regulatory environments for domestic and foreign investors.
4.  **Transparency:** Increasing transparency in the decision-making process for adopting emergency measures, including public consultation and impact assessments.
