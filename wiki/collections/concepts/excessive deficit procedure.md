# Excessive deficit procedure

The **Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP)** is a key mechanism within the EU’s economic governance framework, designed to monitor and correct public finance developments that may pose a risk to the smooth functioning of the Economic and Monetary Union (EMU). Triggered when a Member State’s government deficit exceeds 3% of GDP or its debt exceeds 60% of GDP (or is not diminishing at a satisfactory pace), the EDP requires the Commission and the Council to issue recommendations for fiscal consolidation and structural reforms.

Under the reformed EU economic governance framework, the EDP is increasingly integrated with medium-term fiscal-structural plans, emphasizing not just deficit reduction but also debt trajectory, investment efficiency, and adherence to net expenditure paths. The procedure serves as a critical disciplinary tool to ensure **[[fiscal sustainability]]** and **[[macroeconomic stability]]** across the Eurozone and wider EU, with its activation or closure directly impacting **sovereign credit ratings**, investor confidence, and borrowing costs.

### Significance and Mechanism

The procedure acts as a primary enforcement arm of the **[[Stability and Growth Pact]]**. Its relevance extends beyond simple deficit correction, influencing:
*   **Sovereign Risk Perception**: The status of the EDP is a qualitative factor in sovereign ratings. Closure signals institutional cooperation and policy credibility, while activation can heighten perceived risk and limit **[[fiscal flexibility]]**.
*   **Integration with Structural Reform**: Modern EDP cases are linked to broader structural weaknesses, such as judicial inefficiency, low **[[research and innovation investment]]**, or governance deficits like **[[aggressive tax planning]]** risks.
*   **Net Expenditure Monitoring**: Recent iterations of the procedure (post-2024 reforms) focus heavily on **[[net expenditure growth]]** deviations from Council recommendations, rather than solely on headline deficit figures.
*   **Geopolitical and Defense Spending Adjustments**: The EDP framework now recognizes the need for fiscal flexibility in light of **geopolitical tensions** and increased **defense spending**. The **"national escape clause"** allows for temporary deviations from adjustment paths when justified by security needs or exceptional circumstances, though this requires strict monitoring to prevent permanent fiscal slippage.

### Case Study: France (2024–2025)

The recent application and closure of the EDP for **France** illustrates the procedure's role in managing high-debt economies amidst political and economic headwinds.

*   **Fiscal Context**: In 2024, France recorded a persistently high general government deficit of **5.8% of GDP**, significantly above the 3% reference value. Public debt stood at **113% of GDP**, with interest payments projected to rise to 2.9% of GDP by 2026. These figures triggered concerns regarding **[[debt burden]]** and long-term **[[fiscal sustainability]]**.
*   **Procedural Timeline**:
    *   **2024**: General elections led to political fragmentation, causing delays in budget adoption and complicating fiscal adjustments.
    *   **2024-10-31**: France submitted its medium-term fiscal-structural plan, outlining a seven-year adjustment period.
    *   **2025-01-21**: The Council endorsed France’s medium-term fiscal-structural plan.
    *   **2025-01-21**: The Council recommended the **ending of the excessive deficit situation** in France. This decision was based on the commitment to the fiscal plan, despite the forecast of only slight deficit reduction in 2025 (to 5.6%) and a rise in 2026 (to 5.7%).
*   **Implications for Sovereign Risk**:
    *   The closure of the EDP, contingent on strict adherence to the new fiscal rules, helps maintain **policy credibility**. However, risks remain due to **political instability** and **administrative complexity** that could hinder reform implementation.
    *   The high **[[public expenditure]]** (57.1% of GDP) and rigid spending structures pose ongoing challenges. The effectiveness of the post-EDP period will depend on France’s ability to execute spending reviews and structural reforms (e.g., in the labor market and energy transition) to boost potential growth and improve the debt-to-GDP trajectory.
    *   **Contingent liabilities**, such as those associated with state-owned enterprises like **[[EDF]]**, remain a monitoring focus, linking fiscal policy to broader **[[financial sector health]]** and **[[energy security]]**.

### Case Study: Malta (2024–2025)

The case of **Malta** highlights the EDP's application in economies with low debt but high expenditure growth and structural governance challenges, particularly under the new net expenditure rules.

*   **Fiscal Context**: Despite a low debt-to-GDP ratio of **47.4%** (well below the 60% threshold), Malta faced EDP scrutiny due to **high net expenditure growth** (13.9% in 2024), driven by energy subsidies and capital expenditures (e.g., national airline). The deficit was projected to decline from 3.7% of GDP in 2024 to 2.8% in 2026, yet cumulative net expenditure deviations exceeded Council recommendations.
*   **Procedural Timeline**:
    *   **2024**: Significant concerns raised regarding **net expenditure growth** and weak enforcement of anti-money laundering and aggressive tax planning regulations.
    *   **2025-01-21**: The Council endorsed Malta’s Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan, acknowledging the trajectory toward deficit reduction while noting persistent structural weaknesses.
*   **Key Structural & Governance Factors**:
    *   **Institutional Strength**: The EDP context highlighted threats such as lengthy judicial proceedings (454 days for civil cases) and limited independence of the **[[Malta Fiscal Advisory Council]]**.
    *   **Revenue Vulnerability**: Low tax revenue (26.6% of GDP) and a large **VAT compliance gap** (25.9%) pose risks to **[[fiscal sustainability]]**, alongside risks from **[[aggressive tax planning]]**.
    *   **Long-Term Pressures**: While current debt is manageable, age-related expenditures are projected to rise from 17% to 26% of GDP by 2070, necessitating strict fiscal discipline endorsed under the EDP framework.

### Case Study: Poland (2025 Onwards)

The initiation of the EDP for **Poland** in early 2025 demonstrates the procedure's application to high-growth economies facing acute fiscal deficits driven by **defense spending** and structural investment needs, utilizing the **national escape clause** for temporary flexibility.

*   **Fiscal Context**: Poland recorded a high general government deficit of **6.6% of GDP** in 2024, significantly breaching the 3% reference value. Public debt was projected to rise to **58.0% of GDP** in 2025. The deficit was exacerbated by rising **defense spending** (expected to reach 2.8% of GDP in 2025) and substantial public investment requirements.
*   **Procedural Timeline**:
    *   **2025-01-21**: The Council initiated the **Excessive Deficit Procedure** against Poland and endorsed its **medium-term fiscal-structural plan**. The plan aims to reduce the deficit below 3% by 2028.
    *   **2025-04-30**: Poland submitted its Annual Progress Report on fiscal plan implementation.
    *   **2025-06-30**: Projected operational start of the independent **Polish Fiscal Council**, established to enhance **policy credibility** and **institutional strength**.
*   **Implications for Sovereign Risk**:
    *   **Defense and Geopolitical Factors**: The EDP recognition of Poland’s defense needs allows for a tailored adjustment path. However, the persistence of high deficits poses a threat to **debt sustainability** if growth slows or interest rates remain elevated.
    *   **Structural Challenges**: The EDP closure will depend on Poland’s ability to address **skills shortages**, improve **labor productivity** (currently 67% of EU average), and manage **regional disparities**.
    *   **Fiscal Consolidation Measures**: The plan relies on increasing revenues and curbing expenditure growth, complicated by a complex tax framework and high unit labor costs. The establishment of the Fiscal Council is seen as a strength, enhancing **accountability** and **transparency**.
    *   **External Factors**: Poland’s **current account surplus** and **flexible exchange rate** provide some buffer against external shocks, but reliance on **foreign direct investment** and a net international investment position deficit (-30.8% of GDP) remain risks.

### Key Impacts on Country Risk Analysis

1.  **Sovereign Credit Rating**: The status of the EDP is a qualitative factor in sovereign ratings. Closure can prevent downgrades but does not immediately resolve high debt levels (France) or governance deficits (Malta). Markets view EDP closure as a positive signal of institutional cooperation, provided the medium-term plan is credible. For Poland, the initiation of EDP combined with defense spending increases may lead to rating agency scrutiny regarding long-term debt affordability.
2.  **Fiscal Flexibility**: Countries under or recently exited from EDP have reduced **[[fiscal flexibility]]**. France’s commitment to a seven-year adjustment and Malta’s need to curb net expenditure growth limit their ability to use counter-cyclical fiscal policy during economic slowdowns. Poland’s use of the "national escape clause" provides temporary relief for defense spending but constrains other fiscal buffers.
3.  **Investment and Competitiveness**: The EDP framework encourages alignment of fiscal policy with structural reforms.
    *   For **France**, this includes addressing **[[skills shortages]]** and improving the **[[business environment]]**.
    *   For **Malta**, this involves boosting **[[research and innovation investment]]** (currently 0.64% of GDP) and improving judicial efficiency to counteract high regulatory burdens.
    *   For **Poland**, the EDP context highlights the need to improve **public investment efficiency**, streamline the **regulatory framework**, and accelerate the **energy transition** to maintain competitiveness despite high unit labor costs.
4.  **Debt Sustainability vs. Expenditure Control**: The EDP now distinguishes between countries with high stock risks (debt, e.g., France) and flow risks (expenditure growth, e.g., Malta) or mixed risks (deficit driven by defense/investment, e.g., Poland). In all cases, the procedure mandates a clear path to **[[fiscal sustainability]]**, either through debt reduction trajectories, stabilization of net expenditure relative to potential GDP, or managed adjustment periods for exceptional spending.
5.  **Institutional Credibility**: The effectiveness of the EDP is increasingly tied to the strength of national fiscal institutions. The establishment of independent **Fiscal Councils** (as seen in Malta’s review and Poland’s new council) is viewed positively by markets as it enhances the credibility of fiscal forecasts and enforcement, mitigating some of the risks associated with EDP activation.
