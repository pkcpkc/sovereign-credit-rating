# net expenditure growth

**Net expenditure growth** is a core fiscal metric within the reformed **EU Economic Governance Framework** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1263), serving as the primary reference benchmark for assessing a member state's fiscal discipline. It measures the annual growth rate of net government expenditures, adjusted for specific one-off measures and cyclical factors, to determine compliance with medium-term fiscal-structural plans.

Under the new EU rules, net expenditure growth acts as the central reference trajectory for member states. Governments must align their spending plans with a reference value (typically linked to potential GDP growth and demographic costs) to ensure **debt sustainability**. Deviations from this reference trajectory are generally prohibited unless justified by specific escape clauses or exemptions, such as those for significant security or defence investments, natural disasters, or unemployment-related spending.

## Regulatory Context and Mechanisms

Net expenditure growth is the operational heart of the **Excessive Deficit Procedure** (EDP) reforms. The metric is designed to prevent pro-cyclical fiscal policy by constraining spending growth relative to the economy’s potential. Key mechanisms include:

*   **Reference Trajectory**: A country-specific benchmark that accounts for demographic pressures and energy transition costs.
*   **National Escape Clause**: A legal mechanism allowing temporary deviations from the net expenditure reference value under strict conditions, such as significant security or defence spending, provided that structural reforms accompany the deviation to ensure medium-term debt sustainability.
*   **Cumulative Deviation Monitoring**: The European Commission monitors not just annual growth but also cumulative deviations from the Council recommendations, ensuring that temporary spikes do not derail long-term consolidation paths.

## Comparative Case Studies: Belgium, Malta, and Poland (2025–2029)

The 2025 Country Reports for Belgium, Malta, and Poland illustrate how divergent economic structures, fiscal positions, and geopolitical contexts interact with net expenditure growth constraints under the new EU framework.

### Belgium: Fiscal Deterioration and Defence Spending
The 2025 Country Report for Belgium highlights net expenditure growth as a critical pressure point in the country's fiscal deterioration.

*   **Forecasted Growth vs. Reference**: Belgium's net expenditure growth is forecast to reach **5.0%** in 2025. This figure exceeds the reference trajectory established in its Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP).
*   **National Escape Clause Activation**: The excessive growth rate is legally permitted under the **National Escape Clause** mechanism introduced by Regulation (EU) 2024/1263. Belgium activated this clause specifically to accommodate a sharp increase in **defence spending**, which is projected to rise to 1.7% of GDP in 2025. This mechanism allows for temporary deviations from net expenditure limits while maintaining compliance with the broader debt-reduction roadmap, provided that accompanying structural reforms are implemented.
*   **Fiscal Impact**: Despite the exemption for defence, the high net expenditure growth contributes to a widening structural deficit (projected at 5.4% of GDP in 2025) and an unsustainable **debt burden** trajectory (projected to rise from 104.7% of GDP in 2024 to 126.4% by 2035).
*   **Drivers of Expenditure**: Beyond defence, net expenditure growth is heavily influenced by rigid **ageing costs**, particularly in pensions and long-term care. The **Federal Planning Bureau** and **High Council of Finance** note that without reforms, these demographic pressures will continue to drive net expenditure growth above sustainable levels, eroding fiscal space.

### Malta: High Short-Term Volatility vs. Low Debt Burden
In contrast, Malta presents a case of high short-term net expenditure volatility amidst a favorable debt position.

*   **Record High Growth**: Malta experienced an exceptional **13.9%** net expenditure growth in 2024, driven primarily by temporary energy subsidies and significant capital expenditures related to the national airline and infrastructure.
*   **Cumulative Deviation**: Despite a low government debt ratio (47.4% of GDP in 2024), the cumulative net expenditure deviation for the 2024–2025 period exceeds Council recommendations. This highlights a disconnect between headline debt levels and spending discipline, signaling potential future risks if high expenditure trends persist.
*   **Consolidation Phase**: The government is entering a consolidation phase, with the deficit projected to decline from 3.7% of GDP in 2024 to 2.8% in 2026. This reduction is supported by a decrease in energy subsidies and improved tax revenues, aiming to curb net expenditure growth rates in the medium term.
*   **Long-Term Fiscal Pressures**: Similar to Belgium, Malta faces severe long-term threats from **ageing costs**. Age-related expenditures (pensions and healthcare) are projected to rise from 17% of GDP in 2024 to 26% by 2070—the second-largest increase in the EU. This demographic cliff poses a significant challenge to keeping net expenditure growth within sustainable bounds in the future, necessitating pre-emptive structural reforms.

### Poland: EDP Initiation and Defense-Driven Expenditure
Poland’s situation in the 2025 Country Report underscores the tension between geopolitical imperatives (defense) and fiscal consolidation targets under the new rules.

*   **High Deficit and Rising Debt**: Poland recorded a general government deficit of **6.6% of GDP** in 2024, leading the Council to initiate the **Excessive Deficit Procedure** (EDP) and endorse a Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan aimed at reducing the deficit below 3% by 2028. Public debt is projected to rise to 58.0% of GDP in 2025.
*   **Defense Spending Pressure**: A primary driver of rising public spending and net expenditure growth is high and increasing defense expenditure, expected to reach **2.8% of GDP** in 2025. This substantial outlay, driven by geopolitical tensions and proximity to the war in Ukraine, complicates the fiscal adjustment required to meet EU benchmarks.
*   **Escape Clause Dynamics**: Like Belgium, Poland leverages the **National Escape Clause** to justify deviations from the net expenditure reference trajectory due to security-related spending. However, the scale of this spending, combined with a complex tax framework and legislative volatility, challenges the credibility of the fiscal consolidation path.
*   **Structural Headwinds**: Net expenditure growth in Poland is also pressured by structural issues, including a complex and frequently changing regulatory environment, low labor productivity (67% of EU average), and severe skills shortages. These factors hamper revenue growth, making the compression of net expenditure growth more difficult without severe austerity measures.
*   **Institutional Strengthening**: To enhance oversight of net expenditure growth, Poland established an independent **Fiscal Council** (operational from 2026), aimed at improving ex post evaluation of legislation and monitoring fiscal compliance, although concerns over lobbying transparency and judicial independence persist.

## Impact on Sovereign Credit Ratings and Country Risk

Net expenditure growth serves as a leading indicator for **fiscal sustainability** and directly influences sovereign credit rating assessments through the following channels:

1.  **Policy Credibility and Institutional Strength**:
    *   **Belgium**: Persistent deviations from net expenditure reference values, even if legally excused via escape clauses, signal **policymaking stability** challenges. Discrepancies between government projections and Commission forecasts regarding net expenditure highlight weak budgetary coordination between federal and regional entities, undermining **policy credibility_and_institutional_strength**.
    *   **Malta**: While the Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan provides a clear trajectory, the high regulatory burden and limited independence of the **Malta Fiscal Advisory Council (MFAC)** raise concerns about the enforcement of net expenditure limits. Weak institutional checks may allow spending to drift beyond reference trajectories.
    *   **Poland**: The initiation of the EDP and the reliance on the National Escape Clause for significant defense spending signal heightened **fiscal_performance_and_flexibility** risks. While the establishment of an independent Fiscal Council is a strength, the complexity of the tax framework and legislative volatility deter investment, complicating the sustainability of high net expenditure growth.

2.  **Fiscal Flexibility and Contingent Liabilities**:
    *   High net expenditure growth reduces **fiscal_performance_and_flexibility**, leaving limited room for counter-cyclical policies during economic downturns.
    *   In Malta, contingent liabilities associated with state-owned enterprises (e.g., Enemalta) and energy subsidies contribute to volatile net expenditure figures, increasing the risk of off-budget obligations impacting the fiscal balance.
    *   In Poland, contingent liabilities from banking sector disputes (legacy forex mortgages) and reliance on external funding for infrastructure add uncertainty to the net expenditure outlook.

3.  **Debt Sustainability and Long-Term Structural Risks**:
    *   For countries with high existing debt (like Belgium), uncontrolled net expenditure growth exacerbates **debt_burden**, raising interest payment obligations and crowding out productive investment.
    *   For countries with low debt but high aging costs (like Malta), failure to curb net expenditure growth driven by entitlements threatens long-term **fiscal_sustainability**. Rating agencies view the projected surge in pension and healthcare spending as a critical risk factor that could reverse current favorable debt dynamics.
    *   For Poland, the combination of rising debt (58.0% of GDP), high defense spending, and demographic decline (aging population, low fertility) creates a challenging environment for maintaining sustainable net expenditure growth. The low statutory retirement age for women and special pension schemes further strain long-term fiscal prospects.

## Strategic Implications for Policy Makers

*   **Expenditure Reviews**: To curb excessive net expenditure growth, countries must implement rigorous spending reviews. In Malta, this involves phasing out fossil fuel subsidies and improving tax collection to offset revenue leakage from the VAT compliance gap. In Poland, simplifying the complex tax framework and enhancing tax base efficiency are critical to supporting revenue growth without increasing expenditure pressure.
*   **Structural Reforms**: Addressing the root causes of high net expenditure growth requires structural reforms. For Belgium, Malta, and Poland, this includes pension adjustments (e.g., bonus-malus systems, increasing effective retirement age) and healthcare financing reforms to mitigate the automatic upward pressure on spending from demographic aging. Poland also needs to address skills shortages and improve labor productivity to broaden the economic base.
*   **Coordination and Oversight**: Strengthening the autonomy and resources of independent fiscal councils (e.g., **High Council of Finance** in Belgium, **MFAC** in Malta, and the new **Polish Fiscal Council**) is essential to monitor net expenditure growth accurately and enforce coordination between federal/regional budgets or across government ministries.
*   **Investment vs. Consumption**: Differentiating between growth-enhancing capital expenditures (e.g., renewable energy, digital infrastructure, defense modernization) and consumption is critical. While capital spending can drive short-term net expenditure growth, it must be aligned with long-term productivity gains to ensure debt sustainability. Poland’s progress in clean tech manufacturing offers a potential pathway to offset high defense spending with future growth dividends.
*   **Geopolitical Alignment**: For countries like Poland and Belgium, aligning defense spending increases with EU escape clauses requires clear communication and robust structural reform packages to reassure markets and rating agencies that fiscal anchors remain intact despite higher security expenditures.
