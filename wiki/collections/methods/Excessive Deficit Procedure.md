# Excessive deficit procedure

## Overview

The **Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP)** is a surveillance mechanism within the [[EU Economic Governance Framework]] used by the **European Commission** to monitor and correct fiscal developments in Eurozone and EU Member States. It is triggered when a Member State's deficit exceeds 3% of GDP or when public debt trends are unsustainable. Under the reformed Regulation (EU) 2024/1263, the EDP has evolved to integrate medium-term fiscal-structural planning, allowing for more flexible but strict enforcement of deficit reduction and debt sustainability targets. It serves as a critical institutional safeguard for [[fiscal sustainability]] and [[debt sustainability]], ensuring that excessive fiscal deficits do not undermine the stability of the economic and monetary union.

Recent applications of the EDP highlight its adaptability to complex fiscal landscapes. While **Italy** demonstrated the procedure's role in facilitating negotiated exits from deficit status through a [[Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan]] (MTFSP), and **Malta** illustrates the EDP's focus on structural net expenditure compliance, **Poland** represents a new cohort of countries entering the procedure with high deficits driven by defense spending and structural reforms. Poland’s deficit reached 6.6% of GDP in 2024, leading to the initiation of the EDP in January 2025. This case underscores the EDP's modern function: monitoring not just headline deficits, but the structural trajectory of public spending, leveraging the **National Escape Clause** for justified deviations like defense, and assessing long-term risks such as [[aging population]] pressures on pensions and healthcare.

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

The modernized EDP framework focuses on three core analytical pillars, moving beyond simple deficit thresholds to assess the structural health of public finances:

1.  **Deficit and Debt Sustainability Analysis**:
    *   Assessment of the general government deficit relative to GDP (trigger threshold: 3%).
    *   Evaluation of the public debt trajectory, specifically whether debt is on a declining path or stabilizing at sustainable levels.
    *   Analysis of [[fiscal sustainability]] risks, including projected pressure from ageing populations, interest payments, and the interest-growth differential. In **Malta’s** case, despite low current debt, long-term sustainability is threatened by an aging population: pension spending is projected to rise from 6% to 10.5% of GDP by 2070. In **Poland’s** case, the debt-to-GDP ratio is projected to reach 58.0% in 2025, with affordability managed by growth but threatened by high interest costs. **Italy** faces rising debt service costs despite primary surpluses.

2.  **Net Expenditure Benchmark and Compliance**:
    *   Monitoring of structural net expenditure against a country-specific benchmark derived from the Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP).
    *   Calculation of [[net expenditure growth]] to ensure fiscal adjustment is not offset by revenue windfalls or one-off measures. **Malta** experienced a cumulative net expenditure deviation exceeding Council recommendations in 2024-2025, driven by high energy subsidies. **Poland** utilizes the **National Escape Clause** to account for rising defense expenditure (projected at 2.8% of GDP in 2025), which allows temporary deviations from net expenditure limits while maintaining a credible adjustment path.
    *   In **Italy**, compliance with net expenditure limits was a key factor in the decision to end the procedure.

3.  **Structural Reform Integration**:
    *   The EDP is now tightly coupled with structural reforms. Corrective action is not solely based on deficit reduction but also on implementing reforms that improve [[competitiveness]], [[labour market participation]], and long-term fiscal resilience.
    *   Assessment of the quality of national fiscal frameworks, including the independence and effectiveness of institutions. In **Malta**, concerns were raised regarding the limited independence of the [[Malta Fiscal Advisory Council]]. In **Poland**, the establishment of an independent **Fiscal Council** (operational from 2026) is a key strength enhancing policy credibility, although legislative volatility and complex tax frameworks remain challenges.
    *   Evaluation of reforms addressing specific structural bottlenecks. For **Malta**, this includes addressing [[aggressive tax planning]] and judicial inefficiency. For **Poland**, recommendations focus on improving the vocational education and training (VET) system to address skills shortages, reforming the pension system to address low retirement ages, and enhancing healthcare sustainability.

## Key Metrics & Variables

- **General Government Deficit**: The primary trigger metric. Exceeding 3% of GDP initiates the procedure. In **Belgium** (2024), the deficit was 4.5%. In **Italy**, it was 3.4%. In **Malta**, it was 3.7% (projected to fall to 2.8% by 2026). In **Poland**, the deficit was exceptionally high at 6.6% of GDP in 2024, triggering immediate enhanced surveillance and EDP initiation.
- **Net Expenditure Growth**: A structural metric used to monitor compliance with the fiscal anchor. **Belgium’s** net expenditure growth was forecast at 5.0% for 2025. **Malta** recorded 13.9% growth in 2024. For **Poland**, net expenditure dynamics are scrutinized in the context of the National Escape Clause, as defense spending constitutes a significant portion of the fiscal adjustment narrative.
- **Public Debt-to-GDP Ratio**: Assessed for sustainability. **Belgium’s** debt was 104.7% in 2024. **Italy’s** debt is projected to rise to 136.7% in 2025. **Malta’s** debt remains low at 47.4%. **Poland’s** public debt is projected to rise to 58.0% of GDP in 2025, with concerns over rising interest costs despite manageable affordability due to local-currency debt and growth.
- **Primary Balance**: A key indicator of fiscal effort excluding interest payments. **Italy** achieved a primary surplus of 0.4% of GDP in 2024.
- **Ageing Cost Pressure**: A qualitative and quantitative overlay measuring the projected increase in pension and long-term care costs. **Malta** faces severe long-term pressure (spending to rise from 17% to 26% of GDP by 2070). **Poland** faces significant sustainability risks due to a low statutory retirement age for women (60 years) and special pension schemes, necessitating pension system reforms.
- **Tax Compliance and Revenue Structure**: A variable assessing the robustness of the tax base. **Malta** has a large VAT compliance gap (25.9%). **Poland** suffers from a complex tax framework (ranking last in the EU for complexity) and a low tax-to-GDP ratio, which hampers innovation and revenue stability.
- **Institutional Strength Indicators**: Metrics such as judicial efficiency and fiscal advisory independence. **Malta** scores poorly on regulatory burden. **Poland** has seen recent improvements with the establishment of an independent Fiscal Council and digitalization of public services, though legislative volatility and perceived erosion of judicial independence remain threats to policy credibility.

## Application

1. **Surveillance and Trigger Assessment**:
   The European Commission monitors Member States' budgetary developments. If the deficit exceeds 3% of GDP or debt sustainability concerns arise, the Commission assesses whether the [[Excessive Deficit Procedure]] should be initiated or maintained. For **Malta**, the Commission scrutinizes structural causes like [[aggressive tax planning]]. For **Belgium** and **Italy**, the trigger was direct deficit non-compliance. For **Poland**, the 6.6% deficit in 2024 led to the Council initiating the EDP in January 2025. The assessment includes reviewing the Member State's [[Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan]] and underlying structural weaknesses, such as **Poland’s** low labor productivity (67% of EU average) and skills shortages, and **Malta’s** judicial inefficiencies.

2. **Compliance Monitoring and Escape Clause Evaluation**:
   The Commission evaluates compliance with the net expenditure benchmark. If a Member State deviates, the Commission verifies the nature of the deviation. In **Malta’s** case, deviations were driven by energy subsidies. In **Poland**, the **National Escape Clause** is actively used to accommodate high and rising defense spending (2.8% of GDP in 2025), ensuring that these necessary expenditures do not derail the overall fiscal consolidation path. The Commission also monitors institutional reforms, such as strengthening the independence of the [[Malta Fiscal Advisory Council]] and the operational start of the **Polish Fiscal Council** in 2026.

3. **Recommendations and Corrective Action**:
   Based on the analysis, the Commission issues a Recommendation to the Council to endorse the MTP or impose stricter corrective measures. For **Malta**, recommendations focus on broadening the tax base and closing the VAT compliance gap. For **Italy**, the Council endorsed the MTFSP, ending the EDP. For **Poland**, the Council endorsed the medium-term fiscal-structural plan aiming to reduce the deficit below 3% by 2028. Recommendations also include structural reforms: in **Poland**, this involves accelerating the green transition, improving vocational training to address skills shortages, and reforming the pension and healthcare systems to ensure long-term sustainability.

4. **Review and Adjustment**:
   The procedure involves continuous review through Annual Progress Reports and Country Reports. If deviations occur or reforms are delayed, the Commission may recalibrate the fiscal target. The effectiveness of national institutions, such as the [[Federal Planning Bureau]] in Belgium, the [[Parliamentary Budget Office]] in Italy, and the **Polish Fiscal Council**, is monitored. In **Poland**, the transition involves sustained effort in implementing EU funds (Recovery and Resilience Facility and Cohesion Policy) to support growth and reform, while addressing regional disparities and demographic decline. The Commission tracks **Poland’s** progress in fulfilling milestones, with 25% completed by mid-2025.
