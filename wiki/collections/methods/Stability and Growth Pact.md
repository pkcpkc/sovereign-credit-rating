# Stability and Growth Pact

## Overview

The **Stability and Growth Pact (SGP)** is the core framework for fiscal policy coordination within the **European Union**, implemented under the broader [[European Semester Framework]]. Administered by the **European Commission** and the Council, the SGP aims to ensure member states maintain sound public finances, thereby safeguarding price stability, sustainable growth, and economic convergence. It sets quantitative reference values for government deficits and debt, requiring member states to submit [[Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plans]] to demonstrate fiscal sustainability. The methodology integrates both preventive arms (fiscal rules and structural reforms) and corrective arms (Excessive Deficit Procedure) to manage [[fiscal sustainability]] and [[debt sustainability]]. Recent applications, such as those for Italy in 2025 and Malta in 2025, highlight the framework's role in endorsing country-specific adjustment paths while addressing structural vulnerabilities like productivity stagnation, demographic aging, and tax base resilience.

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

1.  **Fiscal Compliance & Treaty Reference Values**:
    *   **Deficit Rule**: Government deficit must not exceed 3% of GDP. Persistent breaches trigger the Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP). For instance, Italy's 2024 outturn of 3.4% was assessed against this threshold, considering the phase-out of one-off measures.
    *   **Debt Rule**: Gross government debt must not exceed 60% of GDP, or must be diminishing at a satisfactory pace (typically defined as reducing by 1/20th of the excess annually).
    *   **Country-Specific Adjustment Paths**: Member states commit to medium-term budgetary objectives (MTOs) based on country-specific parameters, adjusted for economic cycles and demographic costs. Italy’s seven-year adjustment path under its MTFSP exemplifies this pillar, aiming to ensure debt sustainability despite rising pension costs. Conversely, Malta’s low debt level (47.4% in 2024) provides fiscal space, though its high net expenditure growth (13.9% in 2024) requires monitoring to ensure consolidation credibility.

2.  **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Planning**:
    *   Under the reformed SGP, member states submit [[Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plans]] covering a four-year horizon. These plans outline nominal net expenditure limits and structural reforms.
    *   The analysis focuses on **fiscal flexibility** and the credibility of consolidation measures (e.g., spending cuts, revenue enhancements) to return to compliance.
    *   Integration of **green budgeting** and climate neutrality targets into fiscal planning is increasingly scrutinized, aligning fiscal adjustments with green transition investments (e.g., RRP funding). In Malta, this includes assessing the impact of high fossil fuel subsidies (1.6% of GDP) and low renewable energy uptake on long-term fiscal and environmental sustainability.

3.  **Debt Sustainability Monitoring**:
    *   Utilizes the [[Debt Sustainability Monitor]] to assess the viability of debt trajectories under various economic scenarios.
    *   Evaluates **primary balance requirements** to stabilize or reduce debt ratios, considering interest expenditure burdens and growth differentials. High interest payments (e.g., ~3.9% of GDP in Italy) are critical factors in this assessment.
    *   Considers **contingent liabilities**, such as banking sector support needs and climate adaptation costs. In Italy, the "doom loop" risk between sovereign debt and bank exposure is a key variable. In Malta, risks stem from potential costs associated with state-owned enterprise (Enemalta) subsidies and energy sector interventions, although strong bank capitalization currently mitigates immediate recapitalization needs.

4.  **Qualitative Overlays & Institutional Strength**:
    *   Assesses **policy credibility** and **institutional strength** (e.g., independence of fiscal institutions like the Parliamentary Budget Office in Italy or the Malta Fiscal Advisory Council).
    *   Evaluates **policymaking stability** and accountability mechanisms. For Malta, this includes assessing the limited independence of the MFAC, lengthy judicial proceedings (avg. 454 days for civil/commercial cases), and concerns regarding corruption perceptions (CPI score 46/100) and aggressive tax planning risks.
    *   Adjusts for **geopolitical and political stability** risks that may impact fiscal implementation. For Italy, this includes socio-economic divergence between North and South. For Malta, this includes regulatory burden (OECD PMR indicator second weakest in EU) and social dialogue inconsistencies.

## Key Metrics & Variables

-   **General Government Deficit**: The net borrowing requirement of the general government sector, expressed as a percentage of GDP. Critical threshold: 3%. Malta’s deficit is projected to decline from 3.7% in 2024 to 2.8% in 2026.
-   **Gross Government Debt**: Total outstanding nominal liabilities of the general government sector, expressed as a percentage of GDP. Critical threshold: 60%. Malta’s debt ratio was 47.4% in 2024, well below the EU average and the treaty reference value.
-   **Structural Balance (Primary Balance)**: The budget balance adjusted for the economic cycle and one-off measures, used to assess underlying fiscal effort. A return to primary surplus (e.g., 0.4% of GDP in Italy 2024) is a positive indicator, though driven by specific phase-outs.
-   **Nominal Net Expenditure Growth**: The growth rate of government net expenditure excluding cyclical support and one-off measures, compared against the medium-term plan limits. Malta recorded high growth (13.9% in 2024) driven by energy subsidies and capital expenditures.
-   **Debt-to-GDP Ratio Trajectory**: Projected path of debt over the medium to long term (e.g., to 2070), incorporating demographic pressures and interest-growth differentials.
-   **Age-Related Spending**: Public expenditure related to pensions, healthcare, and long-term care, often projected as a percentage of GDP to assess long-term fiscal pressure. Italy’s high pension spending (16.1% of GDP) is significant. In Malta, age-related expenditure is projected to rise from 17% of GDP in 2024 to 26% in 2070, with pension spending potentially doubling from 6% to 10.5% of GDP.
-   **Fiscal Institutional Strength**: Qualitative assessment of the independence and effectiveness of fiscal councils (e.g., PBO, MFAC) and budgetary frameworks. Weak enforcement of anti-money laundering and limited MFAC effectiveness in Malta pose risks.
-   **Productivity and R&D Investment**: Structural metrics assessing the tax base and growth prospects. Italy shows half the EU average in private R&D intensity. Malta has very low R&D intensity (0.64% of GDP), hindering innovation-led growth.
-   **Financial Sector Health**: Metrics including capital adequacy ratios and non-performing loans. Italy maintains a 20% capital adequacy ratio. Malta’s banking sector exhibits high capital adequacy (CET1 ratio 20.2%) and low NPLs (1.8%), though high concentration in the top 5 MFIs (>75% of assets) is a vulnerability.
-   **Tax Base Resilience & Compliance**: Assesses the breadth and stability of revenue sources. Malta’s tax revenue is low (26.6% of GDP vs EU 39%) with high reliance on corporate income tax and a large VAT compliance gap (25.9%), indicating vulnerability to aggressive tax planning and shocks.

## Application

1.  **Assessment of Fiscal Plans & Compliance**:
    *   Evaluate the member state’s **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** against the 3% deficit and 60% debt thresholds. For Malta, this involves verifying the realism of consolidation measures (e.g., reducing energy subsidies) and their impact on the **structural balance** amidst high net expenditure deviations.
    *   Verify the realism of consolidation measures (e.g., phasing out Superbonus in Italy) and their impact on the **structural balance**.
    *   Check for adherence to **nominal net expenditure limits** and the progress on structural reforms, such as digitalizing public administration (Malta is an EU leader in e-government) or improving procurement transparency.

2.  **Debt Sustainability & Scenario Analysis**:
    *   Apply the **Debt Sustainability Monitor** to project debt dynamics under baseline and stress scenarios.
    *   Analyze the impact of **age-related spending** increases and **interest expenditure** on the debt burden. For Malta, the surge in projected healthcare (5.1% to 7.2% of GDP by 2070) and pension costs represents the second-largest increase in the EU, requiring proactive fiscal adjustment despite current low debt levels.
    *   Assess risks from **contingent liabilities** (e.g., banking sector exposure, state-guaranteed loans, Enemalta subsidies in Malta) and external vulnerabilities (e.g., energy dependency, high gross external assets/liabilities in Malta indicating high financialization).

3.  **Qualitative Adjustment & Council Recommendation**:
    *   Apply qualitative overlays based on **institutional strength**, **policy credibility**, and **geopolitical stability**. For Malta, this involves accounting for judicial inefficiency, corruption perceptions, and regulatory burden. For Italy, this includes accounting for regional disparities.
    *   Consider external factors like **economic diversity**, **growth prospects** (often hampered by low productivity or skills mismatches in Malta), and **external liquidity** (Malta has a strong net international investment position of 100.3% of GDP).
    *   Formulate **Country-Specific Recommendations (CSRs)** via the [[European Semester Framework]], potentially initiating or closing an **Excessive Deficit Procedure** if thresholds are breached and corrective actions are insufficient. In Malta's case, the Council endorsed its MTFSP in January 2025, ending the focus on deficit correction and shifting to structural reform implementation via the [[Recovery and Resilience Facility]].
