# Financial Stability Assessment Program (FSAP)

## Overview

The **Financial Stability Assessment Program (FSAP)** is a core instrument of the [[International Monetary Fund]] (IMF), conducted in cooperation with the [[World Bank]] (IBRD). It serves as the international standard for assessing financial sector resilience and vulnerabilities. The program provides a comprehensive diagnosis of a country's financial sector, identifying potential vulnerabilities and recommending necessary structural and regulatory enhancements to strengthen the stability framework. Unlike sovereign credit ratings which focus primarily on default risk, the FSAP focuses on the integrity and resilience of the financial system itself, including the banking sector, insurance, securities markets, and the broader financial safety net.

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

The FSAP methodology is comprehensive and multi-layered, focusing on the following core analytical components:

1.  **Diagnostic Review of the Financial System**: A broad assessment of the financial sector's structure, size, and importance to the economy. This includes evaluating the legal and institutional framework, market infrastructure, and the role of financial sector institutions.
2.  **Supervision and Regulatory Framework Assessment**: An in-depth review of the supervisory authorities (e.g., [[National Bank of Slovakia]]) against international standards and best practices (such as those from the [[Basel Committee on Banking Supervision]]). This assesses the effectiveness of supervision, regulatory coverage, and the independence of supervisory bodies.
3.  **Financial System Stress Testing**: The core quantitative component of the FSAP. It involves modeling the impact of severe but plausible adverse economic scenarios on the balance sheets of financial institutions.
    *   **Banking Sector Stress Tests**: Evaluating capital adequacy under shock scenarios (e.g., recession, interest rate spikes, asset price crashes).
    *   **Macroprudential Analysis**: Assessing system-wide risks, including credit cycles, real estate bubbles, and external vulnerabilities.
4.  **Resolution and Crisis Management Framework**: Evaluating the authorities' ability to resolve failing financial institutions in an orderly manner, minimizing systemic disruption and taxpayer costs. This includes assessing deposit insurance schemes and lender-of-last-resort facilities.
5.  **Cross-Sectoral Spillovers**: Analyzing linkages between different financial sectors (e.g., banks to non-bank financial institutions) and the real economy to identify contagion risks.

## Key Metrics & Variables

- **Capital Adequacy Ratios (CAR)**: A primary metric for bank resilience, measuring the bank's capital in relation to its risk-weighted assets. The FSAP assesses whether banks hold capital well above regulatory minima to absorb losses during stress scenarios.
- **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)**: Measures a bank's ability to meet short-term liquidity needs. High LCRs (e.g., > 200%) indicate strong liquidity buffers.
- **Non-Performing Loans (NPLs)**: The percentage of loans in default or close to default. Low NPLs are a key indicator of current asset quality and credit risk management effectiveness.
- **Commercial Real Estate (CRE) Exposure**: The proportion of bank loans tied to real estate, particularly office and commercial segments. High exposure is a key vulnerability indicator, as these assets are sensitive to economic downturns and demographic shifts.
- **Household Debt Levels**: The ratio of household debt to disposable income or GDP. High levels combined with variable-rate mortgages create refinancing risks during interest rate hikes.
- **Net International Investment Position (NIIP)**: While primarily an external sector metric, the FSAP considers external debt sustainability and currency mismatches within the financial sector.
- **Macroprudential Policy Tools**: The existence and usage of tools such as countercyclical capital buffers (CCyB), loan-to-value (LTV) limits, and debt-to-income (DTI) ratios to mitigate systemic risk.

## Application

1.  **Mission Preparation and Data Collection**: IMF and World Bank staff engage with national authorities (e.g., [[Ministry of Finance]], [[National Bank of Slovakia]]) to collect detailed data on the financial sector, regulatory frameworks, and economic indicators. This phase includes preliminary diagnostics and identification of key vulnerabilities.
2.  **On-Site Assessment and Stress Testing**:
    *   **Supervisory Review**: Inspectors review supervisory processes, legal frameworks, and compliance with international standards.
    *   **Stress Test Execution**: Banks and financial institutions are subjected to severe adverse scenarios. For example, in [[Slovakia]], tests assessed resilience to shocks in the **commercial real estate (CRE)** sector and **mortgage refixation** risks. The results determine if capital buffers are sufficient to absorb projected losses without requiring state support.
3.  **Report Drafting and Recommendations**: Staff draft a comprehensive FSAP report highlighting strengths, vulnerabilities, and gaps. Key recommendations often include strengthening macroprudential frameworks (e.g., adopting positive neutral countercyclical capital buffers), enhancing resolution frameworks, and addressing specific sectoral vulnerabilities like CRE exposure.
4.  **Peer Review and Executive Board Consideration**: The FSAP report undergoes internal peer review within the IMF and World Bank. In some cases, findings are discussed with the IMF Executive Board, especially if they have significant implications for macroeconomic stability and IMF lending advice.
5.  **Implementation Follow-up**: The FSAP is not a one-off event. The IMF monitors the country's progress in implementing recommended reforms, often integrating FSAP findings into subsequent [[Article IV Consultation]]s and [[Debt Sustainability Analysis]] (DSA) exercises.
