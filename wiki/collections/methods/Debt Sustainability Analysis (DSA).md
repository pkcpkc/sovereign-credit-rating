# Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA)

## Overview

Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) is a core analytical framework employed by the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** to assess the likelihood that a country will face difficulties in meeting its financial obligations. It is a mandatory component of the [[Article IV Consultation]] process, providing a probabilistic assessment of debt sustainability under baseline and alternative scenarios. The analysis evaluates both internal debt (domestic currency) and external debt (foreign currency), focusing on the trajectory of the debt-to-GDP ratio, primary balances, and interest-rate growth differentials. The goal is to identify vulnerabilities to shocks and determine whether current fiscal policies are sufficient to stabilize or reduce debt levels over the medium to long term. 

Recent applications highlight the framework's versatility across different economic structures. The 2025 consultation for **Hungary** emphasizes the integration of geopolitical risks, rule of law considerations affecting funding access (e.g., EU funds), and the sovereign-financial sector nexus. Conversely, the 2024 consultation for **Malta** illustrates how DSA applies to advanced economies within the Eurozone, where external financing constraints are minimal due to currency adoption, but fiscal sustainability is threatened by structural spending pressures (e.g., energy subsidies) and demographic trends. The 2025 consultation for **Slovakia** further refines the DSA for Eurozone members facing significant fiscal slippage, highlighting the critical role of demographic aging costs and the resilience of banking sectors against commercial real estate (CRE) vulnerabilities. For Slovakia, the DSA identified a "moderate" overall risk of sovereign stress but flagged a "high" long-term risk driven by aging-related costs, necessitating an ambitious four-year fiscal consolidation plan.

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

The DSA methodology relies on a combination of quantitative modeling and qualitative judgment to evaluate sovereign risk:

1.  **Quantitative Projections and Stochastic Analysis**:
    *   **Baseline Scenario**: Projects debt dynamics based on current policies and macroeconomic forecasts (GDP growth, interest rates, inflation). For **Hungary**, the baseline projected debt rising to ~79% of GDP by 2030 under current policies. For **Malta**, the baseline indicates debt stabilization at ~50% of GDP, well below the EU ceiling, supported by low interest rates relative to growth. For **Slovakia**, the baseline projects public debt rising to 74.3% of GDP by 2030, reflecting the impact of the recent fiscal deficit widening to 5.8% of GDP in 2024.
    *   **Stress Tests**: Applies shock scenarios to key variables (e.g., growth slowdowns, interest rate spikes, currency depreciation) to test resilience. Specific tests include modeling the fiscal impact of suspended EU funds (€19 billion for Hungary) and trade shocks. For Malta, stress tests consider the fiscal impact of phasing out fixed-energy price policies and the potential costs of climate transition. For **Slovakia**, stress tests incorporate the risk of slower EU fund disbursements due to governance concerns and the fiscal impact of potential automotive sector downturns.
    *   **Stochastic DSA**: Uses historical data to generate probability distributions for debt outcomes, estimating the likelihood of debt distress or crossing predefined thresholds. The IMF assigns a risk of sovereign stress category (e.g., "moderate" for Hungary and Slovakia; "low" for Malta) based on these probabilities.

2.  **Qualitative Assessment and Institutional Strengths**:
    *   Evaluates **policy credibility**, institutional strength, and the capacity for fiscal adjustment. This includes assessing the impact of governance reforms and rule of law concerns on market confidence and funding access. In Malta, the assessment considers the political sensitivity of energy price reforms and the commitment to fiscal consolidation under the new EU framework. For **Slovakia**, the assessment heavily weighs the credibility of the Council for Budgetary Responsibility and the feasibility of the 4-year consolidation plan, while noting risks from political polarization and judicial independence concerns.
    *   Assesses **financial sector health** and its nexus with sovereign debt (e.g., bank holdings of government bonds). In Hungary, the DSA highlights the "sovereign-bank nexus" where well-capitalized banks hold significant sovereign debt. In Malta, the focus is on the concentration of bank loans in real estate (70% of portfolio) and the risk of a sectoral downturn impacting bank capital, thereby creating contingent liabilities for the sovereign. For **Slovakia**, the DSA notes the banking sector's high capital adequacy and liquidity buffers but identifies vulnerabilities in the commercial real estate (CRE) sector and residential mortgage refixation risks, recommending tighter macroprudential frameworks.
    *   Considers **external factors** such as global liquidity conditions, trade tensions, geoeconomic fragmentation, and the potential cancellation of external grants or loans. For Malta, the strong Net International Investment Position (NIIP) of ~95% of GDP provides a robust buffer against external shocks, mitigating the need for complex external debt sustainability modeling compared to emerging markets. For **Slovakia**, despite a negative NIIP (-53.5% of GDP), Eurozone membership ensures ample liquidity access, though high gross external debt (~100% of GDP) remains a monitoring point.

3.  **Debt Dynamics Decomposition**:
    *   Analyzes the drivers of debt changes: the primary balance, interest-rate growth differential, valuation effects, and other factors (e.g., asset sales, local government borrowing).
    *   Specifically accounts for **contingent liabilities**, such as off-balance sheet financing vehicles, state-owned enterprise (SOE) guarantees, and potential bank recapitalization needs. Hungary has significant contingent liabilities, including **14% of GDP in guarantees**. For Malta, contingent liabilities are linked to potential real estate market corrections and government guarantees. For **Slovakia**, contingent liabilities include potential banking sector support costs if CRE non-performing loans rise and SOE liabilities, although the strong banking sector reduces immediate recapitalization risk.
    *   Incorporates **long-term fiscal sustainability pressures**, particularly demographic trends. The Slovakia DSA explicitly models aging-related costs, which are projected to increase fiscal burdens by 5.6% of GDP by 2050, highlighting a gap between medium-term consolidation plans and long-term structural needs.

## Key Metrics & Variables

- **General Government Gross Debt/GDP**: The primary indicator of debt burden. In the case of France, this reached **113.1% of GDP in 2024**. For **Hungary**, public debt reached **73.5% of GDP in 2024** and is projected to rise to ~79% by 2030 under baseline policies. For **Malta**, debt is rising marginally to ~50% of GDP, remaining well below the EU's 60% ceiling. For **Slovakia**, public debt stood at **58% of GDP in 2024** and is projected to rise to 74.3% by 2030 under baseline policies, driven by the large fiscal deficit. Sustainability is threatened if the ratio remains on an upward trajectory without credible consolidation plans.
- **Primary Fiscal Balance**: The fiscal balance excluding interest payments. Persistent primary deficits exacerbate debt dynamics. Hungary’s primary balance has improved, nearing surplus, but the IMF recommends a structural primary surplus of around **1.75% of GDP** in the medium term to ensure sustainability. For Malta, the primary balance is pressured by energy subsidies (accounting for 20% of the fiscal deficit), with a target to reduce the structural deficit to 2.6% of GDP by 2027. For **Slovakia**, the primary balance was significantly negative in 2024, with the IMF endorsing a plan to reduce the overall deficit to 2.2% of GDP by 2028, prioritizing expenditure-based savings.
- **Interest Rate - Growth Differential (r - g)**: A critical driver of automatic debt adjustment. If interest rates on new and outstanding debt exceed nominal GDP growth, debt tends to rise mechanically unless offset by primary surpluses. High risk premia in Hungary contribute to elevated financing costs. In Malta, low interest rates relative to growth support debt stability. For **Slovakia**, Eurozone membership ensures access to lower financing costs, but the rising interest burden relative to the primary balance remains a concern.
- **Net International Investment Position (NIIP)**: For external debt sustainability, the NIIP measures a country's net claim on the rest of the world. France’s NIIP was **-20.3% of GDP**, while Hungary’s NIIP was **-36.3% of GDP in 2024**, indicating significant external liabilities. In contrast, Malta has a large positive NIIP of **~95% of GDP**, providing a strong external buffer. **Slovakia** has a negative NIIP of **-53.5% of GDP**, but this is improving due to strong GDP growth and high inflation, with high FDI inflows mitigating risks of sudden capital outflows.
- **Debt Maturity and Currency Composition**: The proportion of debt denominated in domestic currency and long-term maturities reduces immediate refinancing risks. However, Hungary faces rising exchange rate risk due to a rapid growth in FX debt (over **30% of total debt**). For Malta, adoption of the **Euro** eliminates exchange rate risk and rollover risk for sovereign debt, fundamentally altering the DSA landscape by removing external financing constraints. For **Slovakia**, Eurozone membership eliminates exchange rate risk, and debt has a long average residual maturity (approx. 8 years), reducing refinancing risk.
- **Fiscal Space and Tax-to-GDP Ratio**: High tax-to-GDP ratios limit the ability to raise revenue for consolidation. In Hungary, reliance on consumption-based taxes insulates revenues less from investment weakness. For Malta, strong revenue from corporate income tax supports fiscal space, though reliance on specific sectors (tourism, gaming) poses concentration risks. For **Slovakia**, a broad revenue base with a high tax-to-GDP ratio (over 40%) provides fiscal flexibility, though the IMF cautions against over-reliance on revenue-based consolidation measures.
- **Contingent Liabilities**: Potential future obligations from local governments, state-owned enterprises, or bank bailouts. Hungary has significant contingent liabilities, including **14% of GDP in guarantees** and risks from off-balance sheet financing vehicles. In Malta, contingent liabilities are tied to the high concentration of bank loans in real estate (70% of portfolio), which could require sovereign support in a downturn. For **Slovakia**, contingent liabilities are linked to potential banking sector support costs if CRE/NPLs rise and SOE liabilities.
- **Gross Financing Needs (GFN)**: Measures the refinancing pressure. Hungary faces high GFN averaging **17% of GDP** through 2030. For Malta, GFN is less acute due to deep Eurozone capital markets, but fiscal consolidation efforts aim to reduce dependency on external borrowing for deficit financing. For **Slovakia**, GFN is manageable due to Eurozone integration, but the large deficit implies sustained borrowing needs until consolidation targets are met.
- **External Liquidity and Reserves**: Adequate foreign exchange reserves (e.g., Hungary’s ~110-112% of IMF ARA metric) and current account surpluses provide buffers against external shocks. Malta maintains a current account surplus (projected at 6.3% of GDP in 2024) and large external assets. For **Slovakia**, the current account deficit widened to 2.7% of GDP in 2024, but Eurozone membership ensures ample liquidity access.
- **Real Estate Exposure and Financial Sector Health**: A critical qualitative metric in the DSA for economies with concentrated banking sectors. In Malta, banks' 70% exposure to real estate requires monitoring via macroprudential buffers (e.g., sectoral systemic risk buffer) to prevent systemic shocks from translating into sovereign credit events. For **Slovakia**, vulnerabilities in the commercial real estate (CRE) sector, specifically the office segment, and residential mortgage refixation risks are key monitoring points, despite the sector's overall resilience.
- **Demographic and Structural Risks**: Long-term structural pressures on fiscal sustainability. For **Slovakia**, aging-related costs are projected to increase fiscal burdens by 5.6% of GDP by 2050, highlighting the need for structural reforms in pensions and healthcare beyond medium-term consolidation. Dependence on the automotive sector also poses economic diversity risks.

## Application

1.  **Data Collection and Macroeconomic Forecasting**:
    *   Collect historical data on debt stock, flows, interest rates, exchange rates, and GDP.
    *   Establish a macroeconomic baseline forecast. For **Hungary**, this involved projecting modest growth (0.7% in 2025) driven by consumption, with inflation decelerating to 3% by 2027. The forecast must account for specific risks like the suspension of **€19 billion in EU funds** and geoeconomic fragmentation. For **Malta**, the forecast incorporates projected 4% medium-term growth, driven by domestic demand and service exports, while accounting for capacity constraints (labor shortages, infrastructure). For **Slovakia**, the forecast projects growth moderating to 1.8% in 2025 due to fiscal consolidation and weak external demand, with inflation temporarily rising to 4.1% in 2025 before returning to the 2% target by early 2027. The forecast accounts for the government change in late 2024 and the initiation of the Excessive Deficit Procedure (EDP).

2.  **Debt Dynamics Projection and Scenario Analysis**:
    *   Calculate the projected debt-to-GDP path under the baseline policy assumption.
    *   Run stress tests:
        *   *Growth Shock*: Assess impact of output stagnation or contraction (e.g., Hungary's Q1 2025 contraction of 0.4%). For **Slovakia**, assess the impact of automotive sector slowdowns and weak demand from Germany.
        *   *Interest Rate Shock*: Increase borrowing costs to reflect tightening global conditions and high risk premia.
        *   *Fiscal Slippage*: Model deviations from planned primary balances. For **Slovakia**, model the risk of insufficient expenditure-side savings to meet the 2.2% deficit target by 2028.
        *   *External Shocks*: Model the fiscal impact of lost EU funding or trade tariffs (e.g., US tariffs). For Malta, this includes modeling the fiscal impact of phasing out energy subsidies and the potential costs of climate transition. For **Slovakia**, assess risks from slower EU fund disbursements due to governance concerns.
    *   Assess the probability of debt crossing critical thresholds and assign a risk of sovereign stress rating (e.g., "moderate" for Hungary and Slovakia; "low" for Malta).

3.  **Qualitative Adjustment and Policy Recommendation**:
    *   Integrate qualitative factors: Assess the credibility of the **Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plan (MTFSP)**, the political feasibility of reforms (e.g., elections in 2026), and institutional safeguards. For Malta, this involves evaluating the commitment to the new EU fiscal framework and the roadmap for Corporate Income Tax (CIT) reform aligned with EU Pillar II. For **Slovakia**, the IMF evaluated the ambitious 4-year consolidation plan, emphasizing the need for expenditure rationalization (reducing subsidies, targeting social spending) over further revenue hikes, and the importance of strengthening governance and judicial independence to ensure EU fund absorption.
    *   Evaluate the **sovereign-financial sector nexus**: Check if bank profitability issues or corporate sector risks (e.g., FX exposure) could force sovereign support. In Hungary, the DSA highlighted the need to monitor banks' aggregate short FX positions and housing market overvaluation. In Malta, the DSA emphasizes monitoring real estate sector risks, recommending tighter macroprudential policies (raising sectoral systemic risk buffers) to mitigate the risk of bank losses impacting sovereign finances. For **Slovakia**, the DSA notes the banking sector's resilience but recommends strengthening the macroprudential framework, including adopting a positive neutral countercyclical capital buffer, to address CRE and mortgage refixation risks.
    *   Synthesize findings to recommend policy actions. For **Hungary**, the IMF recommended a cumulative fiscal adjustment of ~2% of GDP over 2025-2028, phasing out distortive price/margin caps, and implementing structural reforms to boost productivity and unlock EU funds. For **France**, the IMF recommended a frontloaded structural fiscal effort of 1.1% of GDP in 2026. For **Malta**, the IMF recommended phasing out fixed-energy price policies to free up fiscal space for investment in productivity and green transition, while strengthening AML/CFT frameworks and judicial reforms to maintain investor confidence. For **Slovakia**, the IMF endorsed the consolidation plan but urged additional expenditure-based measures and structural reforms to diversify the economy away from automotive dependence and address long-term demographic pressures.
