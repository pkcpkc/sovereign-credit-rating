# Corruption Perceptions Index

## Overview

The Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) is an index published annually by [[Transparency International]] that ranks countries by their perceived levels of public sector corruption. It serves as a key indicator of the integrity of the public administration and is widely used by investors, policymakers, and international institutions to assess [[Rule of Law]] and institutional strength. The index aggregates data from multiple independent sources to provide a composite score, typically ranging from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean). The CPI is frequently cited in broader rule of law assessments, such as the [[European Commission]]'s [[Rule of Law Report]], to contextualize the effectiveness of anti-corruption frameworks and judicial independence in member states like [[Poland]] and [[Malta]].

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

The CPI relies on the aggregation of existing data from various reputable sources (such as the World Bank, the World Economic Forum, and the Varieties of Democracy Institute) rather than primary data collection. The methodology focuses on:

- **Perception of Corruption**: Measures the extent to which corruption is perceived to exist among public officials and politicians. In contexts like [[Poland]], this perception is influenced by high-profile investigations and the operational status of bodies like the [[European Public Prosecutor's Office]] (EPPO).
- **Public Sector Integrity**: Assesses the robustness of anti-corruption frameworks. This includes evaluating the independence of the [[Office of the Attorney General]], the effectiveness of bodies like the [[Central Anti-Corruption Bureau]] (now transitioning to a new Central Office for Combating Corruption in [[Poland]]), and the implementation of strategies. The absence of a comprehensive anti-corruption strategy or lobbying rules, as noted in recent reports on [[Poland]], negatively impacts this pillar.
- **Institutional Accountability**: Evaluates checks and balances, including the transparency of asset declarations for high-level officials, the independence of the judiciary (e.g., the [[Constitutional Tribunal]] and [[Supreme Court]] in [[Poland]]), and the oversight mechanisms for anti-corruption agencies. Factors such as broad immunities for top executives and the enforcement of whistleblower protection laws are critical components.
- **Public Procurement Integrity**: Specifically assesses vulnerabilities in public procurement processes, including single-bid rates, bid-rigging risks, and the transparency of tenders. This is a significant variable in countries like [[Poland]] where procurement remains highly vulnerable to corruption despite increased investigations.

## Key Metrics & Variables

- **CPI Score**: A normalized score out of 100, where higher scores indicate lower perceived corruption. In the context of [[Malta]], the CPI score was recorded at 46/100 in 2025, indicating high perceived levels of corruption in the public sector.
- **Source Aggregation**: The index synthesizes data from 13 different sources covering over 180 countries, ensuring a multi-dimensional view of corruption risks.
- **Business Confidence Impact**: The index correlates with business sentiment; for instance, in [[Malta]], 51% of companies perceived corruption as a problem in doing business, and 38% reported that corruption prevented them from winning public tenders. In [[Poland]], low confidence in investment protection due to legislative instability and corruption risks in public procurement are key contextual factors.
- **Institutional Independence Indicators**: While not a direct numeric input to the CPI, the perceived independence of judicial and prosecutorial bodies (such as the separation of the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General) strongly influences the underlying perception data aggregated by the index.

## Application

1. **Data Collection**: Aggregate existing data from reputable third-party sources that assess corruption perceptions in various countries. This includes monitoring institutional developments such as the establishment of the [[European Public Prosecutor's Office]] in [[Poland]] or changes in media freedom that impact transparency.
2. **Standardization and Aggregation**: Normalize the data from different sources to a common scale (0-100) and calculate the average score for each country, ensuring statistical robustness. This step accounts for variations in how different sources weigh factors like public procurement transparency or judicial independence.
3. **Publication and Analysis**: Publish the annual ranking and provide country-specific analysis. This analysis often contextualizes CPI scores with broader rule of law metrics, highlighting trends such as the impact of legislative reforms (e.g., changes in magisterial inquiry thresholds in [[Malta]] or the status of the [[National Council for the Judiciary]] in [[Poland]]) on the perceived corruption landscape. It also considers the operational effectiveness of anti-corruption bodies and the extent of political immunities.
