# Medium-term fiscal-structural plan

## Overview

The **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTFSP)** is a mandatory multiannual framework introduced under the reformed EU Economic Governance Framework ([[Regulation (EU) 2024/1263]]). It requires Euro area member states to submit a plan covering at least four years (e.g., 2025–2029) that outlines a realistic trajectory for achieving fiscal balance or surplus while integrating key structural reforms.

For **Belgium**, the MTFSP serves as the primary mechanism for managing deteriorating public finances amidst high debt levels (104.7% of GDP in 2024). The plan, submitted in March 2025 and endorsed by the Commission in May 2025, utilizes a seven-year adjustment period and incorporates the **National Escape Clause** to accommodate increased defence spending while committing to structural reforms in pensions, taxation, and labour markets.

For **France**, the MTFSP is critical for addressing persistently high general government deficits (5.8% of GDP in 2024) and rising public debt (113% of GDP in 2024). France submitted its plan in October 2024, which was endorsed by the Council in January 2025 alongside the recommendation to end the [[Excessive Deficit Procedure]]. France’s plan also relies on a seven-year fiscal adjustment period, aiming to mitigate high interest expenditure and rigid expenditures through robust spending reviews and targeted reforms, despite political fragmentation and budgetary delays following the 2024 elections.

For **Italy**, the MTFSP is central to managing a public debt-to-GDP ratio projected to rise to 136.7% in 2025, driven by lagged borrowing needs from tax credits and high interest costs. Italy submitted its plan in October 2024, which was endorsed by the Council in January 2025, coinciding with the end of the [[Excessive Deficit Procedure]]. The plan commits to a seven-year fiscal adjustment path, leveraging a primary surplus achieved in 2024 (0.4% of GDP) through the phase-out of the "Superbonus" scheme, while addressing structural vulnerabilities such as sluggish productivity, high pension costs, and regional disparities.

For **Poland**, the MTFSP is a critical tool for addressing a high budget deficit (6.6% of GDP in 2024) and rising public debt (projected 58.0% of GDP in 2025). The Council endorsed Poland’s plan on January 21, 2025, simultaneously initiating the [[Excessive Deficit Procedure]]. Poland’s plan aims to reduce the deficit below 3% by 2028. The fiscal adjustment is complicated by high and rising defence expenditure (expected to reach 2.8% of GDP in 2025), for which Poland leverages the **National Escape Clause**. The plan integrates structural reforms aimed at improving competitiveness, addressing skills shortages, and enhancing the efficiency of public investment, while navigating a complex regulatory environment and demographic decline.

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

The methodology and application of the MTFSP in the context of Belgium, France, Italy, and Poland rely on three core analytical pillars:

1.  **Fiscal Consolidation Pathway**:
    *   Establishes a net expenditure growth ceiling to control [[public finance deterioration]].
    *   Defines a realistic debt reduction trajectory over the medium term.
        *   **Belgium**: Stabilizing debt before 2035.
        *   **France**: Addressing projected debt rise to 118.4% by 2026.
        *   **Italy**: Managing a projected debt rise to 136.7% in 2025 and ensuring long-term sustainability despite high interest expenditure (~3.9% of GDP).
        *   **Poland**: Reducing the deficit below 3% by 2028 and managing rising debt affordability amidst high interest costs and local-currency debt dynamics.
    *   Integrates macroeconomic forecasts from national institutes (e.g., [[Federal Planning Bureau]] for Belgium, [[Banque de France]] for France, [[Bank of Italy]] and [[Parliamentary Budget Office]] for Italy, and [[National Bank of Poland]] for Poland) to validate assumptions regarding GDP growth and interest rates.

2.  **Structural Reform Integration**:
    *   Links fiscal adjustments to specific structural reforms aimed at boosting potential growth and reducing expenditure pressure.
    *   Key areas for **Belgium** include [[pension reform]] (bonus-malus system), [[tax wedge on labour]] reduction, and increasing [[labour market participation]].
    *   Key areas for **France** include enhancing [[labor productivity]], improving the [[business environment]] to boost private investment and R&D intensity, accelerating the [[energy transition]] (renewables and building decarbonization), and addressing [[skills shortages]].
    *   Key areas for **Italy** include:
        *   **Justice System**: Reducing trial durations and backlogs to improve the [[business environment]].
        *   **Labor Market**: Increasing female and youth participation, addressing [[skills mismatches]], and expanding childcare/long-term care services.
        *   **Productivity**: Boosting R&D investment and venture capital markets to counteract long-term productivity stagnation.
        *   **Regional Convergence**: Addressing the North-South divide (Mezzogiorno) through targeted infrastructure and service delivery improvements.
    *   Key areas for **Poland** include:
        *   **Regulatory Environment**: Addressing legislative volatility and complexity to deter investment and improve the [[business environment]].
        *   **Labor Market & Education**: Addressing severe [[skills shortages]] in STEM and vocational fields, improving the effectiveness of [[vocational education and training]], and increasing adult learning participation to support the [[green transition]] and [[digital transition]].
        *   **Pension & Healthcare Sustainability**: Reforms to address the low statutory retirement age for women and the sustainability of special pension schemes; improving the healthcare system’s focus on preventive care and reducing hospital-centrism.
        *   **Energy Transition**: Accelerating [[renewable energy]] deployment, grid modernization, and phasing out fossil fuel subsidies, leveraging Poland’s position as a clean tech hub (e.g., battery manufacturing).
        *   **Regional Development**: Addressing significant disparities between the capital region (Warsaw) and eastern/rural areas through targeted infrastructure and investment.
    *   Assesses the impact of reforms on [[productivity growth]], competitiveness, and [[fiscal sustainability]].

3.  **Institutional Coordination & Monitoring**:
    *   Requires effective budgetary coordination between central and sub-national entities.
        *   **Belgium**: Federal/regional coordination.
        *   **France**: Central/local coordination.
        *   **Italy**: Managing fragmented governance and ensuring alignment with [[Recovery and Resilience Facility]] (RRP) milestones.
        *   **Poland**: Coordinating with the newly established independent [[Fiscal Council]] (operational 2026) to enhance transparency and credibility of fiscal projections.
    *   Monitors adherence through annual progress reports and regular updates, supervised by national independent bodies.
        *   **Belgium**: [[High Council of Finance]] and [[Ministry of Finance]].
        *   **France**: [[High Council of Public Finance]] and [[Cour des Comptes]].
        *   **Italy**: [[Parliamentary Budget Office]] (PBO), which provides broad mandates to assess budgetary forecasts and enhance transparency.
        *   **Poland**: [[Fiscal Council]] and [[Polish Social Insurance Institution]] (ZUS) for monitoring social expenditure sustainability.
    *   Addresses institutional fragmentation risks by mandating transparency in off-budget obligations, contingent liabilities (e.g., state-owned enterprises like [[EDF]] in France; SOE support needs in Italy; potential banking sector dispute costs in Poland), and ensuring that fiscal consolidation does not undermine essential investments in green and digital transitions.

## Key Metrics & Variables

-   **Net Expenditure Growth**: The primary fiscal anchor. Member states set a maximum rate, though deviations are permitted under specific clauses (e.g., defence for Belgium and Poland). For France, Italy, and Poland, strict adherence to net expenditure benchmarks is crucial to curb rigid expenditures and ensure the primary surplus (Italy 2024: 0.4% of GDP) is maintained or expanded sustainably.
-   **Debt-to-GDP Ratio**: The central sustainability metric.
    *   **Belgium**: Targets stabilizing from 104.7% (2024).
    *   **France**: Targets reversing the rise from 113% (2024) to prevent a surge to 118.4% by 2026.
    *   **Italy**: Manages a projected increase to 136.7% in 2025, driven by borrowing needs and high interest costs. The metric assesses the impact of the interest-growth differential and debt maturity profiles.
    *   **Poland**: Manages a projected increase to 58.0% in 2025. While debt affordability remains manageable given growth and inflation, the metric monitors the trajectory towards the 3% deficit target and the impact of high interest costs.
-   **General Government Deficit**: Targeted reduction of the structural deficit.
    *   **Belgium**: Deficit of 4.5% in 2024.
    *   **France**: Deficit of 5.8% in 2024.
    *   **Italy**: Deficit of 3.4% in 2024, with a focus on ensuring primary balance improvements are driven by structural revenue gains rather than one-off measures (e.g., phase-out of Superbonus).
    *   **Poland**: Deficit of 6.6% in 2024. The plan aims to reduce this below 3% by 2028 through revenue-enhancing measures and spending discipline, while accommodating rising defence spending.
-   **Ageing-Related & Social Expenditure**: A critical structural variable.
    *   **Belgium**: Steep increases in age-related spending (pensions +3.5%, long-term care +1.7% by 2070).
    *   **France**: High public expenditure (57.1% of GDP) and rising costs associated with [[pension reform]].
    *   **Italy**: Pension spending among the highest in the EU (16.1% of GDP), with medium-term risks from demographic aging. The 2011 pension reform provides a long-term anchor, but costs remain high.
    *   **Poland**: Sustainability risks due to demographic decline (aging population), low statutory retirement age for women (60 years), and special pension schemes. Reforms aim to increase the effective retirement age and improve healthcare efficiency.
-   **Labour Market Indicators**: Includes [[employment rate]], [[activity rate]], and [[tax wedge on labour]].
    *   **Belgium**: Reforms aim to raise the activity rate.
    *   **France**: Reforms aim to address rising unemployment (7.9% projected in 2025) and [[skills mismatches]].
    *   **Italy**: Record employment rates but low labor force participation (women: 57.4% vs EU avg 70.8%). Reforms target increasing participation and reducing the high tax wedge on labor.
    *   **Poland**: Historically low unemployment (2.9% in 2024) but severe [[skills shortages]] in STEM, vocational, manufacturing, and construction fields. Reforms focus on improving the [[vocational education and training]] system and addressing the disability employment gap (35.6%).
-   **Productivity & Investment Growth**: A key growth driver.
    *   **Belgium**: Addresses weak [[labour productivity growth]] through business dynamism measures.
    *   **France**: Faces stagnant business [[R&D intensity]] and subdued investment.
    *   **Italy**: Sluggish productivity growth due to insufficient R&D (half EU average), underdeveloped venture capital, and low tertiary education attainment. Reforms aim to boost investment in digital and green transitions.
    *   **Poland**: Low labor productivity (67% of EU average) hampered by a complex tax framework and legislative volatility. Reforms aim to improve the [[business environment]], enhance science-business collaboration, and boost efficiency in public investment and clean tech manufacturing.
-   **Contingent Liabilities & SOE Risks**: Assesses risks from state-owned enterprises and off-budget items.
    *   **France**: Risks associated with [[EDF]] and the energy sector.
    *   **Belgium**: Regional fiscal coordination and off-budget obligations.
    *   **Italy**: Exposure to state-guaranteed loans and potential support needs for state-owned enterprises and local governments with limited fiscal capacity.
    *   **Poland**: Potential costs from banking sector disputes (legacy forex mortgages) and reliance on external funding for infrastructure. Strong banking capital buffers mitigate some risks.
-   **Financial Sector Health & Sovereign-Bank Nexus**:
    *   **Italy**: Specific focus on the "doom loop" risk where banks remain heavily exposed to domestic sovereign debt, despite improved capital adequacy (20%) and declining non-performing loans (2.8%).
    *   **Poland**: Resilient banking sector with high capitalization and declining non-performing loans. Legal risks from legacy forex mortgages are monitored, but the sector is considered strong.

For Italy, this includes assessing the impact of phasing out one-off fiscal measures like the Superbonus. For Poland, this includes evaluating the impact of legislative volatility and complex tax frameworks on investment and growth.

2.  **Evaluation of Compliance & Flexibility Clauses**:

The Commission verifies that these reforms are realistic, timely, and sufficient to support fiscal targets and potential growth, particularly in improving competitiveness and addressing regional disparities.
