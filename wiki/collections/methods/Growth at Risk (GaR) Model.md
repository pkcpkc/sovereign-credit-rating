# Growth at Risk (GaR) Model

## Overview

The **Growth at Risk (GaR)** model is a probabilistic methodology developed by the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** to estimate the likelihood and magnitude of severe economic downturns (tailing risks). Unlike standard growth forecasts that focus on the conditional mean, GaR focuses on the lower tail of the growth distribution, typically measuring the expected growth rate at the 10th or 25th percentile of the distribution.

In the context of sovereign credit assessment, such as the **IMF Article IV Consultation** for **Hungary** (2025), GaR is used to quantify the impact of specific vulnerabilities—such as high public debt, external imbalances, financial sector weaknesses, and institutional fragility—on the economy's resilience to shocks. It helps identify structural factors that increase the probability of negative growth outcomes, informing recommendations on fiscal adjustment, monetary policy, and structural reforms.

## Key Pillars & Analytical Components

The GaR methodology integrates financial stability, real sector fundamentals, and institutional quality to assess downside risk:

- **Financial Stress Component**: Measures the impact of financial sector vulnerabilities (e.g., sovereign-bank nexus, credit booms, asset price bubbles) on growth. In Hungary’s case, this includes risks from banks' growing exposure to sovereign debt and corporate FX risks.
- **Real Sector Fundamentals**: Analyzes the impact of macroeconomic imbalances, such as current account deficits, public debt levels, and fiscal deficits, on growth resilience. High public debt (projected to rise to ~79% of GDP by 2030 in Hungary) and high gross financing needs amplify GaR.
- **Institutional & Governance Factors**: Incorporates the quality of institutions, rule of law, and policy credibility. Weak governance (e.g., suspended EU funds due to rule of law concerns in Hungary) significantly increases growth at risk by reducing the effectiveness of policy responses and investment confidence.
- **External Vulnerabilities**: Assesses exposure to global shocks, including trade fragmentation, commodity price volatility, and geopolitical conflicts. Hungary’s integration into global value chains (particularly Germany) and exposure to US tariffs are key external risk drivers.

## Key Metrics & Variables

- **Growth at Risk (GaR)**: The expected growth rate at a specific lower percentile (e.g., 10th or 25th) of the growth distribution. A lower (more negative) GaR indicates higher vulnerability.
- **Public Debt-to-GDP**: Elevated debt levels increase GaR by constraining fiscal space during downturns and raising the risk of debt sustainability issues.
- **Financial Soundness Indicators**: Metrics such as Capital Adequacy Ratios (CAR), Non-Performing Loan (NPL) ratios, and sovereign-bank nexus exposure. High sovereign exposure in banking sectors amplifies systemic risk.
- **Current Account Balance**: Persistent deficits increase external vulnerability and GaR, while surpluses (like Hungary’s 2.2% surplus in 2024) can mitigate it, though structural weaknesses may still persist.
- **Institutional Quality Index**: A composite measure of governance, rule of law, and regulatory framework effectiveness. Poor institutional quality acts as a multiplier for other risks.
- **Fiscal Deficit**: Large primary deficits increase GaR by signaling potential future debt distress and limiting counter-cyclical policy options.

## Application

1. **Data Collection & Calibration**: Gather historical data on GDP growth, financial sector indicators (credit-to-GDP, bank balance sheets), fiscal variables (debt, deficit), and institutional metrics. Calibrate the model to the specific economic structure of the country (e.g., Hungary’s high energy intensity and export dependency).
2. **Estimation of Tail Risk**: Run statistical models (often quantile regressions or dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models) to estimate the 10th or 25th percentile of future growth distributions. This identifies the "worst-case" plausible growth scenarios under current conditions.
3. **Decomposition of Drivers**: Analyze which factors contribute most to the negative tail risk. For instance, in Hungary, the model would highlight the contribution of high public debt, regulatory distortions (price caps), and governance issues to elevated GaR.
4. **Policy Scenario Analysis**: Simulate the impact of policy changes on GaR. For example, assess how additional fiscal adjustment (e.g., reducing deficit by 2% of GDP), phasing out price controls, and restoring EU fund access would improve (lower the magnitude of) Growth at Risk.
5. **Integration into Sovereign Assessment**: Combine GaR results with other IMF tools (Debt Sustainability Analysis, External Sector Assessment) to form a holistic view of sovereign credit risk. High GaR implies a higher probability of stress, necessitating stronger policy buffers and potentially influencing credit ratings by agencies like **Fitch Ratings** or **S&P Capital IQ**.
