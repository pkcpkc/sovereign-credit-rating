## Economic Overview

Croatia is situated within a European economic landscape characterized by a focus on fiscal consolidation and the transition toward green and digital economies. As part of the European Commission's 2025 regional analysis, Croatia is positioned amidst a broader push for administrative simplification, investment in R&D, and the management of public debt-to-GDP ratios to ensure long-term fiscal sustainability. 

The country operates within a regional context—including peers like Austria, Germany, Italy, Belgium, Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Luxembourg, Sweden, Slovakia, Slovenia, Hungary, Ireland, Estonia, Latvia, Cyprus, Malta, and Lithuania—navigating high capital costs, potential disparities in GDP per head, and the necessity of balancing growth with structural reforms. In alignment with broader European trends discussed in recent IMF Article IV consultations, the region faces challenges such as sluggish productivity growth and the need to manage inflation gaps driven by sticky services prices. For Croatia, maintaining stability involves balancing regional growth dynamics with the specific requirements of the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRP) while navigating the same structural pressures affecting neighbors, such as declining labor-cost competitiveness and the need for growth-enhancing reforms in labor markets and product dynamics. 

In the broader regional context of the 2025 IMF assessments, Croatia is noted as part of a group of nations navigating high refinancing costs and the necessity of rebuilding fiscal buffers. While neighboring economies like Hungary face specific challenges regarding stagnant output and high inflation, the overarching regional theme emphasizes the need for aggressive fiscal adjustments (targeting headline deficits below 3%) and the rationalization of social benefits to ensure long-term stability.

## Governance & Rule of Law

The country is subject to the oversight and reporting standards of the [[European Commission]]. Current policy focuses include the "simplification of economic life" to reduce bureaucracy for businesses and the implementation of reforms related to the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRP). Furthermore, regional trends emphasize the importance of improving transparency in the corporate sector and maintaining robust frameworks to combat corruption and ensure financial integrity. These efforts are consistent with broader regional priorities to improve governance to unlock EU funding and foster a more predictable business environment.

## Key Risks & Outlook

Key risks for Croatia, consistent with regional trends in the 2025 report and IMF observations, include:
- **Fiscal Sustainability:** The need for aggressive fiscal consolidation to manage public debt and maintain long-term stability, specifically aiming for sustainable deficit targets. Regional peers face high debt-to-GDP projections (e.g., Belgium reaching 123% by 2030), highlighting the importance of rationalizing social benefits and public wage bills.
- **Labor Market:** Addressing skills gaps and ensuring high labor productivity to support the twin green and digital transitions, while potentially narrowing participation gaps in the workforce. Regional concerns include the impact of automatic wage indexation on international competitiveness and the need to widen the income gap between work and non-work.
- **Energy Transition:** Navigating the administrative complexities of decarbonizing transport and building sectors while seeking a shortage of skilled renovation workers.
- **Economic Growth:** Managing growth amidst high capital costs and potential regional disparities in GDP per head, while navigating global trade uncertainties and potential geoeconomic fragmentation.
- **Structural Alignment:** Ensuring alignment with broader EU trends regarding R&D intensity, administrative efficiency, and the reduction of "red tape" to remain competitive within the single market.
- **Financial Stability:** Monitoring regional risks such as Non-Performing Loans (NPLs) in specific sectors like commercial real estate, where risks have increased due to contracting capital values, and addressing sovereign-bank linkages.
