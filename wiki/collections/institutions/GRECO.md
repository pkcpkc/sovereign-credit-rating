# GRECO

## Mandate & Role

GRECO (Group of States Against Corruption) is a monitoring body of the Council of Europe responsible for assessing and promoting anti-corruption measures in its member states. Its primary role involves evaluating the legal and practical effectiveness of anti-corruption frameworks, including lobbying transparency, asset declaration systems, and conflict of interest regulations. In the context of sovereign risk and institutional strength, GRECO serves as a key benchmark for assessing the integrity of public administration and the credibility of political processes. GRECO provides critical oversight that influences the institutional checks and balances scores used in broader rule of law assessments, such as those conducted by the European Commission.

## Key Activities & Reports

GRECO actively monitors compliance with its recommendations regarding anti-corruption standards across member states.

In its assessment of **Austria**, GRECO has repeatedly called for reforms to strengthen the national lobbying framework, which dates back to 2013 and currently lacks comprehensive transparency, supervision, and enforcement mechanisms. The organization has highlighted the urgent need for a robust transparency register to address weaknesses in the lobbying regime, a gap that remains unresolved as of mid-2025. These findings are cited by the European Commission as evidence of limited progress in Austria's institutional checks and balances and accountability frameworks.

In **Belgium**, GRECO is listed among the key institutions monitoring the anti-corruption framework. While the 2025 Rule of Law Report highlights that Belgium scores relatively low on corruption perceptions, it notes significant gaps in integrity frameworks for politicians and police. Specific areas where GRECO’s standards are relevant include:
- **Lobbying and Revolving Doors:** Limited progress was made on lobbying legislation, with no decision taken by the new Government on draft rules, and binding rules on cooling-off periods remaining absent.
- **Asset Declarations:** The system suffers from a lack of verification and transparency, as declarations are sealed and only accessible during criminal investigations, a setup criticized by civil society and noted in assessments of institutional integrity.
- **Political Integrity:** Concerns persist regarding the monitoring and enforcement of integrity policies for Ministers and MPs, with dispersed powers across various bodies hindering effective oversight.

In **Hungary**, GRECO is identified as a key institution within the anti-corruption framework. The 2025 Rule of Law Report indicates that Hungary faces persistent challenges in aligning with international anti-corruption standards. The perception of corruption remains high (CPI score 41/100), with no robust track record of convictions in high-level cases. Specific deficiencies relevant to GRECO’s monitoring include:
- **Integrity Oversight:** The Integrity Authority continues to face obstacles in accessing data and fulfilling oversight duties, hindering effective accountability.
- **Asset Declarations:** The system lacks effective oversight and systematic follow-up for non-compliance, weakening the verification mechanisms expected under GRECO standards.
- **Lobbying Transparency:** Legislation regarding lobbying and revolving doors remains pending, with plans scheduled for late 2025 but not yet materialized, leaving regulatory gaps in transparency and conflict of interest management.

In **Italy**, GRECO’s standards are increasingly relevant as legislative gaps in regulating lobbying and political party financing persist. The 2025 Rule of Law Report highlights that no progress has been made on establishing an operational lobbying register or addressing the practice of channelling donations through political foundations, areas closely monitored under GRECO’s fourth evaluation round. Additionally, while the National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC) has updated the National Anti-Corruption Plan and issued guidelines on "revolving doors," the lack of concrete measures to establish a National Human Rights Institution and the absence of a single electronic register for political party financing continue to raise concerns regarding compliance with international anti-corruption benchmarks. The Constitutional Court’s validation of the abolition of the "abuse of public office" offence also impacts the integrity framework that GRECO oversees.

In **Poland**, GRECO’s standards remain critical amidst ongoing rule of law reforms. The 2025 Rule of Law Report highlights that while the European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO) is fully operational and a new Central Office for Combating Corruption is being established within the police, significant structural weaknesses persist. Key areas relevant to GRECO’s assessment include:
- **Absence of Comprehensive Strategy:** Poland lacks a comprehensive national anti-corruption strategy, a key benchmark for systematic prevention and enforcement.
- **Lobbying and Transparency:** No progress has been made in introducing lobbying rules, creating a transparency gap in political influence and public procurement, which remains highly vulnerable to corruption due to significant single-bid rates.
- **Executive Immunity:** The continued existence of broad immunities for top executives hinders high-level corruption prosecutions, a concern directly impacting the effectiveness of accountability mechanisms monitored by GRECO.
- **Prosecutorial Independence:** Significant progress has been made in separating the office of the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General, aiming to ensure functional independence, which supports the judicial independence metrics relevant to anti-corruption efficacy.
