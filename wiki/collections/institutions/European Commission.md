# European Commission

## Mandate & Role

The European Commission serves as the executive branch of the European Union, with a critical role in **economic surveillance**, **fiscal policy** coordination, and **Rule of Law** monitoring across member states. Through the **[[European Semester]]** framework, the Commission monitors, evaluates, and recommends economic, employment, and structural policies to ensure macroeconomic stability and sustainable growth. It enforces the **[[Stability and Growth Pact]]**, assessing member states' budgetary positions against EU treaty thresholds (e.g., the 3% deficit limit) and issuing Country-Specific Recommendations (CSRs). Under the reformed **EU Economic Governance Framework** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1263), the Commission evaluates **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plans** (MTFPs) and adjudicates the use of mechanisms such as the **National Escape Clause** for specific expenditures like defence.

The Commission’s surveillance extends to **fiscal sustainability** and **institutional strength**, increasingly integrating assessments of **fiscal federalism** and **accountability mechanisms** at sub-national levels. For instance, in **Belgium**, the Commission has noted the challenges posed by complex fiscal federalism to accountability, emphasizing the need for revitalized fiscal councils and binding burden-sharing rules between federal and regional entities to ensure coherent climate and fiscal targets. In **France**, the Commission monitors the implementation of fiscal consolidation measures aimed at reducing the deficit below 3% by 2029, acknowledging the complexity of the multi-layered local government structure and the need for enhanced fiscal oversight to address recent slippages. The Commission also plays a central role in guiding the **[[Energy Transition]]**, promoting **[[Decarbonisation]]**, and ensuring **[[Fiscal Sustainability]]** by integrating green budgeting principles, monitoring long-term risks such as aging populations, and addressing structural issues like labour market disincentives, productivity gaps, and competitiveness challenges.

In addition to economic governance, the Commission actively addresses **Rule of Law** concerns as integral components of institutional strength and credit risk. It publishes annual **Rule of Law Reports** assessing member states on four pillars: the justice system, anti-corruption framework, media pluralism/freedom, and other institutional checks and balances. This surveillance covers judicial independence, prosecutorial autonomy, legislative quality, and institutional effectiveness, directly informing assessments of policy credibility and governance stability. The Commission evaluates factors such as **legislative stability**, **policymaking stability**, and **accountability**, noting how political friction, accelerated legislative procedures, and civic space restrictions can impact sovereign credit metrics by introducing policy uncertainty and weakening institutional checks. Specific attention is paid to **case allocation transparency**, **public procurement integrity**, and the use of **emergency powers**, which are increasingly cited as threats to legal certainty and business confidence. The Commission closely monitors judicial interpretations of key legal principles, such as **lex mitior** and the **statute of limitations**, to assess risks of impunity and compliance with EU law.

A critical aspect of the Commission's mandate involves the management of EU funds, particularly the **[[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]** and **[[Cohesion Policy]]** funds. The Commission links the disbursement of these funds to rule of law compliance, a mechanism that significantly impacts sovereign credit profiles. For example, the suspension of approximately €19 billion in EU funds for **[[Hungary]]** due to rule of law and governance shortcomings represents a major downside risk to the country's fiscal and external balances, as highlighted in external assessments such as those by the **[[International Monetary Fund]]**. The Commission's enforcement of the **[[EU Rule of Law Mechanism]]** thus serves as both a governance tool and a critical variable in sovereign debt sustainability analysis.

## Key Activities & Reports

The Commission regularly publishes **Country Reports** as part of the European Semester process to evaluate member states' economic health and policy compliance, as well as dedicated chapters in its annual **Rule of Law Report**.

*   **2025 Country Report on Belgium**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted significant deterioration in public finances, with the general government deficit expanding to 4.5% of GDP in 2024 and projected to rise to 5.4% in 2025. Public debt is projected to surge from 104.7% of GDP to 126.4% by 2035 without intervention. The Commission positively assessed Belgium's **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** (2025–2029) and endorsed the country's use of the **National Escape Clause** to accommodate increased defence spending (projected at 1.7% of GDP). The report identified critical structural challenges, including high ageing-related costs (projected to rise by 5.1% of GDP by 2070), low labour productivity growth, weak business dynamism ("zombie firms"), and disincentives to work due to high taxes and complex benefit systems. It also noted Belgium's shortfall in 2030 climate targets and high fossil fuel subsidies. The Commission emphasized the need for coordinated efforts between federal and regional entities to meet climate goals and strengthen the fiscal framework through a revitalized fiscal council.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Slovakia**: Published in July 2025, this chapter highlighted limited to no progress in addressing key rule of law concerns, despite intensive dialogue with the Commission. Key issues include low perceived judicial independence, unresolved safeguards for the dismissal of Judicial Council members, and the broad powers of the Prosecutor General to interfere in high-level corruption cases. The dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities (NAKA and SPO) has led to operational inefficiencies.
    *   **Judicial Independence & Governance**: Perceived judicial independence remains low (32% among the public, 34% among companies). No progress was made in establishing sufficient safeguards for the dismissal of Judicial Council members, particularly those not elected by judges. Amendments to judges' laws introduced bonuses and evaluation changes, raising concerns about potential influence. The role of the **[[Judicial Council (Slovakia)]]** in disciplinary proceedings has expanded, posing risks to independence.
    *   **Anti-Corruption Framework**: There has been no progress in ensuring effective, independent investigation of high-level corruption. The **[[Prosecutor General’s Office (Slovakia)]]** continues to use powers to annul final decisions, including in high-profile cases. The closure of the **[[National Crime Agency (NAKA)]]** and the **Special Prosecution Office** resulted in delays, a drop in corruption cases investigated, and a lack of specialized investigators. A new National Anti-Corruption Strategy 2024-2029 is in preparation.
    *   **Media Freedom & Pluralism**: No progress was made on safeguarding the independence of public service media. The governance structure of **[[Slovak Television and Radio (STVR)]]** raises concerns about politicization. Amendments to the Freedom of Information Act introduced fees and longer response times, facing constitutional challenge. The safety of journalists remains a concern in an increasingly hostile environment.
    *   **Checks & Balances & Civil Society**: The law-making process suffers from limited stakeholder involvement, with 28% of laws in 2024 adopted via fast-track procedure. Civil society faces increased pressure through new reporting obligations for NGOs and reduced funding for some human rights advocates. Proposed constitutional amendments raising concerns about EU law primacy were postponed after first reading.
    *   **Justice System Efficiency**: The justice system faces efficiency challenges, particularly in administrative cases where resolution time rose sharply to 1,040 days in 2023. Slovakia had 31 leading judgments from the **[[European Court of Human Rights]]** pending implementation as of January 2025, rising to 33 by June 2025, indicating weak enforcement of international judicial decisions.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Romania**: Published in July 2025, this chapter highlighted significant progress in addressing judicial independence concerns, particularly through the completion of processes related to the [[Venice Commission]]’s recommendations on the Justice Laws. However, challenges persist in anti-corruption enforcement, media freedom, and legislative quality.
    *   **Judicial Independence & Justice Laws**: Significant progress was made in implementing Venice Commission recommendations. Legislative steps are being explored to extend the mandates of high-ranking prosecutors from three to five years. The Ministry of Justice showed openness to ensuring judicial independence in the reform of the IT system for courts. However, perceived judicial independence has decreased among the public and companies.
    *   **Statute of Limitations & Corruption Case-Law**: A critical development involves the [[High Court of Cassation and Justice]]’s interpretation of **lex mitior**, leading to the discontinuation of many corruption cases and annulment of convictions due to expired limitation periods. This case-law, extending beyond the 2018-2022 legal void, raises systemic risks regarding impunity and EU law compatibility. The HCCJ has referred a question to the [[Court of Justice of the European Union]] (Case C-280/25 Lin II) for clarification.
    *   **Anti-Corruption Strategy & Institutions**: The implementation of the 2021-2025 National Anti-Corruption Strategy is on track, with a new strategy under preparation. The [[National Anti-Corruption Directorate]] (DNA) maintains a stable occupancy rate and continues to secure convictions in high-level cases. However, the [[National Integrity Agency]]’s (ANI) asset declaration system faces challenges after a Constitutional Court ruling deemed the requirement to declare spouses’ and children’s assets unconstitutional, weakening transparency.
    *   **Media Pluralism & Freedom**: No progress was made in enhancing the independent governance and editorial independence of public service media. Untransparent political advertising and financing of private media by political parties have increased, affecting media independence. Journalists continue to face harassment, threats, and political pressure. The [[National Audiovisual Council]] (CNA) lacks sufficient human resources and an updated IT system.
    *   **Legislative Quality & Public Consultations**: Legislative unpredictability, driven by the frequent use of [[Government Emergency Ordinances]] (199 adopted in 2024), remains a major concern for businesses. Public consultations are often criticized as ineffective "box-ticking" exercises. No progress has been made on introducing lobbying rules for Members of Parliament, and legislation to improve transparency in political party financing remains pending.
    *   **Human Rights & Civil Society**: The accreditation process for National Human Rights Institutions ([[Romanian Institute for Human Rights]] and [[People's Advocate]]) is stalled due to questions from [[Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions]] (GANHRI) regarding independence and resources. The Romanian Institute for Human Rights faces severe budget cuts and staffing shortages. Civil society space is considered narrowed, with CSOs facing administrative barriers, although a Strategy for Open Government 2025-2030 was adopted to address these challenges.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Malta**: Published in July 2025, this chapter highlighted mixed progress across the four key pillars of the rule of law: justice, anti-corruption, media pluralism, and checks and balances.
    *   **Justice Sector Independence & Reform**: Perceived judicial independence remains high among the public (69%) and companies (63%). Significant progress was made in removing executive involvement from the appointment of the Chief Justice through ongoing constitutional reform (tabled May 13, 2025). The transfer of prosecutorial powers for serious offenses to the **Office of the Attorney General** was completed in September 2024, enhancing prosecutorial independence. However, critical challenges remain regarding the efficiency of the justice system, with excessive length of proceedings (e.g., 454 days for first-instance civil/commercial cases in 2023) and a backlog of cases negatively impacting business confidence.
    *   **Anti-Corruption Framework**: The perceived level of corruption remains high (CPI score 46/100). While investigative bodies increased resources, there is no robust track record of final judgments in high-level corruption cases. The **Permanent Commission Against Corruption (PCAC)** has yet to demonstrate concrete results, citing insufficient resources. Integrity measures for high-level politicians remain inadequate, with ministers' asset declarations unpublished. A major legal development was the **Court of Justice of the European Union** ruling that Malta’s **investor citizenship scheme** violates EU Law (April 29, 2025), characterizing it as the "commercialisation" of Union citizenship.
    *   **Media Pluralism & Freedom**: Limited progress was made. The government is preparing legislation to align with the **[[European Media Freedom Act]]**. However, no progress was made in safeguarding the independent governance of public service media, which remains at "very high risk" for political bias. Legislation to protect journalists and improve access to official documents remains pending, despite the enactment of a law on cross-border strategic lawsuits (**SLAPPs**) in July 2024.
    *   **Checks & Balances & Civic Space**: No formal framework for public participation in the legislative process has been introduced. A controversial reform (Bill 125) increasing the threshold for citizens to petition magisterial inquiries raised concerns about reduced accountability for high-level offenses. Civil society space is assessed as "narrowed," with draft regulatory frameworks for NGOs under preparation.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Italy**: Published in July 2025, this chapter assessed Italy’s rule of law situation, highlighting progress in judicial digitalisation and anti-corruption enforcement, alongside persistent challenges in legislative stability and transparency.
    *   **Justice System & Efficiency**: Significant strides have been made in digitalising criminal courts with the mandatory use of the digital case management system (**APP**) since January 1, 2025. The **High Council for the Judiciary** is implementing comprehensive justice reform legislation. However, persistent shortages of administrative staff and magistrates (17% shortage) continue, and excessive length of proceedings remains a critical issue despite a positive trend in reducing case backlogs.
    *   **Constitutional & Judicial Reforms**: A draft constitutional reform to separate the careers of judges and prosecutors, approved by the Chamber of Deputies in January 2025, faces strong opposition from the judiciary and the High Council for the Judiciary, raising concerns about judicial autonomy. The **Constitutional Court** declared the law abrogating the offence of "abuse of public office" compliant with the Constitution in May 2025.
    *   **Anti-Corruption & Integrity**: The **National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC)** strengthened its resources and updated the National Anti-Corruption Plan, issuing guidelines on "revolving doors" and whistleblowing. However, legislative gaps remain in regulating **lobbying** and **political party financing**, with no progress on establishing an operational lobbying register or addressing donations channeled through political foundations. Public procurement remains a high-risk area for corruption.
    *   **Media Pluralism & Freedom**: The media regulator **AGCOM** functions independently and is financially equipped. Public service broadcaster **RAI** retained its license fee levels, ensuring financial stability, though governance reforms are pending. Concerns persist regarding journalist safety, including allegations of spyware use (Paragon) and restrictions on accessing judicial information. The Council of Europe registered 11 alerts concerning Italy in 2025.
    *   **Institutional Checks & Civic Space**: The government’s frequent use of urgency decrees (87 in the current legislature) and the new **Security Law** (adopted June 2025) raise concerns about separation of powers and civic space. The Security Law increases penalties for protest-related activities. No concrete measures have been taken to establish a **National Human Rights Institution (NHRI)**, with five draft laws remaining pending.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Hungary**: Published in July 2025, this chapter highlighted stagnation or deterioration in key rule of law areas, significantly impacting Hungary's **policy credibility** and **institutional strength**. The Commission's findings directly inform the suspension of approximately €19 billion in EU funds, a factor cited by the **[[International Monetary Fund]]** as a major downside risk to Hungary's fiscal and external balances.
    *   **Judicial Independence & Reforms**: The **[[National Judicial Council (Hungary)]]** faced significant challenges, including the resignation of its President in December 2024 following protests against judicial reforms. New rules raised the minimum age for judges to 35 and allowed service until age 70, but the consultation process was deemed ineffective. The **[[Kúria (Hungary)]]** continues to enforce uniform case law, though undue pressure on judges persists.
    *   **Case Allocation Transparency**: No progress was made in improving transparency in lower-instance courts, a long-standing recommendation. Discretion allowed to court managers prevents verification of impartiality, whereas allocation at the *Kúria* remains transparent.
    *   **Anti-Corruption Framework**: Corruption perception remains high (CPI score 41/100). The **[[Integrity Authority (Hungary)]]** faces obstacles in accessing data, and the asset declaration system lacks effective oversight. There is no robust track record of prosecuting high-level corruption. Lobbying and "revolving door" regulations remain pending.
    *   **Media Pluralism & Freedom**: No progress was made in ensuring the functional independence of the **[[National Media and Infocommunications Authority (NMHH)]]**. State advertising is disproportionately channeled to pro-government outlets (e.g., **KESMA**). Journalists face pressure, including investigations by the **[[Sovereignty Protection Office (Hungary)]]** for alleged "foreign interests."
    *   **Checks and Balances & Emergency Powers**: The government extensively uses emergency powers to override parliamentary legislation, undermining legal certainty. The law-making process bypasses public consultation, with substantive amendments introduced at late stages. Oversight bodies, including the Constitutional Court, are perceived as lacking independence.
    *   **Civic Space & Foreign Investment**: The environment for civil society organizations (CSOs) has deteriorated. The **[[Sovereignty Protection Office (Hungary)]]** launched investigations into anti-corruption CSOs. A draft law on "transparency in public life" proposed in May 2025 aims to restrict foreign funding, threatening critical voices. Foreign companies face intensified regulatory pressure, discriminatory taxes, and export restrictions, exacerbating legal uncertainty.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Belgium**: Published in July 2025, this chapter assessed Belgium’s rule of law situation, presenting a mixed picture of progress and persistent challenges.
    *   **Judicial Independence & Management**: Perceived judicial independence remains high (64% public, 72% companies). The Government plans to transfer budgetary management of the justice system from the executive to the judiciary, though this is criticized for being linked to disciplinary reforms. Structural resource shortages and unfilled vacancies due to unattractive working conditions persist.
    *   **Justice Efficiency**: Backlogs and excessive length of proceedings remain critical concerns, particularly in the Court of Appeal of Brussels. The Commission noted poor management and fraud risks in the justice digitalisation strategy, citing reports by the **Belgian Court of Audit**.
    *   **Anti-Corruption & Integrity**: A new financial investigation service (**FIOD-SRFF**) and specialised prosecution service are planned. However, concerns persist over political interference and information leaks in high-level corruption cases handled by the **Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC)**. Police integrity is weak due to disparate policies across 181 local zones.
    *   **Lobbying & Revolving Doors**: No progress was made on lobbying legislation; the new Government has not decided on draft rules. "Revolving door" rules remain non-binding circulars.
    *   **Asset Declarations**: The system for politicians lacks verification and transparency, with declarations sealed and only accessible during criminal investigations.
    *   **Media Pluralism & Freedom**: Media regulators (**VRM**, **CSA**, **Medienrat**) have strengthened independence under the **[[Digital Services Act]]**. Public service media (**VRT**, **RTBF**, **BRF**) face budgetary pressures. Belgium decriminalised defamation and introduced harsher penalties for crimes against journalists.
    *   **Civic Space & Court Compliance**: Civic space is rated as "narrowed," with concerns over judicial bans for protesters. Compliance with final court rulings remains limited, with 17 leading **European Court of Human Rights** judgments pending implementation as of January 2025.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Austria**: Published in July 2025, this chapter assessed Austria’s rule of law situation, noting high public perception of judicial independence (86%) but identifying significant structural reform backlogs. Key challenges highlighted include:
    *   **Judicial Appointments**: No progress in introducing judicial involvement in the appointment of administrative court presidents, deviating from European standards.
    *   **Prosecutorial Independence**: Limited progress on establishing an independent Federal Prosecution Office; the Minister of Justice retains power to issue instructions in individual cases, despite findings of past political influence.
    *   **Anti-Corruption & Transparency**: While corruption perception remains low (67/100 CPI), there is limited progress on asset declaration rules for Members of Parliament and no strengthening of the weak lobbying framework.
    *   **Media Landscape**: Strong regulatory independence via **KommAustria**, but concerns over economic pressures on private media and fair distribution of state advertising (spending doubled to EUR 417.9 million in 2024).
    *   **Institutional Checks**: Effective oversight by the **Ombudsman Board** and **Court of Audit**, though appointments to independent authorities remain susceptible to politicization.
    *   **Economic Linkages**: These governance issues impact **policy credibility**, **policymaking stability**, and **accountability** metrics in sovereign credit assessments, particularly regarding the "revolution door" provisions and lobbying transparency.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on France**: Published in July 2025, this chapter highlighted a complex landscape of ongoing reforms and persistent challenges across the pillars of justice, anti-corruption, media freedom, and checks and balances.
    *   **Judicial Independence Under Pressure**: Despite the implementation of the 2023 reform of the status of magistrates, perceived judicial independence remains average. Magistrates face severe personal attacks and threats, particularly after rulings on national elections, prompting the **Superior Council of the Magistracy** and the President of the Republic to reaffirm judicial independence.
    *   **Digitalization and Efficiency Challenges**: Progress continues in digitalizing judicial procedures (e.g., **Portalis** tool), but magistrates report slow implementation and technical issues. Case resolution times remain high (354 days for civil/commercial cases; 1,335 days for bribery), leading to government-led "flash missions" to improve efficiency.
    *   **Anti-Corruption Institutional Strengthening**: The **Anti-Corruption Agency (AFA)** was reorganized into public and private sector departments. The **High Authority for Transparency in Public Life (HATVP)** increased its staff to handle a 48% rise in asset declarations. However, lobbying transparency remains incomplete, as top-executive officials are not subject to disclosure requirements, despite new rules for foreign lobbyists.
    *   **Media Ownership Transparency Improves**: France made significant progress in transparency by preparing a national media ownership database to identify ultimate beneficial owners. The regulator **ARCOM** is expanding its regulatory role under the **[[Digital Services Act]]** and preparing for the **European Media Freedom Act**, though staff resources remain a concern.
    *   **Public Broadcasting Reform Debate**: A proposed reform to create a holding company for public broadcasters is under debate, with concerns over editorial autonomy and funding stability. Funding is shifting from a license fee to a share of VAT, which NGOs view as a temporary and potentially unstable solution.
    *   **Legislative Simplification vs. Democratic Consultation**: While businesses welcome the draft law to "simplify economic life," stakeholders criticize the reduction in citizen consultation mechanisms and the frequent use of accelerated legislative procedures (e.g., Article 49-3), which contribute to legal uncertainty and perceived legislative instability.
    *   **Narrowing Civic Space**: The civic space is rated as "narrowed." Civil society organizations face budgetary constraints, and human rights defenders report intimidation and online harassment. The **National Consultative Commission on Human Rights (CNC DH)** lacks sufficient means to fully execute its mandate.
    *   **ECHR Implementation Progress**: As of June 2025, the number of pending leading **European Court of Human Rights** judgments decreased from 26 to 22, though historical cases remain unresolved.

*   **2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Poland**: Published in July 2025, this chapter highlights significant efforts by the Polish Government to address long-standing rule of law concerns, particularly regarding judicial independence, as outlined in the Action Plan on the Rule of Law.
    *   **Judicial Independence Reforms**: Significant efforts have been made to implement the Action Plan on the Rule of Law. A draft law has been proposed to address the status of ~1,600 judges appointed at the request of the NCJ in its post-2017 composition, involving a classification system and potential removal, pending Venice Commission consultation. Plans to amend the Supreme Court structure include dismantling the Chamber of Extraordinary Control and Public Affairs to strengthen judicial independence.
    *   **Constitutional Tribunal Crisis**: Legislation addressing the NCJ and Constitutional Tribunal has been adopted by Parliament but has not entered into force due to referral to the Constitutional Tribunal by the President. The Government continues to refuse to publish Constitutional Tribunal judgments, asserting the body lacks legitimacy, leading to a constitutional crisis and an ongoing infringement procedure by the European Commission.
    *   **Prosecution Service Independence**: Significant progress has been made in separating the office of the Minister of Justice from the Prosecutor General. A draft law to this end was approved by the Cabinet Standing Committee in December 2024, incorporating Venice Commission recommendations. The National Prosecution Service has also reviewed past cases, identifying irregularities in investigations from 2016-2023.
    *   **Anti-Corruption Framework**: The **European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO)** is fully operational in Poland. A new Central Office for Combating Corruption will be established within the police, dissolving the Central Anti-Corruption Bureau. However, there is no comprehensive anti-corruption strategy, and no progress has been made in introducing lobbying rules or addressing broad immunities for top executives, which hinder high-level corruption prosecutions.
    *   **Media Pluralism and Freedom**: Politically biased content in public service media has decreased. The Government plans a media law reform to implement the **[[European Media Freedom Act]]**, addressing the independence of the media regulator (KRRiT) and public broadcaster governance. Public broadcasters are currently in liquidation, with funds suspended pending a new state-funded model replacing the license fee system.
    *   **Civil Society**: The civic space in Poland was re-rated from "obstructed" to "narrowed" by CIVICUS in March 2025. The Government has established working groups with civil society organizations and launched a new program for NGOs. However, challenges remain, including negative rhetoric against NGOs and operational restrictions at the border.
    *   **Checks and Balances**: Poland strengthened rules for legislative consultations, introducing mandatory impact assessments. The Ombudsperson continues to play a key role in checks and balances. However, the number of pending leading judgments from the **European Court of Human Rights** has increased to 54 as of June 2025.
    *   **Corruption Risks in Public Procurement**: Public procurement remains highly vulnerable to corruption, with significant single-bid rates. Investigations into EU fund misuse and bid-rigging have led to arrests, but systemic transparency issues persist.

*   **2025 Country Report on Austria (Economic Context)**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted Austria's third consecutive year of recession, a projected deficit of 4.4% of GDP in 2025, and rising gross debt to 84.0% of GDP. The report evaluated Austria's **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** (submitted May 13, 2025) and emphasized the need for structural reforms to address skills shortages, regulatory burdens, and energy affordability.

*   **2025 Country Report on Slovakia**: The report highlights a critical divergence between short-term economic stability and deepening structural and fiscal vulnerabilities. While Slovakia’s economy grew by 2.1% in 2024, the outlook has deteriorated, with GDP growth forecast at 1.5% for 2025 and 1.4% for 2026, driven by waning foreign direct investment (FDI), trade tensions (specifically US tariffs), and declining competitiveness due to sluggish labour productivity. Fiscal sustainability is identified as a significant risk, with the general government deficit remaining high at 5.3% of GDP in 2024. Despite a consolidation package approved in late 2024 (including VAT and corporate tax hikes), the deficit is projected to remain elevated at 4.9% in 2025 and rise to 5.1% in 2026 due to permanent spending increases in pensions and healthcare. Public debt is projected to reach 63.0% of GDP by 2026. The Commission flagged severe rule of law and institutional backsliding, citing the dissolution of the **[[National Crime Agency (NAKA)]]** and **Special Prosecution Office**, which undermines anti-corruption efforts and legal certainty. Structurally, Slovakia faces challenges such as heavy reliance on the automotive sector, low private R&D intensity (0.58% of GDP), and an underdeveloped capital market. Energy security remains a concern due to high dependence on Russian crude oil and natural gas. Implementation of the **[[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]** stood at 33% of milestones fulfilled, slowed by administrative inefficiencies.

*   **2025 Country Report on Romania**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted a critical juncture for Romania's economy, characterized by converging living standards (GDP per capita ~80% of EU average) but growing macroeconomic vulnerabilities. Real GDP growth decelerated to 0.8% in 2024, while the government deficit surged to 9.3% of GDP and public debt rose rapidly to 54.8% of GDP. The current account deficit widened to 8.4% of GDP, reflecting high import demand and weak external competitiveness. The Commission noted that implementation of the **[[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]** was significantly delayed, with only 14% of milestones fulfilled due to administrative bottlenecks and political instability. In January 2025, the Council endorsed Romania’s seven-year **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** (2025–2031) and recommended correcting the excessive deficit, contingent on strict adherence to measures such as public wage/pension freezes and tax reforms. Key structural challenges identified include the lowest R&D intensity in the EU (0.52% of GDP), severe skills shortages, outdated infrastructure, high perceived corruption, and regulatory uncertainty which remains the top barrier to investment for 90% of firms. Despite these challenges, Romania benefited from its accession to the **[[Schengen Area]]** on January 1, 2025, and showed progress in renewable energy (48% of electricity mix).

*   **2025 Country Report on Poland**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted Poland as one of the fastest-growing economies in the EU (2.9% GDP growth in 2024), driven by strong private consumption and EU-funded investment. However, it identified significant fiscal challenges, including a general government deficit of 6.6% of GDP in 2024, which triggered an **[[Excessive Deficit Procedure]]** (EDP) initiated by the Council in January 2025. The Commission endorsed Poland's **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan**, which aims to reduce the deficit below 3% by 2028, while utilizing the **National Escape Clause** to accommodate rising defence spending (projected at 2.8% of GDP in 2025). Structural vulnerabilities noted include low labor productivity (67% of EU average), severe skills shortages, a complex and volatile regulatory environment, and high unit labor costs. The report emphasized the need for pension sustainability reforms due to the low statutory retirement age for women and highlighted Poland's emergence as a clean tech hub, particularly in battery manufacturing, despite high reliance on fossil fuels and electricity prices. Implementation of the **[[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]** was assessed at 25% of milestones fulfilled.

*   **2025 Country Report on Hungary**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted a challenging economic landscape with subdued growth (0.5% in 2024, forecast 0.8% in 2025) and persistent inflationary pressures. The Commission identified high medium-term fiscal sustainability risks due to public debt rising to 73.5% of GDP and elevated debt-servicing costs. While the deficit decreased to 4.9% of GDP in 2024, it is projected to remain elevated. The report criticized significant structural vulnerabilities, including 15 years of stagnant labour productivity, heavy reliance on Russian energy, and a business environment distorted by state intervention (e.g., subsidized lending, price controls). It noted that implementation of the **[[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]** had stalled with 0% of milestones achieved due to rule of law and anti-corruption deficiencies. The Commission assessed Hungary's use of the **National Escape Clause** for defence spending amidst high net expenditure growth. Regulatory pressure on foreign companies and discriminatory taxes were noted as threats to growth prospects and economic strength. The Commission's assessment of governance failures directly underpins the suspension of EU funds, a factor cited by the **[[International Monetary Fund]]** in its 2025 Article IV Consultation as a critical risk to Hungary's fiscal trajectory and debt sustainability.

*   **2025 Country Report on Belgium**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted significant deterioration in public finances, with the general government deficit expanding to 4.5% of GDP in 2024 and projected to rise to 5.4% in 2025. Public debt is projected to surge from 104.7% of GDP to 126.4% by 2035 without intervention. The Commission positively assessed Belgium's **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** (2025–2029) and endorsed the country's use of the **National Escape Clause** to accommodate increased defence spending (projected at 1.7% of GDP). The report identified critical structural challenges, including high ageing-related costs (projected to rise by 5.1% of GDP by 2070), low labour productivity growth, weak business dynamism ("zombie firms"), and disincentives to work due to high taxes and complex benefit systems. It also noted Belgium's shortfall in 2030 climate targets and high fossil fuel subsidies.

*   **2025 Country Report on France**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted a slowing economic trajectory amidst heightened political and economic uncertainty following the 2024 elections. While real GDP grew by 1.2% in 2024, growth is forecast to slow to 0.6% in 2025. The Commission noted a deteriorating fiscal position, with a 5.8% of GDP deficit in 2024 and public debt rising to 113% of GDP. France is subject to an excessive deficit procedure, with the Council recommending its end in January 2025 contingent on strict adherence to its medium-term fiscal-structural plan. The Commission positively assessed French fiscal efforts, acknowledging the political compromise on the 2025 budget and the commitment to frontloaded structural fiscal efforts to bring the deficit below 3% by 2029. Key structural challenges identified include high administrative burdens, stagnant business R&D intensity, complex permitting for renewable energy deployment, and worsening social exclusion. The Commission emphasized the need for robust spending reviews and targeted reforms to boost potential growth and ensure fiscal sustainability, while noting France's advanced implementation of Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) measures (82% of milestones fulfilled). The report also highlighted the need for enhanced fiscal oversight at the local government level to address recent slippages and administrative overlaps.

*   **2025 Country Report on Italy**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report presented a mixed economic picture characterized by moderate growth (0.7% in 2024) and improving fiscal indicators, overshadowed by persistent structural vulnerabilities. Italy achieved a primary surplus of 0.4% of GDP in 2024, driven by the phase-out of the "Superbonus" tax credits, yet public debt is projected to rise to 136.7% of GDP in 2025 due to lagged borrowing needs and high debt service costs. The Commission endorsed Italy's seven-year **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan** (MTFSP) in January 2025, which ended the Excessive Deficit Procedure. Key structural challenges identified include sluggish productivity growth due to low R&D investment (half the EU average), an underdeveloped venture capital market, and a justice system with lengthy trial durations. Labor market issues persist, including low female participation (57.4% vs 70.8% EU average) and significant regional disparities, with the South (Mezzogiorno) lagging in infrastructure and employment. The report also highlighted Italy's leadership in clean tech manufacturing but noted high wholesale electricity prices and vulnerability to climate risks.

*   **2025 Country Report on Malta**: Published on June 4, 2025, this report highlighted Malta's strong economic momentum, with real GDP growing by 6.0% in 2024, driven by tourism and professional services. The employment rate reached 83.0%, though this relies heavily on immigration to address severe structural skills shortages. Fiscally, Malta is undergoing consolidation, with the deficit projected to decline from 3.7% of GDP in 2024 to 2.8% in 2026, while debt remains low at 47.4% of GDP. However, the Commission identified significant long-term fiscal pressures from an aging population, with age-related expenditures projected to rise from 17% to 26% of GDP by 2070. Key structural weaknesses include very low R&D intensity (0.64% of GDP), a high regulatory burden (second weakest in the EU), and inefficient judicial processes. The report raised concerns regarding aggressive tax planning, a large VAT compliance gap (25.9%), and corruption perceptions, noting the limited independence of the **Malta Fiscal Advisory Council (MFAC)**. Environmental challenges include low renewable energy uptake (17%) and high reliance on fossil fuel subsidies. The Commission emphasized the need for effective implementation of the **[[Recovery and Resilience Facility]]** and cohesion policy investments to address these structural gaps.

The Commission utilizes methodologies such as the **Debt Sustainability Monitor** to assess long-term viability, factoring in demographic pressures, contingent liabilities, and climate-related risks, while integrating Rule of Law assessments to gauge institutional resilience and policy credibility.
