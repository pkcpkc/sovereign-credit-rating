# Transparency International

## Mandate & Role

Transparency International is an international non-governmental organization focused on combating corruption globally. In the context of sovereign credit ratings and country risk analysis, Transparency International serves as a key reference for assessing governance quality, institutional strength, and corruption risks. Its indices, particularly the Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI), and reports are frequently cited by financial institutions, rating agencies, and supranational bodies like the [[European Commission]] to evaluate the "policy credibility and institutional strength" and "accountability" dimensions of sovereign risk. Specifically, it highlights risks such as weak administrative capacity, low trust in public institutions, and high perceived corruption among businesses, which are critical factors in determining investor confidence and fiscal sustainability.

## Key Activities & Reports

*   **Corruption Risk Assessment (Romania)**: Transparency International's data and findings are referenced in the *2025 Country Report - Romania* issued by the [[European Commission]]. The report cites "perceived corruption" as a significant threat to Romania's policy credibility, noting that it remains a major barrier to long-term investment and a risk factor in public procurement.
*   **Institutional Accountability Analysis (Romania)**: The organization's work underpins the analysis of Romania's accountability framework, where threats include a lack of a lobby register for MPs and a high share of corruption cases closed due to statute of limitations rulings.
*   **Business Environment Evaluation (Romania)**: Transparency International's insights contribute to the understanding of the business environment in countries like Romania, where high perceived corruption and the need for firms to dedicate staff to regulatory compliance are identified as key economic drains.
*   **Governance Benchmarking (Austria)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Austria, Transparency International's metrics are implicitly relevant to the assessment of the country's anti-corruption framework. Austria scored 67/100 in the 2024 Corruption Perceptions Index, reflecting a context where corruption perception remains low despite structural challenges in judicial appointments and lobbying transparency. The report highlights that while the Federal Bureau of Anti-Corruption (BAK) detected 83 potential cases in 2024, there was limited progress on asset declaration rules for MPs and strengthening the lobbying framework, areas often monitored through Transparency International's advocacy standards.
*   **Anti-Corruption Framework Analysis (Belgium)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Belgium, Transparency International is listed among the key institutions monitoring the country's anti-corruption framework. Belgium scores relatively low on corruption perceptions, yet significant gaps persist. The organization's standards inform the scrutiny of integrity frameworks for politicians and police, where concerns remain regarding the handling of high-level corruption cases, information leaks, and potential political interference. Specific areas of concern include limited progress on lobbying transparency, the absence of binding rules on "revolving doors," and a system of asset and interest declarations for politicians that suffers from a lack of verification and transparency, as declarations are sealed and only accessible during criminal investigations.
*   **Anti-Corruption Monitoring (Hungary)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Hungary, Transparency International's data highlights persistent challenges in the country's anti-corruption framework. Hungary’s score of 41/100 in the 2024 Corruption Perceptions Index underscores a high perception of corruption in the public sector. This metric supports the finding that there is no robust track record of prosecuting high-level corruption. The organization's benchmarks inform the critique of the Integrity Authority, which faces obstacles in accessing data and fulfilling oversight duties, as well as the weak asset declaration system that lacks systematic follow-up for non-compliance. Additionally, Transparency International's advocacy standards contextualize the lack of progress in legislating on lobbying and revolving doors, which was scheduled for November 2025 but had not materialized by the reporting period.
*   **Anti-Corruption and Lobbying Oversight (Italy)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Italy, Transparency International is identified as a key institution within the broader anti-corruption and governance landscape. The report highlights that while the [[National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC)]] has updated the National Anti-Corruption Plan and issued guidelines on "revolving doors," significant legislative gaps remain. Transparency International's standards contextualize the lack of progress in establishing an operational lobbying register or addressing the practice of channeling political donations through political foundations. These deficiencies in transparency are cited as threats to policy credibility and institutional strength, impacting the overall assessment of Italy's rule of law environment alongside other concerns such as judicial independence and media freedom.
*   **Anti-Corruption and Integrity Monitoring (Malta)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Malta, Transparency International’s Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) data is central to assessing policy credibility. Malta scored 46/100 in the 2024/2025 period, reflecting high perceived levels of corruption in the public sector. The organization's benchmarks contextualize the weaknesses in the [[Permanent Commission Against Corruption]] (PCAC), which has yet to demonstrate concrete results or refer cases for prosecution due to insufficient resources. Furthermore, Transparency International's standards highlight the inadequacy of integrity measures for high-level officials, such as the fact that ministers' asset declarations remain unpublished and that conflict of interest rules do not sufficiently cover MPs. The data also supports findings that 51% of companies perceive corruption as a problem in doing business, and 38% report that corruption prevented them from winning public tenders, directly impacting growth prospects and investor confidence.
*   **Anti-Corruption Framework Assessment (Poland)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Poland, Transparency International’s methodology (Corruption Perceptions Index) is cited as a key metric for evaluating the country's anti-corruption framework. The report notes that the perception of corruption in Poland remains relatively high. This perception is compounded by systemic weaknesses, including the absence of a comprehensive national anti-corruption strategy, the lack of lobbying rules, and the existence of broad immunities for top executives, which hinder the prosecution of high-level corruption. While the European Public Prosecutor's Office (EPPO) is fully operational and a new Central Office for Combating Corruption is being established, Transparency International’s benchmarks underscore the continued risks associated with these structural deficiencies and their impact on institutional strength and accountability.
*   **Anti-Corruption and Governance Benchmarking (Slovakia)**: In the *2025 Rule of Law Report* on Slovakia, Transparency International is identified as a key institution monitoring the country's anti-corruption framework. The report highlights significant risks to policy credibility and institutional strength, including high perceived corruption levels in the public sector and a fragmented anti-corruption institutional framework. The dissolution of specialized anti-corruption entities, such as the [[National Crime Agency (NAKA)]] and the Special Prosecution Office (SPO), has led to a drop in investigated corruption cases and operational inefficiencies, aligning with Transparency International's concerns regarding the weakening of independent oversight bodies. Furthermore, the lack of progress in regulating lobbying and the consideration of restrictions on public access to asset declarations are scrutinized against international transparency standards advocated by the organization. These factors contribute to a poor business climate perception and threaten the enforcement of international judicial decisions, including those from the [[European Court of Human Rights]].
