# Council of Europe

## Mandate & Role

The Council of Europe serves as a key international organization dedicated to the protection of human rights, the promotion of democratic governance, and the upholding of the rule of law across its member states. In the context of sovereign credit rating analysis and country risk surveillance, the Council provides critical independent oversight through its specialized bodies, notably GRECO (Group of States against Corruption) and the Venice Commission. These bodies assess and monitor the implementation of anti-corruption standards, judicial independence, and institutional integrity frameworks, offering authoritative benchmarks for evaluating political stability, policy credibility, and accountability mechanisms within member nations like Austria, France, Italy, and Slovakia. Additionally, the Council monitors media freedom and journalist safety, providing alerts on threats to press integrity that serve as indicators of institutional health and civic space constraints.

## Key Activities & Reports

*   **GRECO Assessments & Recommendations**: The Council of Europe’s GRECO mechanism actively monitors national anti-corruption frameworks. In Austria, GRECO has repeatedly issued calls for reform regarding the country's lobbying transparency register. In France, GRECO evaluations are referenced alongside OECD assessments to benchmark the consistency of corruption prosecutions and the effectiveness of the National Financial Prosecutor (PNF) and the Anti-Corruption Agency (AFA). In Italy, GRECO’s standards inform the evaluation of the National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC) and the implementation of the National Anti-Corruption Plan, particularly concerning lobbying regulations and political party financing transparency.
*   **Rule of Law & Judicial Independence Benchmarking**: The organization’s standards regarding judicial independence and the separation of powers serve as reference points for evaluations such as those found in the EU Commission’s Rule of Law Reports. For instance, concerns regarding the appointment procedures for administrative court presidents in Austria are assessed against European standards promoted by the Council of Europe. In France, the Council’s frameworks, including the Venice Commission’s opinions, provide context for assessing the implementation of the 2023 reform of the status of magistrates. In Italy, the Council’s benchmarks are relevant to assessing the proposed constitutional reform separating the careers of judges and prosecutors, which faces scrutiny regarding judicial autonomy. In Slovakia, the Council’s standards are relevant to assessing the governance of the Judicial Council, particularly concerning safeguards for the dismissal of members not elected by judges, and the independence of prosecutorial powers.
*   **European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) Monitoring**: The Council oversees the execution of ECHR judgments, which is a critical metric for institutional credibility and rule of law compliance. In France, the implementation of ECHR judgments has been tracked as a proxy for legal efficiency and state accountability; for example, the number of pending leading ECHR judgments decreased from 26 to 22 by mid-2025, although historical backlogs remain a concern for long-term institutional strength. In Slovakia, weak enforcement of international judicial decisions is highlighted by a record of 31 pending leading ECtHR judgments as of January 2025, which rose to 33 by June 2025.
*   **Media Freedom & Journalist Safety Monitoring**: The Council of Europe monitors threats to media pluralism and journalist safety across member states. In Italy, the Council registered 11 alerts concerning the country in 2025, highlighting issues related to the safety of journalists, access to judicial information, and allegations of spyware use targeting the press. These alerts serve as critical risk indicators regarding civic space and the government's adherence to fundamental freedoms. In Slovakia, the Council’s monitoring context includes concerns over the safety of journalists, an increasingly hostile environment for the press, and the politicization of public service media under the new STVR governance structure, alongside restrictions on access to information via amendments to the Freedom of Information Act.
