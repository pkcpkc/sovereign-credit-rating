# Start of 2025 fiscal year and new regulatory measures

## Event Details

The commencement of the 2025 fiscal year in Hungary marked the continuation of a period characterized by economic stagnation and persistent inflationary pressures. As outlined in the [[IMF]] 2025 Article IV Consultation, the Hungarian economy entered 2025 with real GDP stagnation persisting for the third consecutive year, following a contraction of 0.4% y/y in Q1 2025. The IMF projected modest growth of 0.7% for 2025, driven primarily by private consumption and favorable wage dynamics, with a recovery to 2.0% expected in 2026 contingent upon an investment revival.

Key regulatory and policy measures active at the start of the fiscal year included the maintenance of a tight monetary stance by the [[Magyar Nemzeti Bank]] (MNB), which kept the base rate at 6.5% since September 2024 to combat inflation, which remained above the 3% target at 4.4-4.6% in mid-2025. The [[IMF]] staff report critically assessed existing regulatory interventions, specifically price, interest, and margin caps, arguing that these measures distorted market signals, deferred rather than eliminated inflationary pressures, and weakened the transmission of monetary policy. The staff recommended phasing out these distortive controls to anchor inflation expectations and allow for a durable return to the 3% target by 2027.

Fiscal policy at the onset of 2025 faced significant sustainability challenges. Public debt stood at 73.5% of GDP in 2024 and was projected to rise to approximately 79% by 2030 under current policies. The [[IMF]] identified that announced fiscal policies fell short of the authorities' budget targets and debt sustainability needs, with a fiscal deficit projected to remain around 4.5% of GDP. The staff emphasized the need for an additional cumulative fiscal adjustment of ~2% of GDP over 2025-2028, involving high-quality revenue enhancements and spending rationalization, such as phasing out energy subsidies.

External and geopolitical risks loomed large at the start of the fiscal year. Deepening geoeconomic fragmentation, potential escalation in trade measures (particularly exposure to US tariffs), and regional conflicts posed significant downside risks. A critical vulnerability was the continued suspension of approximately €19 billion in [[European Union]] funds due to rule of law and governance concerns, which threatened both fiscal and external balances. The [[IMF]] highlighted that restoring access to these funds required decisive governance reforms to improve the business environment and institutional strength.
