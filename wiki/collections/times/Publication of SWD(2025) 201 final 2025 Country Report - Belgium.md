# Publication of SWD(2025) 201 final: 2025 Country Report - Belgium

## Event Details

On **June 4, 2025**, the [[European Commission]] published the **2025 Country Report for Belgium** (SWD(2025) 201 final), a key component of the [[European Semester]] economic surveillance cycle. The report outlines a trajectory of slowing economic growth accompanied by significant deterioration in public finances, marking a critical juncture for Belgium’s fiscal sustainability amidst demographic pressures and geopolitical shifts.

### Economic Context and Growth Outlook
Following a 1% GDP growth in 2024—driven primarily by resilient private consumption—Belgium’s economic expansion is projected to decelerate to **0.8% in 2025**. This slowdown is attributed to weakening exports, hampered by external shocks such as [[US tariffs]], and a cooling of domestic investment and consumption. Despite these headwinds, the economy retains strengths, including robust [[R&D intensity]] (3.3% of GDP) and a diversified export base. However, long-term growth prospects are constrained by weak labour productivity growth, low business dynamism, and persistent [[institutional fragmentation]] between federal and regional governments.

### Fiscal Deterioration and Debt Sustainability
The most critical challenge identified is the severe pressure on public finances:
*   **Deficit Widening**: The general government deficit expanded to **4.5% of GDP in 2024** and is forecast to rise further to **5.4% in 2025**.
*   **Debt Trajectory**: Public debt stood at **104.7% of GDP in 2024** and is projected to reach **107.1% in 2025**. Without policy intervention, debt is on an unsustainable upward trajectory, projected to surge to **126.4% of GDP by 2035**.
*   **Expenditure Drivers**: Net expenditure growth is forecast at **5.0% for 2025**, driven by rising [[ageing costs]], increased [[defence spending]], and rigid structural expenditure. Revenue collection remains stable at approximately 50% of GDP, offering limited room for additional fiscal space.

### Policy Response: Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP)
To address these imbalances, Belgium submitted a **Medium-Term Fiscal-Structural Plan (MTP)** for 2025–2029 on **March 18, 2025**, which the Commission endorsed on **May 21, 2025**. Key features include:
*   **National Escape Clause**: Belgium activated the **National Escape Clause** under the reformed [[EU Economic Governance Framework]] (Regulation (EU) 2024/1263) to legally exceed net expenditure limits for defence purposes. Defence spending is projected to rise to **1.7% of GDP in 2025**. This mechanism allows the deviation while maintaining compliance with the broader fiscal roadmap, provided structural reforms and investments are delivered.
*   **Fiscal Adjustment**: The plan commits to a seven-year fiscal adjustment period. It proposes structural reforms including a **bonus-malus pension system**, labour tax cuts financed by capital gains and property taxes, and comprehensive spending reviews.
*   **Framework Weaknesses**: The report highlights that Belgium’s national fiscal framework remains weak due to the lack of effective budgetary coordination between federal and regional entities and the under-resourced nature of the [[High Council of Finance]], which lacks sufficient autonomy and staff to monitor fiscal rules effectively.

### Structural Challenges: Labour Market and Competitiveness
Belgium faces persistent headwinds regarding competitiveness and labour market dynamism:
*   **Labour Participation**: While the employment rate reached a historic high of **72.3% in 2024**, the activity rate remains low at **77.2%** (vs. EU average of 83.3%). Disincentives to work include high personal income taxes (high [[tax-wedge on labour]]), unlimited unemployment benefits, and complex non-cash benefits. The new federal coalition plans to limit unemployment benefits to two years for those under 55 and link benefits to income to boost activation.
*   **Productivity**: Labour productivity growth has lagged the EU average since the 2000s. Competitiveness is eroded by high labour costs (exacerbated by automatic wage indexation during inflation) and a stagnant real effective exchange rate. Business dynamism is low, characterized by a high presence of 'zombie firms' and restrictive regulations in services.

### Long-Term Fiscal Risks: Ageing Demographics
Belgium faces one of the steepest projected increases in ageing-related spending in the EU. By **2070**, age-related expenditure is projected to rise by **5.1 percentage points of GDP** (vs. EU average of 1.4), driven primarily by:
*   **Pensions**: Projected to increase by **3.5% of GDP**.
*   **Long-Term Care**: Projected to increase by **1.7% of GDP**.
This demographic burden severely constrains fiscal space and necessitates the structural pension reforms included in the MTP.

### Energy Transition and Climate Goals
The report notes that Belgium is falling short of its [[energy transition]] commitments:
*   **Climate Targets**: Belgium is projected to miss its 2030 climate target for effort-sharing sectors, achieving only a **42.6% reduction** instead of the required 47%.
*   **Barriers**: High electricity-to-gas price ratios (distorted by taxes) disincentivize electrification. The rollout of renewables is slow due to grid constraints and permitting delays.
*   **Subsidies**: The country maintains significant environmentally harmful [[fossil fuel subsidies]] (0.85% of GDP) with no planned phase-out before 2030.

### Institutional and Governance Factors
*   **Political Stability**: Belgium maintains stable democratic institutions but suffers from political fragmentation due to its federal structure, leading to slow legislative processes and complex coordination challenges.
*   **Accountability**: Transparency in public institutions is high, and digital public services enhance administrative accountability. However, lobbying regulations are loose, and the justice system faces efficiency challenges.
*   **Financial Sector**: The banking sector is sound, well-capitalized (CAR 19.1%), and profitable, with low non-performing loan ratios (1.7%), reducing the immediate risk of sovereign-bank bailouts.
